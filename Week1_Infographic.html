109 Fertilization

  • Ovum is surrounded by Zona pellucida.
  • Sperm undergoes capacitation, head swells, ↑ hyaluronidase enzymes.
  • Fertilization occurs at the ampulla of the fallopian tube → zygote.
  • Zygote rapidly divides → morula (round mass of cells).
    • The morula reaches the uterine cavity 3 days after fertilization.
    • Movement is aided by the cilia motility.
  • Fluid will accumulate in morula → blastocyst.
    • It remains free for 3 days in the uterine cavity.
    • It is nourished by secretions from the endometrium.
  • The blastocyst will be divided into 2 masses:
    • Inner cell mass → embryonic mass → will form the embryo.
    • Outer cell mass → trophoblast → responsible for nutrition.

110 Implantation

  • This occurs on 6th – 7th days after ovulation by penetration of the trophoblast into the secretory endometrium (decidua basalis).
  • Functions of the decidua:
    • Site of implantation & nutrition of the blastocyst.
    • Shares in formation of the placenta.
    • Protective layer against the invasive power of trophoblast.

111 Formation of the Villi

  • The trophoblast is differentiated into 2 layers →
    • Cytotrophoblast (inner).
    • Syncytiotrophoblast (outer).
  • Trophoblast forms finger-like projections into decidua → 1° villus.
  • Mesoderm develops in the 1° villus (CT core) → 2° villus.
  • Vascularization of 2° villus with fetal vessels → 3° villus.
  • There are two types of villi:
    • Anchoring villi: fixation & attachment.
    • Free villi: food & nutrition (highly vascular). Functional unit (HCG → maintain CL for 10 weeks).
Clinical Pearl

The cytotrophoblast is the inner cellular layer that retains mitotic activity. The syncytiotrophoblast is the outer multinucleated layer formed by fusion of cytotrophoblast cells. It is the syncytiotrophoblast that secretes HCG, HPL, estrogen, and progesterone.

112 Formation of Choriodecidual Spaces

  • The trophoblast invades the mouth of endometrial vessels → delivers maternal blood into the chorio-decidual space → 1° wave of invasion.
  • At 20 weeks, the trophoblast invades & destroys the media of spiral vessels which convert into wide-mouthed sinuses → 2° wave of invasion.
Note

Failure of the 2° wave of trophoblastic invasion of spiral arteries is a key mechanism in pre-eclampsia and IUGR (intrauterine growth restriction).

113 Placenta

5.1 Structure

  • Shape: discoid, 500 g, 20–25 cm in diameter.
  • Thickness: 2 cm in center, gradually thins towards periphery.
  • Cord insertion: eccentric.
  • 2 Surfaces:
    • Fetal surface is smooth & covered by membranes.
    • Maternal surface is divided into 12–15 cotyledons.
  • 2 Parts:
    • Fetal part → Chorion frondosum.
    • Maternal part → Decidua basalis.

5.2 Decidual Subdivisions

  • The rest of chorion which is not sharing in placental formation → Chorion laeve.
  • The rest of the decidua overlying the developing ovum → Decidua capsularis.
  • The decidua covering the rest of uterine cavity → Decidua parietalis (vera).

5.3 Functions of Placenta

Gas transport, Nutrition, and Excretion
  • Water & electrolytes by simple diffusion.
  • Glucose, amino acids by facilitated diffusion.
  • Ca, Fe, minerals by active transport.
  • Immunoglobulins & LDL by pinocytosis.
Hormones
  • HCG, HPL, estrogen and progesterone.
Placental Barrier
  • Trophoblast, villous stroma, and fetal blood-vessel endothelium.

5.4 Abnormalities of Placenta

5.4.1 Abnormal Shape
  1. Bipartite: two parts united by vessels & membranes.
  2. Bilobate: two lobes connected by placental tissue.
  3. Placenta succenturiata (accessory lobe).
  4. Placenta circumvallate: a whitish ring composed of decidua is seen around the placenta from its fetal surface.
  5. Placenta fenestrate: an opening in the placenta is present (a part of placenta is missed).
5.4.2 Abnormal Site of Implantation
  • In the lower uterine segment (LUS) → placenta previa.
5.4.3 Abnormal Size
  • Small (associated with IUGR or infarcts).
  • Large (hyperplacentosis): Rh, DM, twins, syphilis.
5.4.4 Other Abnormalities
  • Abnormal adherence → placenta accreta.
  • Tumors of placenta → vesicular mole, choriocarcinoma, chorioangioma.
  • Abnormal attachment of the cord.

114 Umbilical Cord

6.1 Structure

  • Shape: 50 cm long & 2 cm in diameter.
  • Contents: 2 arteries & 1 vein in Wharton jelly.
Mnemonic — AVA

Arteries (×2, carry deoxygenated blood Away from fetus) and Vein (×1, brings oxygenated blood Via to fetus). The single vein carries oxygenated blood to the fetus; two arteries carry reduced (deoxygenated) blood from the fetus to placenta.

6.2 Abnormalities

6.2.1 Length
  • Very long → coiling around fetus, cord prolapse.
  • Short cord → malpresentation & non-engagement.
6.2.2 Abnormal Attachment
  • Central, marginal (battledore), velamentous (vasa previa).
6.2.3 Knots in Cord
  • True and false (false knot = localized collection of Wharton's jelly).
6.2.4 Other
  • Congenital tumors or cysts.
  • Absence of one umbilical artery → congenital fetal malformation (CFMF), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR).
Warning

Single umbilical artery (SUA) is associated with congenital fetal malformations and IUGR — warrants detailed ultrasound assessment.

115 Amniotic Fluid

7.1 Source of Amniotic Fluid

  • Maternal: transudation through placenta & cord (mainly 1st half of pregnancy).
  • Fetal: urine (mainly in the 2nd half of pregnancy).
  • Then it is removed by transudation + fetal swallowing — a dynamic circulation.
  • Balance between production & absorption keeps its volume at normal range.

7.3 Composition

  • 98% clear water.
  • 1–2% crystalloids (slightly alkaline), enzymes, lipids, hormones, and proteins.

7.4 Functions of Amniotic Fluid

During Pregnancy During Labor
  • Protection against trauma.
  • Prevents adhesions between fetal skin & amnion.
  • Prevents infection.
  • Thermoregulation.
  • Allows free movement of fetus → muscular development.
  • Development of alveoli.
  • Helps cervical dilatation.
  • Prevents direct fetal & placental compression by the uterine wall.
  • Wash of birth canal.

7.5 Abnormalities

  • Volume: ↑ → polyhydramnios; ↓ → oligohydramnios.
  • Meconium staining → meconium aspiration syndrome.
  • Inflammation → chorioamnionitis.

116 Hormones

8.1 Proteins (Glycoproteins)

8.1.1 Human Chorionic Gonadotrophin (HCG)
  • Source: syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Can be detected one week before the missed period.
  • It is composed of 2 subunits:
    • α subunit — similar to that of FSH, LH and TSH.
    • β subunit — specific to hCG.
Function Use
Maintenance of corpus luteum (luteotropic). Diagnosis of pregnancy.
Immunological suppressive action. Diagnoses of pregnancy abnormalities (↑ in Down syndrome).
Stimulates fetal testosterone secretion. Diagnoses and follow-up of vesicular mole.
8.1.2 Human Placental Lactogen (HPL)
  • Source: syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Functions:
    • Very similar to GH & prolactin → may stimulate growth of breasts.
    • On CHO & fat (anti-insulin effect) → spares glucose & fatty acids for the fetus.

8.2 Steroid Hormones

8.2.1 Estrogen
  • Estrogens are formed by the conversion of DHEAS (dehydroepiandrosterone) from maternal and fetal sources (adrenal gland mainly) to estradiol (E2) and estriol (E3).
  • It is responsible with progesterone for most of the maternal changes due to pregnancy, especially in the genital tract and breasts.
8.2.2 Progesterone
  • Synthetized by syncytiotrophoblast from maternal cholesterol.
  • Provides a precursor for the fetal adrenal to produce glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids.

117 Genital Changes

I. Whole Uterus

  • Position: Dextrorotation.
  • Size: 2.5 × 5 × 7.5 cm (non-pregnant) → 20 × 25 × 35 cm at term.
  • Shape: Pyriform (non-pregnant) → Globular at 8 weeks → Pyriform at 16 weeks till term.
  • Weight: 50 g (non-pregnant) → 1000 g at term.
  • Volume: 10 mL (non-pregnant) → 5000 mL at term.
  • Consistency: softer due to increased vascularity and presence of amniotic fluid.
  • Contractility: Braxton Hicks contractions that cause discomfort late in pregnancy and may account for false labor pains.

II. Myometrial Changes

  • Hypertrophy: estrogenic effect.
  • Hyperplasia: progesterone effect.
  • Isthmus: area of the cervix between anatomical internal os above and the histological internal os below. It is 4–5 mm and lined by atrophic endometrium.
Significance — Lower Uterine Segment

The isthmus and lower ½ inch of the body of the uterus develop to form the lower uterine segment, which starts to be formed at 12 weeks and gradually increases to 10 cm at full term.

III. Uterine Vasculature

Uterine artery lumen is doubled and uterine blood flow increases progressively, reaching about 500 mL/min at term.

IV. Cervix

Hypertrophy, soft and bluish due to increased vascularity.

V. Vagina

Violet and edematous due to increased vascularity.

VI. Vulva

Violet and edematous due to increased vascularity.

VII. Fallopian Tube

Elongated, tortuous.

VIII. Ovary

Increased size due to increased vascularity, particularly the ovary that contains the corpus luteum.

118 Hematological Changes

2.1 Blood Volume

Increases gradually above the non-pregnant level by 30%–40%, reaching its maximum by 32 weeks, then remains constant till full term.

  • Plasma volume: 45% increase.
  • Red cell mass: 30% increase.

The increase in plasma volume is more than the increase in red blood cell mass, resulting in hemodilution (physiological anemia).

2.2 Blood Indices

  • RBCs and hemoglobin concentrations decrease due to hemodilution.
  • WBCs increase from 7,000 /mm³ to 10,500 /mm³, and up to 16,000 /mm³ during labor.

2.3 Coagulation Factors

  • Platelets: increase or decrease (controversial).
  • Fibrinogen: increases nearly doubled (from 200–400 mg% to 400–600 mg%).
  • Factor VIII: tripled.
  • Factor VII and X: doubled.
  • Fibrinolytic activity: decreased.
Warning — Hypercoagulable State

Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state — increased clotting factors + decreased fibrinolysis → ↑ VTE risk.

119 Cardiovascular System

3.1 Heart

  • Position: the uterus elevates the diaphragm and the apex beat is displaced upwards and to the left.
  • Pulse rate: increases by 10 beats /min.
  • Heart sounds: splitting of the 1st heart sound and appearance of 3rd heart sound.

Most of the changes of the pulse and heart sound are due to hyperdynamic circulation caused by decreased peripheral resistance from peripheral vasodilation due to increased progesterone during pregnancy.

3.2 Cardiac Output (COP)

Increases above the non-pregnant level to reach its maximum of 40% above the non-pregnant level at 20 weeks, then remains constant till term (6.5 L/min). This is due to increased stroke volume and heart rate.

During labor: COP increases more, especially during the second stage of labor, due to uterine contractions and bearing-down efforts.

3.3 Arterial Blood Pressure

Although COP is increased, blood pressure is decreased, especially in the second trimester, due to decreased peripheral resistance.

120 Respiratory System

4.1 Anatomical Changes

The enlarged uterus displaces the diaphragm up to 4 cm, leading to reduced diaphragmatic mobility and a widened subcostal angle.

4.2 Respiratory Functions

  • Tidal volume (amount of gas inspired or expired in each respiration) is increased throughout pregnancy by about 40%.
  • Residual volume is reduced.

4.3 Hyperventilation

Due to increased tidal volume from increased sensitivity of the respiratory center to excess progesterone.

121 Urinary System

5.1 Kidney

Renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) increase by 50%. This leads to increased excretion, so:

  • Decreased creatinine.
  • Decreased urea.
  • Increased excretion of glucose → reduced renal glucose threshold → renal glucosuria.

5.2 Ureters

Dilated due to the relaxing effect of progesterone and pressure against the pelvic brim by the uterus, especially on the right side due to dextrorotation of the uterus.

5.3 Urinary Bladder

Frequency is common in early pregnancy due to pressure on the bladder by the enlarging uterus, congestion of the bladder mucosa, and increased glomerular filtration rate.

122 Gastrointestinal Tract

  • Gingivitis: due to increased vascularity and hypertrophy of interdental papillae.
  • Ptyalism: increased salivation due to increased vascularity of salivary glands.
  • Appetite changes (longing): the pregnant woman likes some foods. Reduced taste buds create the desire for markedly sweet, sour and salt foods.
  • Nausea (morning sickness) and vomiting (emesis gravidarum): starts between 4th and 6th weeks, improves by about 12th week of pregnancy.
  • Heart burn: due to reflux of acidic gastric contents to the esophagus from the relaxing effect of progesterone on the esophageal sphincter.
  • Indigestion and flatulence: due to reduced gastric motility and reflux of alkaline intestinal contents to the stomach from the relaxing effect of progesterone.
  • Constipation: due to reduced large-intestinal motility from the relaxing effect of progesterone.
  • Piles: due to laxity of the wall of the veins by progesterone.
  • Appendix: is displaced upward and laterally.
  • Gall bladder: more tendency for gall bladder stones due to delayed emptying of the gall bladder.
  • Liver: decreased albumin and increased globulin (↓ A/G ratio) and increased alkaline phosphatase enzyme.
Clinical Pearl — Progesterone Is the Common Denominator

Many of the GI/urinary smooth-muscle changes of pregnancy (heartburn, constipation, piles, ureteric dilatation, biliary stasis) are driven by the same mechanism: progesterone-induced smooth-muscle relaxation.

123 Metabolic Changes

  • Weight gain: increased by 10–12 kg in pregnancy.
  • Water metabolism: tendency to water and sodium retention due to increased aldosterone.
  • Protein metabolism: positive nitrogen balance and increased daily requirements for protein intake.
  • Carbohydrate: mild fasting hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. Pregnancy is diabetogenic due to pregnancy hormones.
  • Lipid: increased levels of total lipids and cholesterol.

124 Musculoskeletal Changes

Progressive softening of pelvic joints caused by progressive lordosis and foot flattening.

125 Endocrine Changes

  • Anterior pituitary: hypertrophy and increased vascularity, and increased number of prolactin-secreting cells.
  • Thyroid gland: slight enlargement of the gland due to increased vascularity → increased gland activity: ↑ TBG, ↑ total T3 and T4, ↓ TSH, and ↑ BMR.
  • Parathyroid gland: hypertrophy with increased demand for calcium.
  • Suprarenal gland: hypertrophy due to increased vascularity leading to increased aldosterone and cortisol.

126 Skin Changes

  • Palmar erythema: due to increased estrogen.
  • Chloasma gravidarum: on the face.
  • Linea nigra pigmentation: in midline below the umbilicus.
  • Stria gravidarum: pigmentation in the lower abdomen flanks, inner thigh and buttocks due to mechanical stretching.

127 Breast Changes

I. First Month

  • Increased size and vascularity.
  • Mastodynia (from tingling to frank pain).

II. Second Month — Skin Pigmentation

  • Increased pigmentation of nipple and primary areola.
  • Montgomery tubercles: hypertrophy of the lips of the orifices of the lacteal ducts.

III. Third Month

Colostrum secretion.

IV. Fifth Month

Secondary areola — pigmented area around the primary areola.

129 Diagnosis of Early Pregnancy (1st Trimester)

1.1 Symptoms

  • Secondary amenorrhea.
  • Frequency of micturition: pressure by the enlarged pregnant uterus on the urinary bladder, and pelvic congestion.
  • Nausea and vomiting: usually appears at 6 weeks and usually disappears at 12 weeks of pregnancy.
  • Breast symptoms: enlargement, mastodynia and fullness.

1.2 Signs

  • Skin: discussed before.
  • Breast: discussed before.
  • Vaginal and cervical bluish discoloration of vulva, vagina and cervix due to increased vascularity.
  • Uterine signs:
    • Size: increased.
    • Shape: Globular.
    • Consistency: Soft.
  • Hegar sign: depends on the difference in consistency between the cervix and the body of the uterus (body is softer), detected by manual examination.
  • Palmar sign: painless contractions followed by relaxation, not accompanied by cervical dilatation.

1.3 Investigations

  • Pregnancy test: depends on the detection of hCG either in urine or serum. Serum hCG is sensitive at 25 IU/mL and becomes positive one week before the missed period.
  • Ultrasonography:
    • Ring (4 weeks).
    • Sac (5 weeks).
    • Cardiac activity (6 weeks).
    • FHS — fetal heart sound (10 weeks).

130 Diagnosis of 2nd Trimester (12–28 weeks)

2.1 Symptoms

  • Amenorrhea.
  • Morning sickness and frequency of micturition decrease.
  • Abdominal enlargement.
  • Quickening: first perception of fetal movement by the pregnant woman.
    • Primigravida: 18–20 weeks.
    • Multigravida: 16–18 weeks.

2.2 Signs

  • Skin: discussed before.
  • Breast changes: as 1st trimester + 2ry areola.
  • Uterus:
    • Felt abdominally.
    • Braxton Hicks contractions: intermittent painless contractions detected by abdominal examination.
    • Ballottement (solid body moving in fluid medium) — can be:
      • External ballottement: positive after 20 weeks.
      • Internal ballottement: positive after 16 weeks.
    • Uterine soufflé: soft blowing murmur due to blood flow in the uterine arteries, synchronous with the maternal pulse.
    • Auscultation of FHS and umbilical soufflé (sharp whistling murmur due to blood flow in the umbilical vessels, synchronous with FHS) — a sure sign of pregnancy.

2.3 Quickening Window

Clinical Pearl — Synchrony Distinguishes the Two Soufflés

Uterine soufflé is synchronous with the maternal pulse (blood flow in uterine arteries).
Umbilical soufflé is synchronous with the FHS (blood flow in umbilical vessels) and is itself a sure sign of pregnancy.

131 Diagnosis of 3rd Trimester (28–40 weeks)

3.1 Symptoms

  • The same as in the 2nd trimester, in addition to the symptoms of engagement like increased frequency of micturition and lightening.
  • Shelfing.

3.2 Signs

More evident.

  • Ultrasonography is diagnostic.

3.3 Sure Signs of Pregnancy

  • Palpation of fetal parts.
  • Palpation of fetal movement.
  • Auscultation of FHS.
  • Auscultation of umbilical soufflé.
  • Detection of fetal parts by ultrasonography.
Warning — Probable vs. Sure Signs

Many 1st-trimester findings (amenorrhea, nausea, breast changes, Hegar's sign, positive hCG) are probable signs only. A diagnosis of pregnancy becomes definitive only when one of the sure signs above is documented — direct palpation of fetal parts / movement, auscultation of FHS, or visualization by ultrasound.

133 Definition & Objectives of Antenatal Care

Definition

Preventive obstetric program including a group of observations, medical care, investigations and advices aiming at getting pregnancy, labor and puerperium as near to normal as possible.

Objectives

  • Identification of high risk pregnancy.
  • Monitoring of high risk pregnancy.
  • Early detection of complications.
  • Giving advice for all pregnant patients.
  • Management of common minor problems.

  • ABO grouping and Rh typing.
  • Urine analysis for albumin, sugar and bacterial count.
  • CBC.
  • One-hour postprandial glucose test.
  • HBsAg.
  • Rubella antibodies.
  • Cervicovaginal smear.

5.2 For Specific Populations

  • Sexually transmitted diseases (HIV, Syphilis, …etc).
  • Thalassemia (A in Asians; B in Mediterranean).
  • Sickle cell anemia (in black women).
  • Tuberculin skin test.
  • Screen for short cervix by transvaginal ultrasound.
  • Screening or diagnostic testing for trisomy 21 and other genetic disorders.
  • Screen for red blood cell antibodies and administer anti-D immune globulin to D-negative patients.

139 Antenatal Advice — Lifestyle

  • Teeth care: daily cleaning by tooth paste.
  • Clothing: light clothes, avoid high-heeled shoes.
  • Bathing: avoid hot bathing and swimming pools.
  • Sexual activity: whenever there is a threat of abortion or preterm labor, coitus should be avoided. Otherwise, it is allowed with less frequency.
  • Sleep: 8 hours at night and 2 in the afternoon.
  • Travelling: avoided if there is a risk of abortion or PTL (preterm labor).
  • Special habits: avoid smoking, alcohol.

140 Immunization During Pregnancy

  • Live attenuated viral vaccines such as MMR & polio are contraindicated.
  • Inactivated viral vaccines such as influenza and rabies are safe.
Warning — Live Vaccines Contraindicated

Live attenuated vaccines (MMR, polio) are contraindicated in pregnancy. Inactivated vaccines (influenza, rabies) are safe.

141 Exposure to Infection

Avoided for fear of teratogenicity — e.g. Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, …etc.

142 Exposure to Irradiation

Avoided whether diagnostic or therapeutic.

  • < 5 rads: may not harm.
  • 5 – 50 rads: may harm.
  • 50 – 100 rads: CFMF (Central Nervous System MalFormation).
Clinical Pearl — Radiation Thresholds

Below 5 rads is generally considered safe; 5–50 rads is a harm-risk window; above 50 rads is associated with CFMF (CNS malformation). Note: 1 rad = 0.01 Gy.

143 Medications

  • Not to be taken without obstetrician advice.
  • All drugs should be avoided in the first trimester.
  • Give the smallest effective dose for the smallest time.

144 Warning Symptoms of Pregnancy

  1. Any symptoms suggestive of PIH: persistent headache, blurring of vision, vomiting, and epigastric pain.
  2. Swelling of the face, fingers and limbs.
  3. Excessive watery vaginal discharge.
  4. Vaginal bleeding.
  5. Fever, dyspnea.
  6. Severe abdominal pain.
  7. Decreased fetal kicks.
Warning — Seek Immediate Care

Any of the seven symptoms above warrants urgent review. Headaches + visual changes + epigastric pain = think PIH / pre-eclampsia. Decreased fetal kicks may signal fetal compromise.

02

Definition & Etiology

Foundational concepts and the underlying causes of pregnancy loss before viability
Definition
Termination of pregnancy before viability of the fetus, i.e. before 20 weeks.
Incidence: Varies between 12–25%.

Etiology

The following are established causes of spontaneous abortion:

  • Chromosomal abnormalities — 50% of early abortions.
  • Maternal infections — Acute fever.
  • Trauma — External or during operations.
  • Immunological causes — Systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid antibodies.
  • Uterine defects — Septum, Asherman's syndrome.
  • Idiopathic — No identifiable cause.
  • Endocrine — Progesterone deficiency, hypothyroidism.
  • Drugs and environmental causes.
  • Maternal anoxia and malnutrition.
💎
Clinical Pearl: Chromosomal abnormalities account for ~50% of early abortions, making them the single most important etiology. Recurrent genetic testing of abortus tissue is therefore a key investigation in habitual abortion.
03

Threatened Abortion

The earliest, most benign form — pregnancy is still viable and may continue

Clinical Picture

  • Symptoms and signs of pregnancy coincide with its duration.
  • Vaginal bleeding: slight or mild, bright red in colour.
  • Pain: absent or slight.
  • Cervix: closed.
  • U/S: living fetus.

Management

  • Bed rest and avoid strenuous activity and sexual intercourse.
  • Progesterone.
  • Follow up.
📝
Note: The defining feature of threatened abortion is a closed cervix with a living fetus on ultrasound. The pregnancy is still viable and may continue normally.
04

Inevitable Abortion

Vaginal bleeding becomes heavy and the cervix dilates — the pregnancy cannot be saved

Clinical Picture

  • Symptoms and signs of pregnancy.
  • Heavy vaginal bleeding with clots.
  • Suprapubic colic referred to the back.
  • Dilated cervix.

Management

  • Medical: Oxytocin drip, prostaglandins.
  • Surgical: Evacuation.
  • Blood transfusion if needed.
⚠️
Warning: The combination of heavy bleeding + dilated cervix + colicky suprapubic pain referred to the back indicates the abortion is now inevitable and active management is required to prevent further blood loss.
05

Complete Abortion

All products of conception have been expelled and the uterus is empty

Definition

All products of conception have been expelled from the uterus.

Clinical Picture

  • Bleeding: mild and decreases.
  • Cessation of pain.
  • Closed cervix.
  • Uterus: slightly larger than normal.
  • Ultrasound: shows empty cavity.

Management

Ecbolics and antibiotics and no evacuation.

💎
Clinical Pearl: Unlike incomplete abortion, complete abortion requires no evacuation — only ecbolics (uterotonic agents) and antibiotics. Ultrasound confirmation of an empty cavity is the key diagnostic step.
06

Incomplete Abortion

Part of the products of conception are retained, requiring evacuation

Definition

Retention of a part of the products of conception inside the uterus.

Clinical Picture

  • Passage of a part of the conception products.
  • Bleeding is continuous.
  • Opened cervix.
  • Uterus: less than period of amenorrhea.
  • Ultrasonography: shows the retained contents.

Management

Evacuation (medical or surgical).

⚠️
Warning: The uterus is smaller than the period of amenorrhea would suggest because some products have already been passed. Continuous bleeding through an opened cervix mandates evacuation to prevent haemorrhage and sepsis.
07

Cervical Abortion

The products of conception are separated from the uterus but trapped in the cervical canal

Definition

The products of conception is separated from the uterus but retained inside the cervical canal.

Clinical Picture

  • Bleeding with severe lower abdominal pain referred to the back.
  • On examination, the products of conception are felt through the dilated cervix.

Management

Cervical dilatation and evacuation of cervical contents followed by curettage.

📝
Note: Cervical abortion is a clinical situation where the products of conception have separated from the uterine wall but become impacted in the cervical canal. Manual removal via cervical dilatation is required.
08

Missed Abortion

The fetus has died in utero but is retained — symptoms of pregnancy regress

Definition

Retention of dead products of conception.

Symptoms

  • Regression of pregnancy symptoms as nausea, vomiting and breast symptoms.
  • The abdomen does not increase.
  • The fetal movements are not felt.
  • A dark brown vaginal discharge may occur (prune juice discharge).

Signs

  • The uterus fails to grow and becomes firmer.
  • The cervix is closed.
  • Absent fetal heart sounds.

Investigations

  • Pregnancy test becomes negative within two weeks from the ovum death.
  • Ultrasound shows a collapsed gestational sac, absent fetal heart pulsations or fetal movement.

Treatment

Evacuation.

  • <12 weeks: Surgical evacuation is done.
  • >12 weeks: Medical evacuation (e.g. by Prostaglandins or Oxytocin).
💎
Clinical Pearl: The prune juice discharge is a classic clinical sign of missed abortion — old, dark-brown altered blood from a long-retained dead pregnancy. The uterus shrinks and firms up rather than growing.

Septic Abortion

Any type of abortion complicated by infection — a potentially life-threatening emergency

Definition

It is any type of abortion complicated by infection.

Microbiology

The most causative organisms are:

  • E. coli
  • Bacteroids
  • Anaerobic streptococci
  • Clostridia
  • Streptococci
  • Staphylococci

Clinical Picture

General Examination
  • Pyrexia, rigors and tachycardia.
  • Malaise, sweating, headache, and joint pain.
  • Jaundice.
Abdominal Examination
  • Tenderness, rigidity and distension.
Local Examination
  • Offensive vaginal discharge.
  • Tender uterus.

Treatment

  • Resuscitation
  • Prompt administration of intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics followed by uterine evacuation.
  • Rest in semi-sitting position.
  • Observation for vital signs.
  • Oxytocin infusion: to control bleeding and enhances expulsion.
  • Evacuation of the uterus after commencing IV therapy.
  • Hysterectomy may be the last choice to safe life.
🆘
Danger — Emergency: Septic abortion is a life-threatening condition. Resuscitation must begin immediately, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics must be started before any evacuation, and hysterectomy may be required as a last resort to save the patient's life.

Recurrent (Habitual) Abortion

Three or more consecutive spontaneous abortions — requires structured workup

Definition

Three (two by some authors) or more consecutive spontaneous abortions.

Causes

(Persistent causes of abortion)

  • Genetic factors: Paternal chromosomal rearrangements, embryonic chromosomal abnormalities.
  • Anatomical factors: Septate uterus / cervical incompetence.
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome.
  • Endocrinological abnormalities: DM, thyroid abnormalities.
  • Thrombophilias.
  • Infective agents: STORCH.

Investigations

  1. Laboratory:
    • Urine analysis: for pus cell (UTI), proteins (nephritis) & glucose (DM).
    • Blood: serum creatinine, antiphospholipid antibodies and coagulation profile.
  2. U/S: To exclude submucous fibroid, incompetent cervix.
  3. HSG (Hysterosalpingography): To exclude submucous fibroid, incompetent cervix, intrauterine adhesions.
  4. Hysteroscopy: To exclude submucous fibroid, intrauterine adhesions, congenital anomalies of uterine cavity.
  5. Genetic study: of the abortus to exclude chromosomal aberrations.

Treatment

a- Before Pregnancy: Treat Detectable Causes
  • Hysteroscopic resection of uterine septum or submucous fibroid.
  • Treatment of luteal phase defect.
  • Strict control of DM.
b- During Pregnancy
  • Rest: Physical, mental (sedation) and sexual abstinence.
  • Diet: Good balanced diet.
  • Natural progesterone: if proved progesterone deficiency.
  • Others: Strict DM control, cerclage for incompetent cervix, heparin for the antiphospholipid syndrome.
💎
Clinical Pearl — STORCH Mnemonic: The classic congenital-infective causes of recurrent pregnancy loss are remembered as Syphilis · Toxoplasmosis · Other (varicella, parvovirus B19) · Rubella · Cytomegalovirus · Herpes / HIV. Always screen for these in recurrent abortion workup.
💎
Clinical Pearl — Cerclage for Cervical Incompetence: McDonald cerclage (a purse-string suture around the cervix) placed at 12–14 weeks is the standard surgical treatment for cervical incompetence in subsequent pregnancies.

Definition, Incidence & Sites

Implantation of the fertilized ovum outside the uterine cavity
Definition
Implantation of the fertilized ovum outside the uterine cavity.
Incidence: 2% of all pregnancies.

Sites of Ectopic Pregnancy

  • Tubal pregnancy — the commonest site (99% of all ectopic pregnancy).
  • Cervical pregnancy.
  • Pregnancy in rudimentary horn.
  • Corneal (cornual) pregnancy.
  • Ovarian pregnancy.
  • Abdominal pregnancy.
  • Broad ligamentary pregnancy.
💎

Etiology & Risk Factors

Anything that impairs tubal transport or the endometrial environment
  • PID — Pelvic Inflammatory Disease.
  • Previous ectopic pregnancy.
  • Previous tubal surgery.
  • Previous pelvic or abdominal surgery.
  • Tubal pathology.
  • IUCD — Intrauterine Contraceptive Device.
  • Tubal sterilization.
  • Cigarette smoking.
⚠️

Investigations

β-hCG, ultrasound, discriminatory zone, progesterone, laparoscopy

149 Quantitative Beta Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (β-hCG)

If the test is negative, normal and abnormal pregnancy including ectopic are excluded.

Doubling time
  • In normal pregnancy, the β-hCG level is doubling every 48 hours during the first 42 days of gestation.
  • Ectopic pregnancy usually shows less than 66% increase in β-hCG level within 48 hours.
💎
Clinical Pearl: A <66% rise in β-hCG over 48 hours in early pregnancy is highly suspicious for ectopic — normal pregnancy doubles in 48 hours during the first 42 days.

150 Ultrasound and Doppler

Visualization of intrauterine sac + fetal pulsation: mostly excludes ectopic pregnancy.

Signs of ectopic pregnancy
  1. Empty uterine cavity.
  2. Free fluid in Douglas pouch.
  3. Adnexal swelling: if associated with fetal pulsation → sure sign of ectopic.

Expectant Management

Watchful waiting — only in highly selected, stable patients

Indications

  • Women who are clinically stable.
  • Size of the ectopic pregnancy is < 3.5 cm with no visible heartbeat on TVU.

For unruptured tubal ectopic in hemodynamically stable patients

Indications

Interstitial (Cornual) Pregnancy

Implantation in the proximal tubal segment within the muscular uterine wall
  • Interstitial pregnancy implants in the proximal tubal segment that lies within the muscular uterine wall.
  • Swelling lateral to the insertion of the round ligament is the characteristic anatomic finding.
  • Because of the proximity of these pregnancies to the uterine and ovarian arteries, there is a risk of severe hemorrhage.
  • Surgical management involves cornual resection either by laparotomy or laparoscopy.
  • The risk of uterine rupture with subsequent pregnancies is high. Thus, careful observation of these women, along with consideration of elective cesarean delivery, is warranted.
🛑
Danger: Cornual pregnancy can rupture catastrophically because of the rich anastomosis between uterine and ovarian arteries. Always plan for large-bore IV access and blood on standby, and counsel the patient about future uterine rupture risk and elective cesarean delivery in subsequent pregnancies.

Ovarian, Cervical, Cesarean-Scar, Heterotopic & PUL

Special ectopic types — diagnostic criteria and management

Ovarian Ectopic Pregnancy

C/P is the same as tubal ectopic. The diagnosis is by Spiegelberg criteria:

  1. The pregnancy occupies the site of the ovary and cannot be separated from it & surrounded by healthy ovarian tissues.
  2. The gestational sac is connected to the uterus by ovarian ligament.
  3. No adhesion between the tube and the ovary.
  4. Fallopian tube on the affected side is healthy & the mesosalpinx on the affected side is free from hemorrhage or masses.

Treatment: oophorectomy.

Cervical Ectopic Pregnancy

Two diagnostic criteria are necessary for confirmation of cervical pregnancy:

  • The presence of cervical glands opposite the placental attachment site.
  • A portion of or the entire placenta must be located below the entrance of the uterine vessels.

Early diagnosis of cervical pregnancy is important to avoid severe hemorrhage and subsequent hysterectomy in these women.

Treatment: medically by methotrexate or by cervical evacuation and packing. In severe bleeding, hysterectomy is done.

Cesarean Section Scar Pregnancy

Implantation of the pregnancy sac within the scar of a previous cesarean delivery. It can cause serious maternal morbidity and mortality from massive hemorrhage.

Differentiating between a cervical ectopic pregnancy and a cesarean delivery scar pregnancy can be difficult. There are four sonographic criteria that must be met to reach the diagnosis:

  1. An empty uterine cavity.
  2. An empty cervical canal.
  3. A gestational sac in the anterior part of the uterine isthmus.
  4. Absence of healthy myometrium between the bladder and gestational sac.

Imaging with three-dimensional color Doppler or magnetic resonance imaging can aid in the evaluation.

Treatment: using methotrexate, or resection by laparoscopy or laparotomy.

Heterotopic Pregnancy

  • Presence of a uterine pregnancy with an extrauterine pregnancy.
  • When a tubal pregnancy coexists with a uterine pregnancy, potassium chloride can be injected into the tubal pregnancy sac.
  • Methotrexate is contraindicated due to the detrimental effects on the normal pregnancy.

Pregnancy of Unknown Location (PUL)

A condition in which there is a positive pregnancy test and absent intrauterine gestational sac without any evidence of extrauterine pregnancy. This term is used till a final diagnosis is made, either an abnormal or normal intrauterine pregnancy or ectopic.

2Definition

Tumors arising from the trophoblastic cells of the placenta.

Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (GTD) is an umbrella term for a spectrum of pregnancy-related tumors that originate from the abnormal proliferation of trophoblast — the outer layer of the blastocyst that would normally form the placenta.

PearlAlthough GTD arises from placental tissue, it is a tumor, not a true pregnancy, and is unique in that it is a fetal allograft — the host (mother) is exposed to paternal antigens.

3Classifications

GTD is divided into two broad categories: Benign (hydatidiform mole) and Malignant (gestational trophoblastic tumor).

A. Benign — Hydatidiform Mole
  • Complete hydatidiform mole
  • Partial hydatidiform mole
B. Malignant — Gestational Trophoblastic Tumor (GTT)
  • Invasive mole
  • Choriocarcinoma
  • Placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT)
WarnAlthough the benign moles are histologically non-invasive, they still carry a measurable risk of malignant transformation: ~16% for complete mole and ~0.5% for partial mole. Strict postmolar follow-up is mandatory.

4Hydatidiform Mole (Vesicular Mole)

Definition

It is a benign tumor of trophoblastic tissue in which hydropic degeneration of the chorionic villi occurs.

The word "hydatid" derives from the Greek hydatis ("a drop of water") — referring to the characteristic fluid-filled vesicles that replace normal chorionic villi. "Vesicular mole" is the descriptive synonym.

5Incidence

Molar pregnancy is rare but not negligible. The two subtypes differ in frequency.

1 / 1 000
Complete Hydatidiform Mole
3 / 1 000
Partial Hydatidiform Mole

Reported per pregnancy. The partial mole is roughly three times more common than the complete mole, but the complete mole has a much higher risk of malignant sequelae.

6Types — Complete & Partial Mole

The two histological types differ in their genetic origin, fetal involvement, and clinical course.

(i) Complete Hydatidiform Mole
  • The uterus is full of vesicles.
  • No embryo is present.
  • It is the result of fertilization of an anucleated ovum (has no chromosomes) with a normal sperm → duplicate → 46 chromosomes of paternal origin only.
(ii) Partial Hydatidiform Mole
  • A part of trophoblastic tissue only shows molar changes.
  • A fetus or parts of it is present.
  • It is the result of fertilization of an ovum by 2 sperms → the chromosomal number is 69 chromosomes.

7Risk Factors

Five risk factors are recognised for molar pregnancy.

  1. Age: the most consistent risk factor in all regions and races — pregnancies below the age of 16 years and above the age of 45 years have the highest risk.
  2. Previous GTN (Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia):
    • Following one mole, the risk is less than 2%.
    • Following two moles, the risk rises to 16%.
    • Following three moles, the risk becomes 50%.
  3. Family history
  4. Dietary factor: low intake of animal fat may be associated with increased risk of complete mole.
  5. Ethnic groups: more common in Asian populations.
WarnA patient with a history of two or more molar pregnancies has a substantial risk of recurrence — careful pre-conception counselling and early first-trimester ultrasound in subsequent pregnancies are indicated.

8Pathology

Gross appearance

The uterus is full of thin walled, translucent, grape-like vesicles of different sizes.

Microscopic appearance

  • Trophoblastic proliferation, with mitotic activity affecting both syncytial and cytotrophoblastic layers.
  • Leads to excessive secretion of:
    • hCG
    • Chorionic thyrotrophin
    • Progesterone

Ovarian effect

  • The high hCG → multiple theca lutein cysts.
  • The high hCG → exaggeration of the early pregnancy symptoms and signs.
PearlThe triad of (1) trophoblastic proliferation, (2) theca lutein cysts, and (3) exaggerated pregnancy symptoms is explained by a single mechanism — massive hCG secretion.

10Clinical Features

The classic clinical picture of a complete hydatidiform mole:

  • Amenorrhea.
  • Vaginal bleeding.
  • Symptoms of pregnancy in exaggerated form.
  • Early onset pre-eclampsia before 20th weeks.
  • Hyperemesis gravidarum.
  • Hyperthyroidism.
  • Complications of theca lutein cyst of ovary (rupture, torsion).
DangerPre-eclampsia before 20 weeks is a red-flag finding. In a normal pregnancy, pre-eclampsia essentially never occurs this early — its presence should always prompt evaluation for molar pregnancy.
PearlAll three unusual features (hyperemesis, hyperthyroidism, early pre-eclampsia) are explained by excess hCG, which cross-reacts with the TSH receptor (thyrotrophic effect) and stimulates the corpus luteum / ovarian theca cells.

11Examinations

Abdominal examination

  • Uterus is large and its consistency is soft and doughy.
  • No fetal part or fetal heart can be detected.

Pelvic examination

  • Sometimes grape like vesicles of mole may be detected which confirm the diagnosis.
WarnThe "size > dates" discrepancy (uterus larger than expected for gestational age) combined with absent fetal heart sounds is a classic bedside clue.

12Investigations — Ultrasound

Ultrasound is the cornerstone of diagnosis.

  1. Snow storm appearance — uterine cavity filled with multiple sonolucent areas of varying size and shape.
  2. Absence of fetus.
  3. Theca lutein cyst of ovary — bilateral multicystic ovarian enlargement due to hyperstimulation by hCG.

β-hCG is highly elevated (> 100 000 mIU/mL) in complete hydatidiform mole.

This is dramatically higher than the hCG level of a normal pregnancy of the same gestational age, and reflects the exuberant trophoblastic proliferation.

14Treatment

Evacuation of the uterus

Evacuation by suction curettage is performed under general anesthesia with available blood.

  • Suction curettage is the method of choice for uterine evacuation in molar pregnancy.
  • Cross-matched blood must be available because of the high risk of hemorrhage.

Hysterectomy

Hysterectomy is indicated for:

  • Older women or those of high parity.
  • Severe uncontrolled hemorrhage.

Theca lutein cysts

Theca lutein cysts regress in size and need no special treatment, unless complicated (rupture, torsion).

Histopathological examination

All evacuated specimens must be sent for histopathological examination to confirm the diagnosis and rule out choriocarcinoma.

Anti-D prophylaxis

Anti-D should be given to non-sensitized Rh negative mother.

DangerFailure to perform histopathological examination on the evacuated specimen is a medico-legal pitfall — a hidden focus of choriocarcinoma may be missed and present months later with metastatic disease.

15Postmolar Follow-up

Strict surveillance after evacuation is mandatory because of the malignant transformation risk.

  • hCG measurement every 1 to 2 weeks until it becomes –ve.
  • Monthly for 1 year after hCG becomes –ve is recommended for all patients with molar gestation.

Aim is to detect any changes suggestive of malignancy because:

  • 16% of complete hydatidiform mole and
  • 0.5% of partial hydatidiform mole

undergo malignant transformation.

WarnContraception must be used during the entire 1-year surveillance period — a new pregnancy would invalidate the β-hCG trend and delay detection of malignancy.

16Contraception

The combined oral pills is the method of choice.

Combined oral contraceptives (COC) are preferred during the postmolar surveillance period because:

  • They are highly effective at preventing pregnancy (which would confuse hCG follow-up).
  • They regulate withdrawal bleeding, allowing earlier detection of abnormal uterine bleeding as a warning sign.

17Chemotherapy Indications

Chemotherapy is indicated if any of the following are present:

  • hCG titer rises or plateaus during follow-up.
  • Evidence of choriocarcinoma.
  • Persistent uterine bleeding and positive hCG.
PearlGestational trophoblastic neoplasia is one of the most chemotherapy-curable solid tumors in humans — even metastatic choriocarcinoma has a cure rate > 90% with appropriate single-agent or multi-agent chemotherapy (e.g. methotrexate, actinomycin-D, EMA-CO).

18Subsequent Pregnancy

In any subsequent pregnancy after a molar gestation:

An early ultrasound should be performed in all subsequent pregnancies because of the 1–2% risk of recurrence.

  • Confirm the new pregnancy is intrauterine.
  • Exclude a new molar pregnancy at the earliest possible gestational age.
  • Send the placenta / products of conception for histopathology after delivery.
WarnA "false-positive" β-hCG from a recurrent mole must be excluded by ultrasound before the new pregnancy is allowed to continue unmonitored.

22Definition & Incidence

Definition

Systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on two separate occasions at least 6 hours apart during pregnancy.

Incidence: 10–20 % of all pregnancies.

DangerIt is the second leading cause of maternal death and perinatal morbidity and mortality after severe bleeding. Every antenatal clinic must screen for it at every visit.

23Classification of HTN in Pregnancy

Hypertension in pregnancy (HTNP) is classified into four categories:

1Chronic (pre-existing) hypertension

Diagnosed before the 20th week of gestation.

2Gestational hypertension

New hypertension after 20 weeks without proteinuria.

3Preeclampsia

Hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria / end-organ involvement.

4Chronic HTN + superimposed preeclampsia

Worsening BP + proteinuria after 20 weeks in a chronic hypertensive.

PearlThe 20-week threshold is the pivot of the classification: HTN appearing before 20 weeks = chronic; after 20 weeks with proteinuria = preeclampsia.

24Chronic (Pre-existing) Hypertension

Definition

BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg diagnosed either before pregnancy or develops before 20 weeks of gestation or both or persists more than 42 days postpartum.

Causes

  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Primary aldosteronism
  • Pheochromocytoma

Management

Mild chronic hypertensionSevere chronic hypertension
Serial antenatal screening. Start antihypertensive medications e.g. alpha methyldopa, labetalol or calcium channel blockers to maintain blood pressure less than 160/100 mmHg.
Serial blood pressure measurement. Serial BP measurement and urine protein assessment.
Serial urine protein assessment. Delivery at 37 wks.
Termination of pregnancy at term by induction of labor if cervix is favorable. If delivery planned before 34 wks, mother should be given steroids for fetal lung maturity.
WarnTarget BP in severe chronic HTN is < 160/100 mmHg — over-treating below this risks reduced uteroplacental perfusion and IUGR. The "lower is better" rule of chronic HTN in non-pregnant patients does not apply here.

25Chronic HTN + Superimposed Preeclampsia

Pre-existing hypertension associated with further worsening of BP after 20 weeks of gestation, followed by proteinuria (≥ 3 g/24 h).

NoteManagement is the same as preeclampsia (see §13). The superimposed category is reserved for the chronic hypertensive who develops new proteinuria or end-organ involvement — it carries the worst prognosis of the four categories.

26Gestational Hypertension

Definition

Pregnancy induced hypertension develops after 20 weeks of gestation with absence of proteinuria.

Diagnosis

  • No symptoms of preeclampsia.
  • Laboratory tests are normal.
  • Proteinuria is absent.
  • The key finding is sustained elevation of BP.

Management

  • Conservative outpatient management.
  • Serial laboratory testing to rule out preeclampsia development.
  • Antihypertensive treatment is only indicated if blood pressure exceeded 160/100 mmHg.

Time of Delivery

At term by either vaginal delivery if the cervix is favorable or by cesarean section.

WarnUp to 25–50% of gestational hypertension eventually progresses to preeclampsia — do not stop monitoring until 42 days postpartum. BP that persists > 42 days reclassifies the patient as chronic hypertensive.

27Preeclampsia — Definition, Risk Factors, Etiology

Definition

The presence of hypertension of at least 140/90 mmHg recorded on two separate occasions at least 4 hours apart and in the presence of at least 300 mg protein in a 24 hour's collection of urine after the 20th week gestation in a previously normotensive women.

Incidence: 5–10 % of pregnancies.

Risk factors

  • More in primigravida.
  • Conditions in which the placenta is enlarged (diabetes mellitus, multiple pregnancies, hydrops).
  • Pre-existing hypertension or renal diseases.
  • Pre-existing vascular disease (e.g. diabetes, autoimmune vasculitis).
  • Obesity.

Etiology

Preeclampsia is a disorder of unknown etiology that is peculiar to human pregnancy. Many theories regarding its etiology have been suggested, including:

  • Abnormal placentation.
  • Abnormal trophoblastic invasion. In normal implantation, the uterine spiral arteries undergo extensive remodeling as they are invaded by endovascular trophoblasts. In preeclampsia, there is incomplete trophoblastic invasion. Endothelial damage leads to pathologic capillary leak that can present in the mother as rapid weight gain, nondependent edema (face or hands), pulmonary edema, hemoconcentration, or a combination.
  • Immunologic phenomena.
  • Renin-angiotensin system.
  • Coagulation abnormalities.
  • Abnormal cardiovascular adaptation.
  • Dietary factors.
  • Genetic factors.
  • Vasculopathy and inflammatory changes.
PearlThe unifying hypothesis: incomplete trophoblastic invasion of spiral arteries → placental hypoperfusion → release of anti-angiogenic factors → systemic endothelial dysfunction → hypertension, proteinuria, edema.

28Pathology of Preeclampsia

Vasospasm and hemoconcentration are the main pathology of preeclampsia and account for the development of hypertension. They lead to vascular changes and local hypoxia of the surrounding tissues leading to hemorrhage, necrosis and other pathological changes.

  • 1. Central nervous system: ischemia, hemorrhages and oedema.
  • 2. Liver: subcapsular hemorrhage, periportal necrosis and infarctions.
  • 3. Kidney: decrease in renal blood flow, glomerular damage (glomerular endotheliosis) leading to:
    • decrease glomerular filtration rate by about 50%.
    • loss of protein in urine (albuminuria).
  • 4. Heart and lungs: myocardial and endocardial hemorrhage and necrosis. Lungs shows hemorrhage and secondary bronchopneumonia.
  • 5. Retina: hemorrhage, exudate and rarely retinal detachment in severe cases.
  • 6. Coagulation status:
    • Fibrin production is increased.
    • Fibrinolytic activity is decreased.
    • Factor VII, factor VIII concentrations are increased.
    • Fibrin degradation products (FDP) is increased.
    • Thrombocytopenia.
  • 7. Endocrine glands: necrosis and hemorrhage in pituitary, pancreas and adrenal glands.
DangerEvery organ system is a target — but the brain, liver, kidney, and coagulation system drive most of the maternal mortality. Glomerular filtration rate drops by ~50% even in "mild" preeclampsia.

29Diagnostic Criteria & Investigations

Signs
  1. Hypertension: Blood pressure: ≥ 140/90 mmHg after 20 weeks of gestation in a woman with previously normal blood pressure.
  2. Proteinuria: It is urinary protein greater than 0.3 gm/L in 24 hours collection or spot urine protein/creatinine ratio of 0.3 or greater.
Symptoms
  • Headache and blurred vision.
  • Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain.
  • Nausea and vomiting.
Investigations
  1. Complete urine examination: for proteinuria.
  2. Kidney function.
  3. Liver function tests.
  4. Coagulation status: Platelet count, fibrinogen and FDP as DIC may develop.
  5. Fundus examination.
  6. Fetal assessment tests:
    • Daily fetal movement assessment (kick counts).
    • Non stress test (NST) twice weekly.
    • Biophysical profile if nonreactive NST.
    • Amniotic fluid volume assessment weekly.
    • Ultrasound evaluation of fetal growth every 3 weeks.
WarnSymptoms are the RED FLAGS: headache + blurred vision + epigastric pain is the classic preeclampsia-with-severe-features triad — do not wait for BP to climb further.

30Screening of Preeclampsia

  • Mid-trimester mean blood pressure: If it is more than 90 mmHg, there is increased risk of developing PE.
  • Urinary assay for microalbuminuria.
  • Blood urate: serial increase is a warning sign.
  • Doppler velocimetry to detect uteroplacental hypoperfusion.
PearlUterine artery Doppler in the 2nd trimester (22–24 w) showing notch or raised resistance index is the single best non-invasive screen for preeclampsia in a primigravida.

31Types of Preeclampsia

Preeclampsia (mild)

Blood pressure < 160/110 mmHg with absence of symptoms and normal laboratory data.

Preeclampsia with severe features

Blood pressure: ≥ 160/110 mmHg on two occasions at least six hours apart in a woman on bed rest with:

  • ↑ Liver enzymes, hepatic hemorrhage & rupture.
  • ↑ Creatinine, acute tubular necrosis & renal failure.
  • HELLP syndrome.
  • IUGR, oligohydramnios, IUFD, placental abruption.
  • Presence of symptoms like headache, blurring of vision, epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, nausea and vomiting.
Other features
  • Oliguria (less than 500 ml of urine in 24 hours).
  • Pulmonary edema or cyanosis.
  • Thrombocytopenia (<100,000 platelets/ µL).
NoteIn 2013, The ACOG replaces the term of severe PE with a new term "preeclampsia with severe features".

32Complications of Preeclampsia

5Maternal effects
  • Accidental hemorrhage.
  • Acute renal failure.
  • HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelet count).
  • Acute pulmonary edema & heart failure.
  • Ante partum eclampsia.
  • DIC.
  • Cerebrovascular accidents.
  • Residual and recurrent hypertension.
6Fetal effects
  • IUGR: due to the placental insufficiency caused by vasospasm.
  • IUFD.
DangerHELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelet count) is the most feared maternal complication — and the only cure is delivery, regardless of gestational age.

33Treatment of Preeclampsia

Prophylactic

  1. Proper antenatal care: for early detection of high risk patients.
  2. Low dose aspirin.
  3. Calcium.
  4. Antioxidants.

Curative

Delivery remains the ultimate treatment for pre-eclampsia. When possible, vaginal delivery is preferable to avoid the added physiologic stressors of cesarean delivery. In case of cesarean delivery, regional anesthesia is preferred because it carries less maternal risk. In the presence of coagulopathy, use of regional anesthesia is contraindicated.

7General measures

Hospitalization for observation of maternal and fetal conditions.

8Medical treatment
  • Antihypetensive drugs: alpha-methyl-dopa (Aldomet), calcium channel blockers (Nifedipine), Labetalol and Hydralazine.
  • Diuretics: in cases of heart failure and pulmonary edema.

9Obstetric management

  • Magnesium sulphate to prevent convulsions (Its dose is 4–6 g loading dose followed by a continuous infusion at a rate of 1–2 gm/hour).
  • Control of maternal blood pressure within a safe range.
  • Induction of labor to initiate delivery.
DangerMgSO4 is the only drug that prevents eclampsia — every preeclampsia admission should be on a MgSO4 protocol. Antihypertensives control BP; they do not prevent fits.

34Timing of Delivery & Antihypertensive Dosing

Timing of delivery in preeclampsia

  • At ≥ 37 weeks → Delivery.
  • < 37 weeks → Expectant management (maternal and fetal monitoring).

Timing of delivery in preeclampsia with severe features

  • At ≥ 34 weeks: Delivery is indicated, after rapid maternal stabilization.
  • < 34 weeks: Expectant management is acceptable in a tertiary care center if blood pressure is adequately controlled with antihypertensive agents, good fetal and maternal status. Delivery is indicated if there is maternal or fetal complication.

Antihypertensives

35Severe PE — Flowchart & Indications

Severe PE diagnosed on the basis of blood pressure measurement or transient laboratory abnormality — at a tertiary health care hospital with on-site blood banking, ICU & NICU services.

Medications
  • Magnesium sulphate for 48 hours.
  • Antihypertensive drugs (initial I.V. then oral).
  • Steroids.
Observation
  • Maternal: Bp, urine protein, symptoms & weight.
  • Fetal: kicks, NST, BPP & serial U/S for growth & amniotic fluid.
Maternal indications (Urgent Delivery)
  • Symptoms.
  • Eclampsia (2%).
  • HELLP (20%).
  • Uncontrolled BP.
  • End organ damage (2–5%).
  • APH (abruption).
  • PROM or labour pains.
Fetal indications (Urgent Delivery)
  • Non-reassuring FHR tracing.
  • Persistent oligohydramnios.
  • FGR (40%).
  • Reaching 34 weeks.
WarnThe two strongest maternal triggers for delivery are HELLP (20%) and eclampsia (2%) — both make delivery non-negotiable regardless of gestational age.

36Initiation of the Process for Delivery

  • Vaginal delivery, unless obstetrically contraindicated, is the preferred method of delivery.
  • Oxytocin infusion for induction or augmentation of labor may be administered simultaneously with the magnesium sulphate infusion.
  • Total fluid intake is limited to 80–100 ml per hour (1 ml/kg/hour).
  • Continuous fetal monitoring should be performed.
  • Following delivery, the patient should be monitored under close observation for a minimum of 24 hours and magnesium sulphate should be continued for 48 hours.
PearlThe classic fluid-restriction rule (1 ml/kg/h) prevents iatrogenic pulmonary edema — the leading immediate cause of maternal death in severe preeclampsia is fluid overload, not BP.

37Eclampsia

Definition

Eclampsia is the development of convulsions or coma unrelated to other cerebral conditions during pregnancy or in the postpartum period in patients with signs and symptoms of preeclampsia.

Clinical features

It is that of PE plus fits (convulsions). The fit may follow stimuli of various kinds as visual, auditory or touch. Each fit occurs in 4 stages without intervals inbetween:

38
Premonitory stage

The pupils are dilated & the eyes move from side to side. Muscular twitches occur in the tongue, face & hands.

39
Tonic stage

All the voluntary muscles of the body undergo tonic contraction. The respiratory muscles are affected causing cyanosis, the swallowing muscles are affected causing accumulation of saliva in the mouth. The patient may acquire certain positions due to spasm of the muscles of the back.

40
Clonic stage

The voluntary muscles of the body undergo irregular intermittent contractions & relaxation. The tongue may be bitten. The patient may pass urine or stools involuntarily.

41
Coma stage

This stage may last for minutes, hours or even days. A new fit may start while the patient is still in coma or after recovery from coma. The body temperature rises during a fit because of increased muscular activity.

Causes

Cerebral edema and cerebral vasoconstriction leading to ischemia & cerebral hypoxia, microthrombi or retention of sodium ions causing cerebral irritability.

Differential diagnosis

  • Idiopathic: epilepsy.
  • Brain lesions: head trauma, encephalitis, abscess, tumor or aneurysm.
  • Metabolic: renal or hepatic failure, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia and hypoparathyroidism.
  • Toxic: arsenic poisoning, barbiturate withdrawal and alcohol withdrawal syndrome.

Types of eclampsia

Antepartum eclampsia

The fits start during pregnancy before the onset of labor.

Intrapartum eclampsia

The fits start for the first time during labor.

Postpartum eclampsia

The fits start after delivery, usually within the first 48 hours.

Signs of bad prognosis (Traditional or Eden's criteria)

Eclampsia is considered severe with bad prognosis if two or more of the following signs are present:

> 6 hDuration of coma
> 120Pulse rate (beats/min)
> 39 °CTemperature

> 40Respiratory rate (/min)

Complications

  • Abruptio placentae.
  • Acute renal failure.
  • Intracerebral hemorrhage.
  • Pulmonary edema.
  • Aspiration pneumonia.
  • Retinal detachment.

Treatment

The basic principles in the management of eclampsia is stabilization of general condition by the following then termination of pregnancy.

Support of cardio-respiratory functions
  • Airway patency should be assessed and established to ensure adequate maternal oxygenation.
  • Suction should be used as needed and the patient protected from injury by making sure the bed side-rails are elevated and padded.
  • Oxygen should be administered to improve maternal oxygen concentration and to increase oxygen delivery to the fetus.
Control of convulsions and prevention of recurrent ones — Magnesium sulphate therapy
  • The dose is the same like preeclampsia.
  • Patients receiving magnesium sulphate therapy must be monitored for evidence of drug toxicity by:
    • Reflexes: absence of patellar reflex (first sign of toxicity).
    • Urinary output assessment: < 30 ml/hour in case of toxicity.
    • Respiratory rate: respiratory depression in case of toxicity.
  • In cases of magnesium toxicity: if magnesium toxicity is recognized, 10 ml of 10 % calcium gluconate is administered intravenously.

Delivery remains the ultimate treatment for eclampsia.

DangerThe 3-monitor check for MgSO4 toxicity: reflexes (patellar first to go) + urine output (<30 ml/h) + respiratory rate. The antidote is 10 ml of 10% calcium gluconate IV.

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulopathy (DIC)

A consumption coagulopathy unique to its obstetric triggers — abruptio placentae, amniotic fluid embolism, severe preeclampsia, vesicular mole, and IUFD. Recognizing the lab pattern (↑PT/↑PTT/↑D-dimer, ↓platelets/↓fibrinogen) early is the difference between life and death.

44Definition of DIC

Definition

A disorder of hemostasis in which intravascular activation of both clotting and fibrinolytic systems occurs leading to consumption of coagulation factors and platelets.

DangerDIC is a consumption coagulopathy — both the clotting and the fibrinolytic cascades are activated simultaneously, so the patient bleeds and micro-thromboses at the same time. The single best description is "running out of coagulation factors while the system is still turned on."

45Clinical Picture

10Surgical / Operative

The patient will experience systemic hemorrhage and oozing during operations.

11Obstetric

Postpartum hemorrhage.

WarnThe single most distinctive bedside clue is oozing from every puncture site, suture line, and raw surface at once — a feature not seen in ordinary postpartum hemorrhage. When the surgeon reports "everything is bleeding," think DIC.

46Etiology — Obstetric Triggers

DIC in obstetrics is almost always secondary — the trigger is one of a small list of well-defined obstetric events.

1

Preeclampsia and eclampsia.

2

Amniotic fluid embolism.

3

Abruptio placenta.

4

Vesicular mole.

5

IUFD and missed abortion.

6

Incompatible blood transfusion.

7

Rupture uterus and placenta accreta.

PearlThe two highest-yield triggers to memorize are abruptio placentae and amniotic fluid embolism — both release massive thromboplastin into the maternal circulation and can flip a stable patient into fulminant DIC within minutes.
PearlRetained dead fetus syndrome is the chronic form — low-grade consumptive coagulopathy over days, classically seen with IUFD retained > 4 weeks. Coagulopathy corrects within hours of delivery of the fetus.

47Investigations — Lab Pattern

The classical DIC panel is a mirror image — clotting factors up, the components that get consumed down.

TestDirection in DICMechanism
Prothrombin time (PT) ↑ Increased Consumption of extrinsic-pathway factors (V, VII, X, II / fibrinogen).
Partial thromboplastin time (PTT) ↑ Increased Consumption of intrinsic-pathway factors (VIII, IX, XI, XII).
Fibrin degradation products (D-dimer) ↑ Increased Fibrinolysis of cross-linked fibrin — the most sensitive single marker.
Platelet count ↓ Decreased Consumed in micro-thrombi and by splenic/hepatic sequestration.
Fibrinogen ↓ Decreased Converted to fibrin faster than the liver can replace it.
PearlThe single most useful test to order first is the D-dimer — a normal D-dimer effectively rules out DIC in pregnancy (it has very high negative predictive value). A fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL in pregnancy is highly suggestive of DIC, because normal pregnancy fibrinogen is elevated (400–600 mg/dL).

48Treatment

Three pillars — and the order matters: stop the trigger first, then replace what's been consumed.

12Treatment of the cause

Remove the obstetric trigger (deliver the fetus in abruptio, evacuate the mole, deliver in IUFD, etc.).

13Replacement transfusion

Transfusion of fresh blood, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate and platelets.

14Anticoagulant

Used selectively — typically low-dose heparin in chronic consumptive states (e.g., retained IUFD, vesicular mole) to slow further consumption. Avoid in active hemorrhage.

DangerTreatment of the cause comes first — no amount of plasma or platelets will stop DIC if the trigger (e.g., retained dead fetus, undiagnosed abruption, molar tissue) is still in place. Delivery of the fetus in abruptio placentae is itself the cure.
WarnAnticoagulation in DIC is a double-edged sword — useful in chronic low-grade consumptive states, but dangerous in fulminant obstetric DIC with active bleeding. Junior clinicians often over-anticoagulate. Follow local Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP) and hematology guidance.

Diabetes Mellitus with Pregnancy

A carbohydrate-metabolism disorder in which pregnancy and diabetes interact in both directions — the disease threatens the fetus (macrosomia, anomalies, IUFD, neonatal hypoglycemia) and pregnancy modifies the disease (insulin requirements rise, microangiopathy worsens, ketoacidosis can occur at lower glucose levels).

  • To be able to describe how to screen for diabetes mellitus in pregnancy
  • To be able to explain the management of diabetic pregnant woman

51Definition & Incidence

Definition

A disorder in the carbohydrate metabolism characterized by hyperglycemia and glucosuria.

Incidence

  • 3–5 % of pregnancies are associated with gestational diabetes.
  • 0.5–1 % are complicated by pregestational diabetes.
PearlGestational diabetes is roughly 5–10× more common than pregestational diabetes, because the placenta is a strong insulin-resistant organ in the second half of pregnancy — any woman with even subtle pre-existing β-cell dysfunction will be unmasked.

52Classification

[A] Pregestational diabetes

Diabetes that exists before pregnancy.

  • Type 1 → Juvenile onset (autoimmune).
  • Type 2 → Adult onset (insulin resistance).
[B] Gestational diabetes

First diagnosed in pregnancy.

  • Definition: Carbohydrate intolerance of variable severity first diagnosed during pregnancy.
  • Classic GDM diagnosed at 24–28 weeks.
  • GDM diagnosed before 24 weeks → probably undiagnosed type 2 DM and is managed as pregestational diabetes.
NoteAny GDM diagnosed before the 24-week threshold is reclassified as pregestational because the placental insulin-resistant hormones have not yet had time to drive the hyperglycemia — so the disease must have pre-existed.

53Physiologic Changes During Pregnancy

  • Hyperplasia of the pancreatic islet cells which leads to a doubling in insulin production between the first and the third trimesters.
  • Initial increase in insulin sensitivity during first trimester.
  • Progressive glucose intolerance with advanced gestation due to release of insulin-resistant hormones (HPL, glucagon, progesterone and corticotrophin releasing hormone) from the placenta.
  • Increased glucose uptake of the fetus and increased peripheral uptake.
  • Increased glycogenesis and reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis.
  • Reduced renal tubular threshold for glucose → glucosuria.
  • Overall, fasting glucose levels fall by 10–20 %, and postprandial levels are higher.
PearlThe placenta is the main driver of pregnancy-induced insulin resistance via human placental lactogen (HPL) — HPL acts as a "counter-insulin" hormone to shunt glucose preferentially to the fetus. The clinical implication: insulin requirements rise through pregnancy and the diabetic woman's doses must be titrated upward.

54Effect of Pregnancy on Diabetes Mellitus

  • The disease may appear for the first time during pregnancy.
  • The insulin requirements gradually increase after the 3rd month until term and ketoacidosis can occur with lower levels of blood glucose.
  • Aggravation of diabetic microangiopathy (nephropathy, retinopathy & neuropathy).
  • During delivery, there is liability to hypoglycemia because of uterine activity.
  • After delivery, insulin requirement decreases due to drop in the placental hormones.
DangerDiabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in pregnancy can occur at lower blood glucose levels than in non-pregnant patients (sometimes < 200 mg/dL) because of the accelerated starvation metabolism and the buffering effect of the fetus. Any unexplained nausea, vomiting, or Kussmaul breathing in a pregnant diabetic is DKA until proven otherwise.
WarnInsulin requirements fall sharply within hours of placental delivery — resume pre-pregnancy doses or lower in the immediate postpartum to avoid hypoglycemia.

55Effects of DM on Pregnancy

Fetal Risks
  • Miscarriage.
  • Congenital anomaly (doubled) (cardiovascular & NTDs).
  • Macrosomia.
  • Prematurity.
  • Shoulder dystocia & birth injury.
  • Respiratory distress.
  • Neonatal hypoglycemia.
  • IUFD.
  • IUGR.
  • Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, hypocalcemia, and hypothermia.
  • Risk of having diabetes: 15 % in type 2 & 2 % in type 1.
Maternal Risks
  • Hypoglycemia.
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Polyhydramnios.
  • Obstructed labor.
  • Operative delivery.
  • Worsening of retinal disease.
  • Worsening of renal disease.
  • Pre-eclampsia.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage, and infection.
DangerThe single highest-yield maternal-fetal risk combination: poor first-trimester glycemic control → doubled congenital anomaly rate (cardiovascular & neural tube defects). This is the clinical rationale for preconception counseling and tight HbA1C < 6.5 % before pregnancy.
PearlMacrosomia (Pedersen hypothesis) is driven by fetal hyperinsulinemia in response to maternal hyperglycemia → anabolic growth. The same fetal hyperinsulinemia is what causes neonatal hypoglycemia in the first 24 h after delivery (insulin is still high but the maternal glucose supply is cut).

56Risk Factors for GDM

  • BMI > 30 kg/m².
  • Previous macrosomic infant ≥ 4.5 kg.
  • Previous G.D. (30–84 %).
  • First degree relative with diabetes.
  • Glycosuria 2+.
  • Unexplained IUFD.
  • Current macrosomia and polyhydramnios.
PearlThe two highest-yield risk factors for boards are prior macrosomic infant ≥ 4.5 kg and previous GDM (recurrence 30–84 %) — both indicate pre-existing β-cell dysfunction that pregnancy will unmask.

57Screening & Diagnosis — GCT & GTT

One-hour Glucose Challenge Test (GCT)

  • A 50-g one-hour glucose challenge test at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation.
  • Patients do not have to fast for this test.
  • To be considered normal, serum or plasma glucose values should be less than 140 mg/dL.
  • Positive screening test require confirmation with oral glucose tolerance test.

Three-hour Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT)

  • If the GCT ≥ 140 mg/dL, then the 3-hour diagnostic GTT should be done by administering 100 g of glucose orally after fasting for 8 hours.
  • The American Diabetes Association has recommended the use of Carpenter and Coustan values.
  • If any two or more of the diagnostic values are met or exceeded, then the diagnosis of GDM is made.

58Management of Gestational DM

Antenatal care by:

  1. Frequent antenatal care visits.
  2. Glycemic control.

GDM is divided into two categories

I. GDM-A1 — Euglycemia achieved by diet alone

Diet: Daily intake of 1,800 to 2,400 kilocalories (30 kcal/kg/day) made up of:

  • 15 % to 20 % protein
  • 50 % to 60 % carbohydrates
  • 20 % fat
  • A nutritional consultation should be provided.

Exercise: Moderate exercise has been shown to lower maternal glucose concentrations in women with GDM.

II. GDM-A2 — Glycemic status inadequately controlled by diet alone

Glucose monitoring: is carried out using a glucometer. The patient should record fasting glucose values and 1-hour (or 2-hour) postprandial glucose values with each meal to determine the adequacy of diabetic control for goal values.

Goal values for adequacy of control:

  • Fasting: 60–90 mg/dl.
  • 1 hour postprandial: < 140 mg/dl.
  • 2 hours postprandial: < 120 mg/dl.
  • Bedtime: < 120 mg/dl.
  • 2:00–6:00 A.M.: 60–90 mg/dl.

Oral hypoglycemic agents: Metformin (an oral agent) for women who decline insulin therapy.

15Assessment of fetal wellbeing

Ultrasound and nonstress test in the last trimester every 2 weeks.

PearlThe mnemonic "A1 = diet, A2 = diet + meds" captures the entire GDM classification. GDM-A1 is by far the more common and milder form.

60Management of Labor & Delivery

Timing of delivery

TimingIndication
At 38 weeks In controlled DM with no fetal or maternal complications.
Delivery before 38 w In uncontrolled DM, fetal complications (as IUGR), maternal complications (as preeclampsia).

Route of delivery

  • Induction of labor: in controlled DM (no maternal or fetal indications as macrosomia or malpresentation).
  • C.S.: uncontrolled DM, maternal or fetal complications, failed induction, macrosomia, malpresentations, presence of obstetric indication.

Glucose control and monitoring during labor

  • The patient must be euglycemic during the intrapartum period because maternal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperinsulinemia → neonatal hypoglycemia → neonatal seizures and death.
  • The infusion fluid is adjusted based on blood glucose levels. Short-acting insulin boluses may be added to bring glucose levels to the target range of 80 to 100 mg/dl. Blood glucose values should be checked every 1 to 2 hours.
  • The patient should receive her normal insulin dose during the previous evening. On the morning of her induction, the patient's usual insulin dose should be withheld.

Management of elective cesarean section

  • The patient should withhold her morning insulin dose. Glucose levels should be monitored frequently during and immediately after surgery.
DangerThe Pedersen cascade (maternal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperinsulinemia → neonatal hypoglycemia → seizures & death) is the entire rationale for tight intrapartum glucose control (80–100 mg/dL, checked every 1–2 h). Failure to achieve this is a leading cause of preventable neonatal morbidity.

61Postpartum Care, Breastfeeding & Contraception

Glucose monitoring

  • After delivery, glucose levels should be checked every 4 to 6 hours, while administering 5 % dextrose with lactated Ringer's or normal saline (at approximately 125 mL/hr).
  • During the initial postpartum period, short-acting insulin is used only when glucose levels are higher than 150 mg/dL.
  • Once the patient is taking a full diabetic diet, insulin can be started at one third to one-half of the antepartum dosage.

Breastfeeding

  • Breastfeeding increases the frequency of hypoglycemia in insulin-dependent diabetics, thus women should have a snack before or during breastfeeding.
  • Women with pre-existing type 2 diabetes can safely take metformin while breastfeeding.

Contraception

  • Progesterone only methods and mechanical methods can be used.
  • The IUCD can cause pelvic inflammatory disease and combined pills are contraindicated.

Postpartum evaluation in GDM

  • In the postpartum period, a woman with GDM should have a follow-up fasting blood glucose and 2-hour 75-g glucose tolerance test (6 to 12 weeks postpartum) to assess for persistent diabetes.
  • If the threshold values are met or exceeded in postpartum testing, the patient is given a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes.
PearlCombined oral contraceptives are contraindicated in diabetic women (estrogen + progestin increase thrombotic and vascular risk in a population already predisposed). Progesterone-only pills (POP) and barrier methods are the preferred options.

63Diagnostic Investigations

Three investigations are mentioned in the source for the work-up of bacteriuria in pregnancy. Each is appropriate to a different clinical scenario.

Mandatory
MSU culture

Culture of a midstream urine (MSU) sample is mandatory. Presence of > 100,000 organisms / mL is diagnostic of asymptomatic bacteriuria.

If pyrexia present
CBC + RFT

CBC and renal function tests (RFT) are indicated in the presence of pyrexia (rule out systemic involvement and renal impairment).

If infections are repeated
U/S of urinary tract

Ultrasound of the urinary tract is indicated in repeated infections (look for structural abnormality, calculus, obstruction, or residual urine).

67Superficial Thrombophlebitis

Common in veins of calf, thigh, inguinal region and vulva.

Clinical picture

  • Localized discomfort.
  • Erythema.
  • Localized superficial tenderness.
  • Pain.
  • Palpable lump or palpable cord over the course of the affected superficial veins.
  • Low grade fever.

Complications

  • Superficial venous thrombosis increases the risk of DVT.

Treatment

  • Bed rest.
  • Analgesic.
  • Elastic support.
  • Give heparin if DVT is confirmed.
PearlSuperficial thrombophlebitis is often considered a "benign" condition, but in pregnancy it is a marker of prothrombotic risk — confirmed DVT triggers a switch from local measures to therapeutic anticoagulation. The single most important clinical action: rule out DVT (compression duplex US) before settling on a management plan.

68Deep Venous Thrombosis (DVT)

Definition

A medical condition that occurs when a blood clot forms in a deep vein (in the lower leg, thigh, or pelvis).

Predisposing factors in pregnancy — Virchow's triad

Hypercoagulation

Increased procoagulant activity in pregnancy.

Vascular damage

Endothelial injury at delivery or surgery.

Venous stasis

Mechanical compression of the left iliac vein in pregnancy.

Prothrombotic changes in pregnancy

  • Increased procoagulants such as fibrinogen.
  • Decrease fibrinolysis like decrease protein S.
  • Mechanical factors such as compression of left iliac vein.
PearlThe mechanical compression of the left iliac vein by the right iliac artery and the gravid uterus (May–Thurner physiology) is the reason most DVTs in pregnancy are left-sided — the most common in deep veins of the left side (see §5 below).

72Management of DVT — Prophylactic & Active

A · Prophylactic Treatment
  • Early ambulation after delivery.
  • Prophylactic heparin in risky cases.
B · Active Management
  • Graduated compression stockings.
  • Start anticoagulant therapy (see §8 UFH and §9 LMWH).
PearlGraduated compression stockings are an adjunct to anticoagulation — they reduce post-thrombotic syndrome and edema but do not dissolve or prevent propagation of the clot. Anticoagulation (heparin, never warfarin in pregnancy) is the primary active treatment.

73Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)

Mode of action

  • Enhance antithrombin activity.
  • Increase factor Xa inhibitor activity.
  • Inhibit platelet aggregation.

Dose

Start with a bolus of 5,000 IU, then give IV Heparin at a dose of 15,000 IU by intravenous drip every 12 hours for 5–10 days, or until clinical improvement is noted, then give maintenance therapy by subcutaneous injection of 10,000 IU / 8–12 hours to keep a PTT 1.5–2.5 times control.

Complications

  • Hemorrhage.
  • Osteoporosis.
  • Thrombocytopenia.
  • Skin necrosis.

Antidote

1 % protamine sulphate solution 10 mg.

DangerHeparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a paradoxical, immune-mediated reaction in which heparin therapy itself triggers platelet activation and thrombosis. Platelet counts should be monitored periodically throughout UFH therapy; a > 50 % fall from baseline is a red flag and mandates immediate switch to a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g. argatroban, fondaparinux).
PearlProtamine sulphate 1 % — 10 mg neutralizes approximately 1,000 IU of UFH (1 mg of protamine per 100 IU of heparin given in the last 2 hours). It is the only clinically available UFH antidote. There is no comparable effective antidote for LMWH (only partial reversal with protamine).

74Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH) — Enoxaparin

Mode of action

  • Inhibit factor Xa activity.
  • Given SC once or twice daily.

Advantages of LMWH over UFH

  • Better bioavailability.
  • Longer plasma half-life.
  • Lower risk of hemorrhagic complications.

Dose of Enoxaparin (Calexane)

Prophylactic dose: 40 mg daily.

Therapeutic dose: 1 mg / kg body weight / 12 hours.

Treatment in relation to pregnancy should continue for 6–12 weeks after delivery.

PearlEnoxaparin is the most commonly used LMWH in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta (heparin molecules are too large to cross) and is not teratogenic. Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic, crosses placenta, causes warfarin embryopathy and fetal hemorrhage) — UFH and LMWH are the only safe options for anticoagulation in pregnancy.
NoteThe 6–12 week postpartum extension reflects the prolonged prothrombotic window of the puerperium — the maternal VTE risk remains elevated for at least 6 weeks after delivery, and remains above baseline until 12 weeks postpartum.

83 Definition & Etiology

Rh incompatibility is an immunological disorder resulting in destruction of fetal red blood cells by antibodies which pass through placenta from maternal blood.

Rh genetics

There are 5 main Rh genes (C, D, E, c, and e); the most important gene is D. Rh factor is a lipoprotein component on the cell wall of red blood cells. About 85% of the population are Rh positive and about 15% are Rh negative.

When incompatibility occurs

It occurs when an Rh-negative mother is pregnant from an Rh-positive husband. The fetus will be Rh positive or Rh negative according to whether the father is homozygous (2D) or heterozygous (Dd):

  • Homozygous father: 100% of babies will be Rh positive.
  • Heterozygous father: 50% Rh positive and 50% Rh negative.

Sensitization triggers

Sensitization of an Rh-negative mother occurs when Rh-positive red blood cells enter maternal circulation:

During pregnancy
  • Placental separation (most important).
  • Abortion.
  • Ectopic pregnancy.
  • Vesicular mole.
  • Amniocentesis.
  • External cephalic version.
  • Antepartum hemorrhage.
Before pregnancy
  • Previous blood transfusion of Rh-positive blood.
  • In utero from her Rh-positive mother (grandmother syndrome).

84 Pathogenesis & Clinical varieties

When maternal antibodies pass into the fetal circulation they cause hemolysis of fetal RBCs and jaundice.

Pathogenesis cascade

Attachment of IgG to fetal RBCs causes hemolysis leading to:

  1. Anemia → anemic heart failure and generalized edema.
  2. Extramedullary hematopoiesis → hepatosplenomegaly → portal hypertension, ascites, hypoproteinemia, generalized edema.
  3. Excess bilirubin production.

Clinical varieties

Hydrops fetalis (most severe)
IUFD due to anemic heart failure. Macrosomic baby. Severe hemolytic anemia (cord Hb < 8 gm/dL), hepatosplenomegaly, generalized edema, ascites, scalp edema, hydrothorax, large edematous placenta.
Icterus gravis neonatorum (commonest)
Occurs after delivery. Nausea, vomiting, anemia (Hb 8-12 gm/dL), jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly. Severe cases: bilirubin crosses immature blood-brain barrier → kernicterus (extrapyramidal effects and convulsions). Yellow staining of amniotic fluid, cord and vernix.
Congenital hemolytic anemia (mildest)
Mild anemia at birth (Hb < 14 gm/dL), mild pallor and transient jaundice.

85 Investigations

I. Screening

  • Assessment of maternal and paternal blood group and Rh.
  • If mother is Rh-negative and husband Rh-positive: indirect Coomb's test to detect maternal immunization.
  • Critical titer: greater than 1/16 → significant possibility of sensitization & stillbirth.

II. Diagnosis of the affected fetus

During pregnancy: amniocentesis, cordocentesis, ultrasound.

After delivery: cord blood sampling.

Fetal or neonatal blood examined for:
  1. ABO group & Rh typing
  2. Hemoglobin & Hct value
  3. Reticulocytic count
  4. Direct Coomb's test
  5. Bilirubin level
  6. Serum protein level

Ultrasound

  • Preliminary before amniocentesis or cordocentesis.
  • Signs of hydrops fetalis: scalp edema, increased amniotic fluid, placental thickening, hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Doppler on middle cerebral artery (MCA) to predict fetal anemia by measuring peak systolic velocity.

Serial amniocentesis

Indications
High maternal antibody titer to certify degree of fetal hemolysis. Past history of hydrops fetalis or exchange transfusion.
Value
Indirect assessment of fetal RBC hemolysis by measuring amniotic fluid bilirubin by spectrophotometry plotted against gestational age on the Liley chart.
Timing
  • Before 22-24 weeks if high antibody titer.
  • Otherwise between 30-32 weeks.
  • If past history of IUFD: 10 weeks earlier than expected time of that event.

86 Management — Prophylactic & pregnancy

Prophylactic management

  • No Rh-positive blood is given to Rh-negative mother.
  • Anti-D immunoglobulin should be given to Rh-negative mothers after labor or abortion.

During pregnancy

Management algorithm by titer

If titer < 1/16 (unsensitized): no intervention required. At 28 weeks, a standard dose of 300 µg of anti-D immunoglobulin is administered.

If indirect Coomb's test above critical value: do amniocentesis and put results on Liley curve:

  • Zone 1 (A): Minimal or absent fetal affection. Allowed to deliver at term.
  • Zone 2 (B): Moderate affection (low & high zone B). Pregnancy can be allowed until AF bilirubin levels increase or until pregnancy reaches 32 weeks' gestation.
  • Zone 3 (C): Severe fetal affection. Risk of IUFD within 7-10 days. Must be delivered or transfused.
Modern update: MCA Doppler as a noninvasive test has eliminated the need for amniocentesis in many centers. Done every 1-2 weeks to detect anemia; testing may begin as early as 16 to 18 weeks. MCA peak systolic velocity is increased in severe anemia due to decreased blood viscosity.

Maternal plasmapheresis

To wash antibodies from maternal circulation. Indicated for severe cases before 24 weeks of pregnancy when intrauterine transfusion cannot be done.

87 Intrauterine fetal transfusion

Timing

24-32 weeks gestation.

Indications

  • Severe fetal affection: low hematocrit value, high zone 2 or zone 3 on Liley chart.
  • 10 weeks earlier than previous IUFD.

Type of blood

Group O, Rh-negative blood.

Procedure

Intraperitoneal
Needle passed into fetal peritoneal cavity guided by ultrasound.
Intravascular
Into the umbilical vein.

Amount of transfused blood: (Gestational age in weeks - 20) × 10 (i.e. 10 mL for each week after 20 weeks).

Complications

Fetal injuries, bradycardia, cord hematoma, fetal death, chorioamnionitis.

88 Obstetric & postpartum management

Route of delivery

  • Vaginal delivery preferred.
  • Caesarean section reserved for obstetric indication and compromised fetus.

Cord management at delivery

  • Cord immediately clamped to avoid further passage of antibodies from the placenta.
  • Divided 2-3 inches from the umbilicus to facilitate exchange transfusion.
  • Manual removal of the placenta should be avoided.

Postpartum screening & anti-D

Neonate Rh D-negative
Anti-D immunoglobulin is not necessary.
Neonate Rh D-positive + mother antibody negative
Standard dose of anti-D immunoglobulin is given in the 1st 72 hours.
Neonate Rh D-positive + mother antibody positive
No anti-D immunoglobulin. Mother's next pregnancy managed as Rh sensitized.
Both screened
Patient and infant are screened after delivery.

89 Neonate — Exchange transfusion & phototherapy

Immediate neonatal care

  • Immediate cord clamping.
  • Admission to neonatal ICU.
  • Cord blood sample for blood group, Rh, hemoglobin, hematocrit, serum bilirubin, direct Coomb's test.

Exchange transfusion — Indications

  1. Cord blood bilirubin > 5 mg%.
  2. Cord hemoglobin < 10 gm.
  3. Rapidly rising bilirubin in the first 24 hours.
  4. Clinical jaundice in the first 12 hours.
  5. Past history of severely affected baby.

Aims

  1. Correction of neonatal anemia.
  2. Removal of excess bilirubin.

Phototherapy & hydration

Used as adjuvant for exchange transfusion in cases of high but not toxic bilirubin.

Mechanism: Blue or blue-green light converts unconjugated bilirubin into non-toxic, water-soluble isomers which are excreted in bile and urine without the need for hepatic conjugation.

Phenobarbitone

Enzyme inducer that increases activity of glucuronyl transferase enzyme, leading to increased bilirubin conjugation and excretion.

90 ABO incompatibility

Differs from Rh incompatibility: Can affect the first baby because most group O women have Anti-A and Anti-B. Most are IgM type so cannot cross the placenta. No need for amniocentesis or preterm delivery. Mild condition that rarely causes significant anemia. Treated by phototherapy.

Comparison: ABO vs Rh incompatibility

Feature ABO incompatibility Rh incompatibility
First baby affected Yes (natural Anti-A / Anti-B) No (requires sensitization)
Antibody type crossing placenta Mostly IgM (cannot cross) IgG (crosses freely)
Amniocentesis / preterm delivery Not required Often required
Severity Mild; rare significant anemia Can be severe / fatal
Treatment Phototherapy Exchange transfusion ± IUT
Coomb's test Negative Positive

91 Causes of neonatal jaundice & Hydrops fetalis

Causes of neonatal jaundice

  1. Physiological jaundice.
  2. Rh incompatibility.
  3. ABO incompatibility.
  4. Congenital biliary atresia.
  5. Breast milk jaundice.
  6. Infections: hepatitis, STORCH.
  7. Congenital spherocytosis.
  8. Infant of diabetic mother.
  9. Drug induced.

Hydrops fetalis

Accumulation of fluid in the interstitial tissue (skin) and two or more body cavities (pleural, pericardial effusions or ascites).

Types

Immune hydrops fetalis
Due to Rh and ABO isoimmunization.
Non-immune hydrops fetalis — causes
Genetic: Aneuploidy syndromes.
Cardiovascular: Structural AVSD, hypoplastic Rt, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia.
Pulmonary: pulmonary lymphangiectasia.
Infections: CMV, Toxoplasma.
Renal/ GI: Congenital nephrosis, gut duplication, malrotation.
Hematological: Alpha thalassemia, aplastic anemia.
Placental: AV fistula, chorioangiomas.
Others: twin-to-twin transfusion, liver cirrhosis, true knots of the umbilical cord.
