1. Physiology of Reproduction

Female Reproductive System Anatomy — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

1. Fertilization

  • Ovum is surrounded by Zona pellucida.
  • Sperm undergoes capacitation, head swells, ↑ hyaluronidase enzymes.
  • Fertilization occurs at the ampulla of the fallopian tube → zygote.
  • Zygote rapidly divides → morula (round mass of cells).
    • The morula reaches the uterine cavity 3 days after fertilization.
    • Movement is aided by the cilia motility.
  • Fluid will accumulate in morula → blastocyst.
    • It remains free for 3 days in the uterine cavity.
    • It is nourished by secretions from the endometrium.
  • The blastocyst will be divided into 2 masses:
    • Inner cell mass → embryonic mass → will form the embryo.
    • Outer cell mass → trophoblast → responsible for nutrition.

2. Implantation

  • This occurs on 6th – 7th days after ovulation by penetration of the trophoblast into the secretory endometrium (decidua basalis).
  • Functions of the decidua:
    • Site of implantation & nutrition of the blastocyst.
    • Shares in formation of the placenta.
    • Protective layer against the invasive power of trophoblast.

3. Formation of the Villi

  • The trophoblast is differentiated into 2 layers:
    • Cytotrophoblast (inner).
    • Syncytiotrophoblast (outer).
  • Trophoblast forms finger-like projections into decidua → 1° villus.
  • Mesoderm develops in the 1° villus (CT core) → 2° villus.
  • Vascularization of 2° villus with fetal vessels → 3° villus.
  • There are two types of villi:
    • Anchoring villi: fixation & attachment.
    • Free villi: food & nutrition (highly vascular). Functional unit (HCG → maintain CL for 10 weeks).
💎 Clinical Pearl
The cytotrophoblast is the inner cellular layer that retains mitotic activity. The syncytiotrophoblast is the outer multinucleated layer formed by fusion of cytotrophoblast cells. It is the syncytiotrophoblast that secretes HCG, HPL, estrogen, and progesterone.

4. Formation of Choriodecidual Spaces

  • The trophoblast invades the mouth of endometrial vessels → delivers maternal blood into the chorio-decidual space → 1° wave of invasion.
  • At 20 weeks, the trophoblast invades & destroys the media of spiral vessels which convert into wide-mouthed sinuses → 2° wave of invasion.
⚠️ Warning
Failure of the 2° wave of trophoblastic invasion of spiral arteries is a key mechanism in pre-eclampsia and IUGR (intrauterine growth restriction).

5. Placenta

Structure

  • Shape: discoid, 500 g, 20–25 cm in diameter.
  • Thickness: 2 cm in center, gradually thins towards periphery.
  • Cord insertion: eccentric.
  • 2 Surfaces: Fetal surface is smooth & covered by membranes. Maternal surface is divided into 12–15 cotyledons.
  • 2 Parts: Fetal part → Chorion frondosum. Maternal part → Decidua basalis.

Decidual Subdivisions

  • The rest of chorion which is not sharing in placental formation → Chorion laeve.
  • The rest of the decidua overlying the developing ovum → Decidua capsularis.
  • The decidua covering the rest of uterine cavity → Decidua parietalis (vera).

Functions of Placenta

Gas transport, Nutrition, and Excretion:

  • Water & electrolytes by simple diffusion.
  • Glucose, amino acids by facilitated diffusion.
  • Ca, Fe, minerals by active transport.
  • Immunoglobulins & LDL by pinocytosis.

Hormones: HCG, HPL, estrogen and progesterone.

Placental Barrier: Trophoblast, villous stroma, and fetal blood-vessel endothelium.

Abnormalities of Placenta

  1. Bipartite: two parts united by vessels & membranes.
  2. Bilobate: two lobes connected by placental tissue.
  3. Placenta succenturiata (accessory lobe).
  4. Placenta circumvallate: a whitish ring composed of decidua.
  5. Placenta fenestrate: an opening in the placenta.

6. Umbilical Cord

  • Shape: 50 cm long & 2 cm in diameter.
  • Contents: 2 arteries & 1 vein in Wharton jelly.
💎 Mnemonic — AVA
Arteries (×2, carry deoxygenated blood Away from fetus) and Vein (×1, brings oxygenated blood Via to fetus).

Abnormalities

  • Very long → coiling around fetus, cord prolapse.
  • Short cord → malpresentation & non-engagement.
  • Abnormal attachment: Central, marginal (battledore), velamentous (vasa previa).
  • True and false knots (false knot = localized collection of Wharton's jelly).
  • Absence of one umbilical artery → CFMF, IUGR.
⚠️ Warning
Single umbilical artery (SUA) is associated with congenital fetal malformations and IUGR — warrants detailed ultrasound assessment.

7. Amniotic Fluid

Source

  • Maternal: transudation through placenta & cord (mainly 1st half).
  • Fetal: urine (mainly in the 2nd half of pregnancy).

8. Hormones

HCG (Human Chorionic Gonadotrophin)

  • Source: syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Can be detected one week before the missed period.
  • α subunit — similar to FSH, LH and TSH. β subunit — specific to hCG.
FunctionUse
Maintenance of corpus luteum (luteotropic).Diagnosis of pregnancy.
Immunological suppressive action.Diagnoses of pregnancy abnormalities (↑ in Down syndrome).
Stimulates fetal testosterone secretion.Diagnoses and follow-up of vesicular mole.

HPL (Human Placental Lactogen)

  • Source: syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Very similar to GH & prolactin → may stimulate growth of breasts.
  • Anti-insulin effect → spares glucose & fatty acids for the fetus.

Estrogen

Estrogens are formed by the conversion of DHEAS from maternal and fetal sources (adrenal gland mainly) to estradiol (E2) and estriol (E3). Responsible with progesterone for most maternal changes of pregnancy.

Progesterone

Synthetized by syncytiotrophoblast from maternal cholesterol. Provides a precursor for the fetal adrenal to produce glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids.

2. Maternal Adaptation to Pregnancy

Maternal Physiological Changes During Pregnancy — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

1. Genital Changes

Whole Uterus

  • Position: Dextrorotation.
  • Size: 2.5 × 5 × 7.5 cm (non-pregnant) → 20 × 25 × 35 cm at term.
  • Shape: Pyriform (non-pregnant) → Globular at 8 weeks → Pyriform at 16 weeks till term.
  • Weight: 50 g (non-pregnant) → 1000 g at term.
  • Volume: 10 mL (non-pregnant) → 5000 mL at term.
  • Consistency: softer due to increased vascularity and amniotic fluid.
  • Contractility: Braxton Hicks contractions.

Myometrial Changes

  • Hypertrophy: estrogenic effect.
  • Hyperplasia: progesterone effect.
  • Isthmus: area of the cervix between anatomical internal os above and histological internal os below. It is 4–5 mm and lined by atrophic endometrium.
💎 Significance — Lower Uterine Segment
The isthmus and lower ½ inch of the body of the uterus develop to form the lower uterine segment, which starts to be formed at 12 weeks and gradually increases to 10 cm at full term.

Uterine Vasculature

Uterine artery lumen is doubled and uterine blood flow increases progressively, reaching about 500 mL/min at term.

Cervix, Vagina, Vulva, Fallopian Tube, Ovary

  • Cervix: Hypertrophy, soft and bluish due to increased vascularity.
  • Vagina: Violet and edematous due to increased vascularity.
  • Vulva: Violet and edematous due to increased vascularity.
  • Fallopian Tube: Elongated, tortuous.
  • Ovary: Increased size due to increased vascularity, particularly the ovary that contains the corpus luteum.

2. Hematological Changes

Blood Volume

Increases gradually above the non-pregnant level by 30%–40%, reaching its maximum by 32 weeks.

  • Plasma volume: 45% increase.
  • Red cell mass: 30% increase.

The increase in plasma volume is more than the increase in red blood cell mass, resulting in hemodilution (physiological anemia).

Blood Indices

  • RBCs and hemoglobin concentrations decrease due to hemodilution.
  • WBCs increase from 7,000 /mm³ to 10,500 /mm³, and up to 16,000 /mm³ during labor.

Coagulation Factors

  • Platelets: increase or decrease (controversial).
  • Fibrinogen: increases nearly doubled (200–400 → 400–600 mg%).
  • Factor VIII: tripled.
  • Factor VII and X: doubled.
  • Fibrinolytic activity: decreased.
⚠️ Warning — Hypercoagulable State
Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable state — increased clotting factors + decreased fibrinolysis → ↑ VTE risk.

3. Cardiovascular System

  • Heart position: uterus elevates the diaphragm → apex beat displaced upwards and to the left.
  • Pulse rate: increases by 10 beats/min.
  • Heart sounds: splitting of the 1st heart sound and appearance of 3rd heart sound.
  • Cardiac Output: increases to reach its maximum of 40% above non-pregnant level at 20 weeks, then remains constant till term (6.5 L/min).
  • During labor: COP increases more, especially during the second stage.
  • Blood Pressure: Although COP is increased, BP is decreased in the second trimester due to decreased peripheral resistance.

4. Respiratory System

  • The enlarged uterus displaces the diaphragm up to 4 cm → reduced diaphragmatic mobility and widened subcostal angle.
  • Tidal volume is increased throughout pregnancy by about 40%.
  • Residual volume is reduced.
  • Hyperventilation due to increased sensitivity of the respiratory center to excess progesterone.

5. Urinary System

  • Kidney: Renal blood flow and GFR increase by 50% → decreased creatinine, decreased urea, renal glucosuria.
  • Ureters: Dilated due to progesterone and pressure against the pelvic brim, especially on the right side.
  • Bladder: Frequency is common in early pregnancy due to pressure, congestion, and increased GFR.

6. Gastrointestinal Tract

  • Gingivitis: due to increased vascularity.
  • Ptyalism: increased salivation.
  • Appetite changes (longing): desire for sweet, sour and salt foods.
  • Nausea and vomiting (emesis gravidarum): starts between 4th and 6th weeks, improves by about 12th week.
  • Heart burn: reflux of acidic gastric contents due to progesterone on esophageal sphincter.
  • Indigestion and flatulence: reduced gastric motility.
  • Constipation: reduced large-intestinal motility from progesterone.
  • Piles: laxity of the wall of the veins by progesterone.
  • Appendix: displaced upward and laterally.
  • Gall bladder: more tendency for stones due to delayed emptying.
  • Liver: decreased albumin and increased globulin (↓ A/G ratio) and increased alkaline phosphatase.
💎 Clinical Pearl — Progesterone Is the Common Denominator
Many of the GI/urinary smooth-muscle changes of pregnancy (heartburn, constipation, piles, ureteric dilatation, biliary stasis) are driven by the same mechanism: progesterone-induced smooth-muscle relaxation.

7. Metabolic Changes

  • Weight gain: increased by 10–12 kg.
  • Water metabolism: tendency to water and sodium retention due to increased aldosterone.
  • Protein metabolism: positive nitrogen balance and increased daily requirements.
  • Carbohydrate: mild fasting hypoglycemia and postprandial hyperglycemia. Pregnancy is diabetogenic.
  • Lipid: increased levels of total lipids and cholesterol.

8–11. Other Changes

  • Musculoskeletal: Progressive softening of pelvic joints, progressive lordosis and foot flattening.
  • Endocrine: Anterior pituitary hypertrophy, thyroid enlargement (↑ TBG, ↑ T3/T4, ↓ TSH, ↑ BMR), parathyroid hypertrophy, adrenal hypertrophy.
  • Skin: Palmar erythema, chloasma gravidarum, linea nigra, stria gravidarum.
  • Breast Changes: 1st month — ↑ size/vascularity, mastodynia. 2nd month — ↑ pigmentation, Montgomery tubercles. 3rd month — colostrum. 5th month — secondary areola.

3. Diagnosis of Pregnancy

Pregnancy Test — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

1. Diagnosis of Early Pregnancy (1st Trimester)

Symptoms

  • Secondary amenorrhea.
  • Frequency of micturition: pressure by the enlarged pregnant uterus on the urinary bladder, and pelvic congestion.
  • Nausea and vomiting: usually appears at 6 weeks and usually disappears at 12 weeks.
  • Breast symptoms: enlargement, mastodynia and fullness.

Signs

  • Skin: discussed before.
  • Breast: discussed before.
  • Vaginal and cervical bluish discoloration due to increased vascularity.
  • Uterine signs: Size increased, Shape Globular, Consistency Soft.
  • Hegar sign: difference in consistency between cervix and body of uterus (body is softer).
  • Palmar sign: painless contractions followed by relaxation, not accompanied by cervical dilatation.

Investigations

  • Pregnancy test: detection of hCG in urine or serum. Serum hCG is sensitive at 25 IU/mL and becomes positive one week before the missed period.
  • Ultrasonography: Ring (4 weeks), Sac (5 weeks), Cardiac activity (6 weeks), FHS (10 weeks).

2. Diagnosis of 2nd Trimester (12–28 weeks)

Symptoms

  • Amenorrhea.
  • Morning sickness and frequency of micturition decrease.
  • Abdominal enlargement.
  • Quickening: Primigravida: 18–20 weeks. Multigravida: 16–18 weeks.

Signs

  • Uterus: Felt abdominally.
  • Braxton Hicks contractions: intermittent painless contractions.
  • Ballottement (solid body moving in fluid medium) — External: positive after 20 weeks. Internal: positive after 16 weeks.
  • Uterine soufflé: soft blowing murmur due to blood flow in uterine arteries, synchronous with maternal pulse.
  • Auscultation of FHS and umbilical soufflé (sharp whistling murmur due to blood flow in umbilical vessels, synchronous with FHS) — a sure sign of pregnancy.
💎 Clinical Pearl — Synchrony Distinguishes the Two Soufflés
Uterine soufflé is synchronous with the maternal pulse.
Umbilical soufflé is synchronous with the FHS and is itself a sure sign of pregnancy.

3. Diagnosis of 3rd Trimester (28–40 weeks)

  • The same as in the 2nd trimester, in addition to the symptoms of engagement like increased frequency of micturition and lightening.
  • Shelfing.
  • Ultrasonography is diagnostic.

Sure Signs of Pregnancy

  1. Palpation of fetal parts.
  2. Palpation of fetal movement.
  3. Auscultation of FHS.
  4. Auscultation of umbilical soufflé.
  5. Detection of fetal parts by ultrasonography.
⚠️ Warning — Probable vs. Sure Signs
Many 1st-trimester findings (amenorrhea, nausea, breast changes, Hegar's sign, positive hCG) are probable signs only. A diagnosis of pregnancy becomes definitive only when one of the sure signs is documented.

4. Antenatal Care

Antenatal Ultrasound at 12 Weeks — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

1. Definition & Objectives

Preventive obstetric program including a group of observations, medical care, investigations and advices aiming at getting pregnancy, labor and puerperium as near to normal as possible.

Objectives

  • Identification of high risk pregnancy.
  • Monitoring of high risk pregnancy.
  • Early detection of complications.
  • Giving advice for all pregnant patients.
  • Management of common minor problems.

2. Frequency of Visits

Gestational WindowLow RiskHigh Risk
0 – 28 weeksEvery monthEvery 2 weeks
28 – 36 weeksEvery 2 weeksEvery week
36 weeks – deliveryEvery weekHospitalization till delivery

3. Initial Visit

  • Diagnosis of pregnancy and accurate dating.
  • Complete clinical evaluation: history, general, abdominal, and local examination.

4. Subsequent Visits

  • Ask about any complaints.
  • BP, weight, edema, and abdominal examination.
  • Local examination (if indicated).
  • Urine for albumin, sugar, and other investigations according to the situation.

5. Screening Tests

Routine Tests

  • ABO grouping and Rh typing.
  • Urine analysis for albumin, sugar and bacterial count.
  • CBC.
  • One-hour postprandial glucose test.
  • HBsAg.
  • Rubella antibodies.
  • Cervicovaginal smear.

For Specific Populations

  • Sexually transmitted diseases (HIV, Syphilis, …etc).
  • Thalassemia (A in Asians; B in Mediterranean).
  • Sickle cell anemia (in black women).
  • Tuberculin skin test.
  • Screen for short cervix by transvaginal ultrasound.
  • Screening or diagnostic testing for trisomy 21 and other genetic disorders.
  • Screen for red blood cell antibodies and administer anti-D immune globulin to D-negative patients.

6. Antenatal Advice — Nutrition

7. Lifestyle Advice

  • Teeth care: daily cleaning by tooth paste.
  • Clothing: light clothes, avoid high-heeled shoes.
  • Bathing: avoid hot bathing and swimming pools.
  • Sexual activity: whenever there is a threat of abortion or preterm labor, coitus should be avoided. Otherwise, it is allowed with less frequency.
  • Sleep: 8 hours at night and 2 in the afternoon.
  • Travelling: avoided if there is a risk of abortion or PTL.
  • Special habits: avoid smoking, alcohol.

8. Immunization During Pregnancy

  • Live attenuated viral vaccines such as MMR & polio are contraindicated.
  • Inactivated viral vaccines such as influenza and rabies are safe.

9–10. Exposure to Infection & Irradiation

  • Infection: Avoided for fear of teratogenicity — e.g. Rubella, CMV.
  • Irradiation: Avoided whether diagnostic or therapeutic. < 5 rads: may not harm. 5–50 rads: may harm. 50–100 rads: CFMF (CNS malformation).

11. Medications

  • Not to be taken without obstetrician advice.
  • All drugs should be avoided in the first trimester.
  • Give the smallest effective dose for the smallest time.

12. Warning Symptoms of Pregnancy

  1. Any symptoms suggestive of PIH: persistent headache, blurring of vision, vomiting, and epigastric pain.
  2. Swelling of the face, fingers and limbs.
  3. Excessive watery vaginal discharge.
  4. Vaginal bleeding.
  5. Fever, dyspnea.
  6. Severe abdominal pain.
  7. Decreased fetal kicks.
⚠️ Warning — Seek Immediate Care
Any of the seven symptoms above warrants urgent review. Headaches + visual changes + epigastric pain = think PIH / pre-eclampsia. Decreased fetal kicks may signal fetal compromise.

5. Abortion

Ultrasound of Complete Miscarriage — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition & Etiology

📝 Definition
Termination of pregnancy before viability of the fetus, i.e. before 20 weeks. Incidence: 12–25%.

Etiology

  • Chromosomal abnormalities — 50% of early abortions.
  • Maternal infections — Acute fever.
  • Trauma — External or during operations.
  • Immunological causes — SLE and antiphospholipid antibodies.
  • Uterine defects — Septum, Asherman's syndrome.
  • Endocrine — Progesterone deficiency, hypothyroidism.
  • Drugs and environmental causes.
  • Maternal anoxia and malnutrition.
💎 Clinical Pearl
Chromosomal abnormalities account for ~50% of early abortions, making them the single most important etiology.

Threatened Abortion

  • Vaginal bleeding: slight or mild, bright red.
  • Pain: absent or slight.
  • Cervix: closed.
  • U/S: living fetus.

Management: Bed rest, progesterone, follow up.

Inevitable Abortion

  • Heavy vaginal bleeding with clots.
  • Suprapubic colic referred to the back.
  • Dilated cervix.

Management: Medical (oxytocin drip, prostaglandins) or surgical evacuation. Blood transfusion if needed.

Complete Abortion

  • Bleeding: mild and decreases.
  • Cessation of pain. Closed cervix.
  • Uterus: slightly larger than normal.
  • Ultrasound: shows empty cavity.

Management: Ecbolics and antibiotics and no evacuation.

Incomplete Abortion

  • Passage of a part of the conception products.
  • Bleeding is continuous. Opened cervix.
  • Uterus: less than period of amenorrhea.

Management: Evacuation (medical or surgical).

Cervical Abortion

  • Products of conception separated from the uterus but retained inside the cervical canal.
  • Bleeding with severe lower abdominal pain referred to the back.

Management: Cervical dilatation and evacuation followed by curettage.

Missed Abortion

  • Prune juice discharge (dark brown).
  • Regression of pregnancy symptoms.
  • Uterus fails to grow and becomes firmer. Cervix closed.
  • Pregnancy test becomes negative within two weeks from ovum death.
  • Ultrasound: collapsed gestational sac, absent fetal heart pulsations.

Treatment: Evacuation. <12 weeks: surgical. >12 weeks: medical (prostaglandins/oxytocin).

Septic Abortion

Any type of abortion complicated by infection.

Microbiology

E. coli, Bacteroids, Anaerobic streptococci, Clostridia, Streptococci, Staphylococci.

Clinical Picture

  • General: Pyrexia, rigors, tachycardia, malaise, sweating, headache, joint pain, jaundice.
  • Abdominal: Tenderness, rigidity and distension.
  • Local: Offensive vaginal discharge, tender uterus.

Treatment

  • Resuscitation.
  • Prompt IV broad-spectrum antibiotics followed by uterine evacuation.
  • Semi-sitting position.
  • Oxytocin infusion.
  • Hysterectomy may be the last choice to save life.

Recurrent (Habitual) Abortion

Three or more consecutive spontaneous abortions.

Causes

  • Genetic: Paternal chromosomal rearrangements, embryonic abnormalities.
  • Anatomical: Septate uterus / cervical incompetence.
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome.
  • Endocrinological: DM, thyroid abnormalities.
  • Thrombophilias.
  • Infective: STORCH.
💎 STORCH Mnemonic
Syphilis · Toxoplasmosis · Other (varicella, parvovirus B19) · Rubella · Cytomegalovirus · Herpes / HIV.

6. Ectopic Pregnancy

Ectopic Pregnancy — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition, Incidence & Sites

📝 Definition
Implantation of the fertilized ovum outside the uterine cavity. Incidence: 2% of all pregnancies.

Sites of Ectopic Pregnancy

  • Tubal pregnancy — the commonest site (99%).
  • Cervical, Rudimentary horn, Corneal (cornual), Ovarian, Abdominal, Broad ligamentary.
💎 Clinical Pearl
~99% of ectopic pregnancies are tubal — always think tubal first.

Risk Factors

  • PID — Pelvic Inflammatory Disease.
  • Previous ectopic pregnancy.
  • Previous tubal surgery.
  • Previous pelvic or abdominal surgery.
  • Tubal pathology.
  • IUCD.
  • Tubal sterilization.
  • Cigarette smoking.

Tubal Implantation Sites

  • Ampulla — 80% of tubal pregnancy.
  • Isthmus — 12% of tubal pregnancy.
  • Interstitial part, Fimbrial end.

Clinical Picture

General Symptoms

  • History of missed period.
  • Vaginal bleeding.
  • Abdominal or pelvic pain.

General Signs

  • Abdominal or pelvic tenderness. Abdominal rigidity.
  • Soft enlarged uterus. Cervical motion tenderness (jumping sign).

Three Clinical Patterns

7. Gestational Trophoblastic Disease

Hydatidiform Mole (Gestational Trophoblastic Disease) — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition & Classification

📝 Definition
Tumors arising from the trophoblastic cells of the placenta. GTD is a spectrum of pregnancy-related tumors from benign hydatidiform mole to malignant choriocarcinoma.

Classifications

A. Benign — Hydatidiform Mole
  • Complete hydatidiform mole
  • Partial hydatidiform mole
B. Malignant — GTT
  • Invasive mole
  • Choriocarcinoma
  • Placental site trophoblastic tumor (PSTT)

Incidence

1/1000
Complete Hydatidiform Mole
3/1000
Partial Hydatidiform Mole

Types — Complete & Partial Mole

(i) Complete Hydatidiform Mole
  • The uterus is full of vesicles. No embryo is present.
  • Fertilization of an anucleated ovum with a normal sperm → 46 chromosomes of paternal origin only.
(ii) Partial Hydatidiform Mole
  • A part of trophoblastic tissue only shows molar changes.
  • A fetus or parts of it is present.
  • Fertilization of an ovum by 2 sperms → 69 chromosomes.
💎 Pearl
Complete mole = androgenetic (only paternal DNA, no embryo). Partial mole = triploid (paternal + maternal DNA, embryo present).

Risk Factors

  1. Age: pregnancies below 16 years and above 45 years have highest risk.
  2. Previous GTN: One mole → <2% risk. Two moles → 16%. Three moles → 50%.
  3. Family history.
  4. Dietary: low animal fat may increase risk.
  5. Ethnic groups: more common in Asian populations.

Pathology

  • Gross: thin walled, translucent, grape-like vesicles of different sizes.
  • Microscopic: Trophoblastic proliferation affecting both syncytial and cytotrophoblastic layers → excessive secretion of hCG, chorionic thyrotrophin, progesterone.
  • Ovarian effect: High hCG → multiple theca lutein cysts + exaggerated pregnancy symptoms.

Clinical Features

  • Amenorrhea. Vaginal bleeding.
  • Symptoms of pregnancy in exaggerated form.
  • Early onset pre-eclampsia before 20th weeks.
  • Hyperemesis gravidarum.
  • Hyperthyroidism.
  • Complications of theca lutein cyst of ovary (rupture, torsion).
🚨 Danger
Pre-eclampsia before 20 weeks is a red-flag finding. In a normal pregnancy, pre-eclampsia essentially never occurs this early — its presence should always prompt evaluation for molar pregnancy.

Investigations

Ultrasound

  • Snow storm appearance — uterine cavity filled with multiple sonolucent areas.
  • Absence of fetus.

8. Hypertension with Pregnancy

Preeclampsia — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition & Incidence

📝 Definition
Systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on two separate occasions at least 6 hours apart during pregnancy. Incidence: 10–20%.
🚨 Danger
It is the second leading cause of maternal death and perinatal morbidity and mortality after severe bleeding.

Classification

1. Chronic (pre-existing) hypertension

Diagnosed before the 20th week of gestation.

2. Gestational hypertension

New hypertension after 20 weeks without proteinuria.

3. Preeclampsia

Hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria / end-organ involvement.

4. Chronic HTN + superimposed preeclampsia

Worsening BP + proteinuria after 20 weeks in a chronic hypertensive.

💎 Pearl
The 20-week threshold is the pivot: HTN before 20 weeks = chronic; after 20 weeks with proteinuria = preeclampsia.

Chronic Hypertension

Mild chronic hypertensionSevere chronic hypertension
Serial antenatal screening.Start antihypertensive medications (alpha methyldopa, labetalol or calcium channel blockers) to maintain BP < 160/100 mmHg.
Serial blood pressure measurement.Serial BP measurement and urine protein assessment.
Serial urine protein assessment.Delivery at 37 wks.
Termination of pregnancy at term.If delivery before 34 wks, steroids for fetal lung maturity.

Gestational Hypertension

  • Pregnancy induced hypertension develops after 20 weeks with absence of proteinuria.
  • Conservative outpatient management. Serial laboratory testing.
  • Antihypertensive treatment only if BP > 160/100 mmHg.
  • At term by vaginal delivery or cesarean section.
⚠️ Warning
Up to 25–50% of gestational hypertension eventually progresses to preeclampsia.

Preeclampsia

📝 Definition
Hypertension ≥ 140/90 mmHg recorded on two separate occasions at least 4 hours apart and in the presence of at least 300 mg protein in 24h urine after 20th week in a previously normotensive woman. Incidence: 5–10%.

Risk Factors

  • More in primigravida.
  • Conditions in which the placenta is enlarged (DM, multiple pregnancies, hydrops).
  • Pre-existing hypertension or renal diseases.
  • Pre-existing vascular disease. Obesity.

Etiology

Abnormal placentation, abnormal trophoblastic invasion, immunologic phenomena, renin-angiotensin system, coagulation abnormalities, abnormal cardiovascular adaptation, dietary and genetic factors.

Pathology of Preeclampsia

Vasospasm and hemoconcentration are the main pathology:

  • CNS: ischemia, hemorrhages and oedema.
  • Liver: subcapsular hemorrhage, periportal necrosis.
  • Kidney: decreased renal blood flow, glomerular endotheliosis → decrease GFR by 50%, proteinuria.
  • Heart and lungs: myocardial and endocardial hemorrhage and necrosis.
  • Retina: hemorrhage, exudate and rarely retinal detachment.
  • Coagulation: ↑ fibrin production, ↓ fibrinolytic activity, thrombocytopenia.

Diagnostic Criteria

Signs

  1. Hypertension: BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg after 20 weeks in previously normotensive.
  2. Proteinuria: urinary protein > 0.3 gm/L in 24h or PCR ≥ 0.3.

Symptoms (RED FLAGS)

  • Headache and blurred vision.
  • Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain.
  • Nausea and vomiting.

Investigations

  1. Complete urine examination for proteinuria.
  2. Kidney function. Liver function tests.
  3. Coagulation status: Platelet count, fibrinogen and FDP.
  4. Fundus examination.
  5. Fetal assessment: kick counts, NST twice weekly, BPP, AFI weekly, U/S growth every 3 weeks.

Types of Preeclampsia

  • Mild: BP < 160/110 mmHg, no symptoms, normal lab data.
  • With severe features: BP ≥ 160/110 mmHg with:
    • ↑ Liver enzymes, hepatic hemorrhage & rupture.
    • ↑ Creatinine, acute tubular necrosis.
    • HELLP syndrome.
    • IUGR, oligohydramnios, IUFD, placental abruption.
    • Headache, blurred vision, epigastric pain.
    • Oliguria (<500 ml/24h), pulmonary edema, thrombocytopenia (<100,000/µL).
💎 Pearl
In 2013, ACOG replaces "severe PE" with "preeclampsia with severe features".

Complications

Maternal Effects
  • Accidental hemorrhage (abruptio placentae).
  • Acute renal failure.
  • HELLP syndrome.
  • Acute pulmonary edema & heart failure.
  • Eclampsia. DIC.
  • Cerebrovascular accidents.
  • Residual and recurrent hypertension.
Fetal Effects
  • IUGR: due to placental insufficiency caused by vasospasm.
  • IUFD.

Treatment

Prophylactic

  1. Proper antenatal care.
  2. Low dose aspirin. Calcium. Antioxidants.

Curative

Delivery remains the ultimate treatment. Vaginal delivery is preferable. Regional anesthesia is preferred.

General Measures

Hospitalization for observation of maternal and fetal conditions.

Medical Treatment

  • Antihypertensive drugs: alpha-methyl-dopa (Aldomet), calcium channel blockers (Nifedipine), Labetalol and Hydralazine.
  • Diuretics: in cases of heart failure and pulmonary edema.

Obstetric Management

  • Magnesium sulphate to prevent convulsions (4–6 g loading dose followed by 1–2 gm/hour).
  • Control of maternal blood pressure within a safe range.
  • Induction of labor to initiate delivery.
🚨 Danger
MgSO₄ is the only drug that prevents eclampsia — antihypertensives control BP; they do not prevent fits.

Timing of Delivery

Therapeutic target is NOT "normal BP" — it is diastolic 90–100 / systolic 140–150. Drop below that and uteroplacental perfusion falls.

Eclampsia

📝 Definition
Development of convulsions or coma unrelated to other cerebral conditions during pregnancy or in the postpartum period in patients with signs and symptoms of preeclampsia.

Four Stages of Each Fit

  1. Premonitory stage: Pupils dilated, eyes move from side to side. Muscular twitches in tongue, face & hands.
  2. Tonic stage: All voluntary muscles undergo tonic contraction. Respiratory muscles affected → cyanosis. Swallowing muscles affected → saliva accumulation.
  3. Clonic stage: Irregular intermittent contractions & relaxation. Tongue may be bitten. Involuntary urination/defecation.
  4. Coma stage: May last minutes, hours or days. Body temperature rises during fit.

Types of Eclampsia

  • Antepartum: Fits start during pregnancy before labor.
  • Intrapartum: Fits start during labor.
  • Postpartum: Fits start after delivery, usually within first 48 hours.

Eden's Bad Prognosis Criteria

Eclampsia is considered severe with bad prognosis if two or more of the following signs are present:

  • > 10 number of fits
  • > 6 h duration of coma
  • > 120 pulse rate (beats/min)
  • > 39 °C temperature
  • > 200 systolic BP (mmHg)
  • > 40 respiratory rate (/min)

Complications of Eclampsia

  • Abruptio placentae. Acute renal failure.
  • Intracerebral hemorrhage. Pulmonary edema.
  • Aspiration pneumonia. Retinal detachment.

Treatment of Eclampsia

Basic principles: stabilization of general condition then termination of pregnancy.

  • Airway patency assessed and established.
  • Suction as needed. Patient protected from injury (bed side-rails elevated and padded).
  • Oxygen administered to improve maternal oxygen concentration.
  • Magnesium sulphate: same dose as preeclampsia.
  • Monitoring for drug toxicity:
    • Reflexes: absence of patellar reflex (first sign of toxicity).
    • Urinary output: < 30 ml/hour.
    • Respiratory rate: respiratory depression.
  • If toxicity: 10 ml of 10% calcium gluconate IV.
🚨 Danger — MgSO₄ Toxicity
The 3-monitor check: reflexes (patellar first to go) + urine output (<30 ml/h) + respiratory rate. Antidote: 10 ml of 10% calcium gluconate IV.

9. DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulopathy)

DIC with Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

📝 Definition
A disorder of hemostasis in which intravascular activation of both clotting and fibrinolytic systems occurs leading to consumption of coagulation factors and platelets.
🚨 Danger
DIC is a consumption coagulopathy — both the clotting and the fibrinolytic cascades are activated simultaneously, so the patient bleeds and micro-thromboses at the same time.

Clinical Picture

  • Surgical/Operative: Systemic hemorrhage and oozing during operations.
  • Obstetric: Postpartum hemorrhage.
⚠️ Warning
The single most distinctive bedside clue is oozing from every puncture site, suture line, and raw surface at once. When the surgeon reports "everything is bleeding," think DIC.

Etiology — Obstetric Triggers

  1. Preeclampsia and eclampsia.
  2. Amniotic fluid embolism.
  3. Abruptio placenta.
  4. Vesicular mole.
  5. IUFD and missed abortion.
  6. Incompatible blood transfusion.
  7. Rupture uterus and placenta accreta.
💎 Pearl
The two highest-yield triggers: abruptio placentae and amniotic fluid embolism — both release massive thromboplastin into the maternal circulation.
💎 Pearl — Retained Dead Fetus Syndrome
The chronic form — low-grade consumptive coagulopathy over days, classically seen with IUFD retained > 4 weeks. Coagulopathy corrects within hours of delivery.

Investigations — Lab Pattern

TestDirection in DICMechanism
Prothrombin time (PT)↑ IncreasedConsumption of extrinsic-pathway factors.
Partial thromboplastin time (PTT)↑ IncreasedConsumption of intrinsic-pathway factors.
D-dimer↑ IncreasedFibrinolysis of cross-linked fibrin — the most sensitive single marker.
Platelet count↓ DecreasedConsumed in micro-thrombi.
Fibrinogen↓ DecreasedConverted to fibrin faster than the liver can replace it.
💎 Pearl
The single most useful test to order first is the D-dimer. A fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL in pregnancy is highly suggestive of DIC, because normal pregnancy fibrinogen is elevated (400–600 mg/dL).

Treatment — Three Pillars

  1. Treatment of the cause: Remove the obstetric trigger (deliver the fetus in abruptio, evacuate the mole, etc.).
  2. Replacement transfusion: Fresh blood, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate and platelets.
  3. Anticoagulant: Used selectively — typically low-dose heparin in chronic consumptive states. Avoid in active hemorrhage.
⚠️ Warning
Treatment of the cause comes first — no amount of plasma or platelets will stop DIC if the trigger is still in place. Delivery of the fetus in abruptio placentae is itself the cure.

10. Diabetes Mellitus with Pregnancy

Gestational Diabetes Monitoring Kit — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition & Incidence

📝 Definition
A disorder in the carbohydrate metabolism characterized by hyperglycemia and glucosuria.
3–5%
Gestational Diabetes
0.5–1%
Pregestational Diabetes

Classification

[A] Pregestational diabetes

Diabetes that exists before pregnancy.

  • Type 1 → Juvenile onset (autoimmune).
  • Type 2 → Adult onset (insulin resistance).
[B] Gestational diabetes

First diagnosed in pregnancy. Classic GDM diagnosed at 24–28 weeks.

  • GDM before 24 weeks → probably undiagnosed type 2 DM → managed as pregestational.

Physiologic Changes During Pregnancy

  • Hyperplasia of pancreatic islet cells → doubling in insulin production.
  • Initial increase in insulin sensitivity during first trimester.
  • Progressive glucose intolerance due to insulin-resistant hormones (HPL, glucagon, progesterone, CRH).
  • Increased glucose uptake by fetus and peripheral tissues.
  • Reduced renal tubular threshold → glucosuria.
  • Fasting glucose levels fall by 10–20%, and postprandial levels are higher.

Effect of Pregnancy on Diabetes

  • Disease may appear for the first time during pregnancy.
  • Insulin requirements gradually increase after 3rd month until term.
  • Aggravation of diabetic microangiopathy (nephropathy, retinopathy & neuropathy).
  • During delivery: liability to hypoglycemia.
  • After delivery: insulin requirement decreases due to drop in placental hormones.
🚨 Danger
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in pregnancy can occur at lower blood glucose levels than in non-pregnant patients (sometimes < 200 mg/dL). Any unexplained nausea, vomiting, or Kussmaul breathing is DKA until proven otherwise.

Effects of DM on Pregnancy

Fetal Risks
  • Miscarriage. Congenital anomaly (doubled) (cardiovascular & NTDs).
  • Macrosomia. Prematurity. Shoulder dystocia & birth injury.
  • Respiratory distress. Neonatal hypoglycemia.
  • IUFD. IUGR.
  • Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, hypocalcemia, and hypothermia.
  • Risk of having diabetes: 15% in type 2 & 2% in type 1.
Maternal Risks
  • Hypoglycemia. Diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Polyhydramnios. Obstructed labor.
  • Operative delivery.
  • Worsening of retinal and renal disease.
  • Pre-eclampsia. Postpartum hemorrhage and infection.
💎 Pearl — Pedersen Hypothesis
Macrosomia is driven by fetal hyperinsulinemia in response to maternal hyperglycemia → anabolic growth. The same fetal hyperinsulinemia causes neonatal hypoglycemia after delivery.

Risk Factors for GDM

  • BMI > 30 kg/m².
  • Previous macrosomic infant ≥ 4.5 kg.
  • Previous GDM (30–84% recurrence).
  • First degree relative with diabetes.
  • Glycosuria 2+.
  • Unexplained IUFD.
  • Current macrosomia and polyhydramnios.

Screening & Diagnosis

1-hour GCT (24–28 weeks)

  • 50g glucose challenge test, no fasting required.
  • Normal: < 140 mg/dL. Positive → confirm with GTT.

3-hour GTT

100g glucose after 8h fasting. Any two or more of the values met/exceeded = GDM diagnosis.

11. UTI in Pregnancy

Urinary Tract Infection Diagram — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Diagnostic Investigations

Mandatory

MSU culture. Presence of > 100,000 organisms / mL is diagnostic of asymptomatic bacteriuria.

If pyrexia present

CBC and renal function tests (RFT) are indicated to rule out systemic involvement.

If infections are repeated

Ultrasound of the urinary tract to look for structural abnormality, calculus, obstruction.

💎 Pearl
The > 100,000 organisms / mL threshold is the classic Kass count. In pregnancy, the threshold for treatment is far lower — any bacteriuria in pregnancy should be treated.

Treatment

A · Asymptomatic bacteriuria

Oral antibiotic for 3 days.

B · Cystitis

Treatment for 7 days.

C · All bacteriuria in pregnancy

Should be treated to prevent pyelonephritis and preterm delivery. Guided by C&S.

D · Pyelonephritis

Hospitalized, IV fluid, antipyretics, parenteral broad-spectrum antibiotics (cephalosporins) till culture results or fever settles; then oral antibiotic for total 10–14 days.

Pyelonephritis Step-down Pathway

  1. Hospitalize the patient.
  2. IV fluids + antipyretics.
  3. Parenteral broad-spectrum antibiotics — cephalosporins — start empirically.
  4. Continue parenteral therapy until culture results return or fever settles.
  5. Step down to oral antibiotic according to sensitivity for total 10–14 days.
🚨 Danger
Pyelonephritis in pregnancy is a leading cause of septic shock, preterm labor, and ARDS. Any pregnant woman with fever + flank pain + pyuria is pyelonephritis until proven otherwise.

12. Thromboembolism during Pregnancy

Deep Vein Thrombosis — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Superficial Thrombophlebitis

Common in veins of calf, thigh, inguinal region and vulva.

Clinical Picture

  • Localized discomfort, erythema, pain, palpable lump or cord.
  • Low grade fever.

Complications

Superficial venous thrombosis increases the risk of DVT.

Treatment

Bed rest, analgesic, elastic support. Give heparin if DVT is confirmed.

Deep Venous Thrombosis (DVT)

📝 Definition
A blood clot forms in a deep vein (lower leg, thigh, or pelvis).

Virchow's Triad in Pregnancy

H
Hypercoagulation — Increased procoagulant activity
V
Vascular damage — Endothelial injury at delivery/surgery
S
Venous stasis — Compression of left iliac vein

Prothrombotic Changes in Pregnancy

  • Increased procoagulants such as fibrinogen.
  • Decreased fibrinolysis like decreased protein S.
  • Mechanical factors such as compression of left iliac vein.
💎 Pearl
The mechanical compression of the left iliac vein by the right iliac artery and the gravid uterus is the reason most DVTs in pregnancy are left-sided.

DVT Risk Factors

  1. Past history of VTE.
  2. Thrombophilia.
  3. Old age. DM. Hypertension.
  4. Prolonged labor. CS. Dehydration.
  5. Gross varicose veins. Prolonged immobilization.

Clinical Picture

Most common in deep veins of left side.

  • Fever. Leg swelling and pain.
  • Hotness and cyanosis of the leg. Tender calf muscle.
  • Positive Homan's sign (calf pain with foot dorsiflexion).
  • 50% of cases are asymptomatic.
🚨 Danger
50% of DVTs in pregnancy are asymptomatic — the absence of leg swelling or pain does NOT rule out DVT.

Investigations

  • Compression Duplex ultrasonography — first-line imaging.
  • Venography (for suspected pelvic veins thrombosis).
  • MRI for suspected pelvic veins thrombosis.
  • If PE obvious → start heparin immediately, then V/Q scan, CT, MRI.
  • CBC, coagulation profile, D-dimer.

Management of DVT

Prophylactic

  • Early ambulation after delivery.
  • Prophylactic heparin in risky cases.

Active Management

  • Graduated compression stockings.
  • Start anticoagulant therapy.

Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)

Mode of Action

  • Enhance antithrombin activity.
  • Increase factor Xa inhibitor activity.
  • Inhibit platelet aggregation.

Dose

Bolus of 5,000 IU, then IV Heparin 15,000 IU by IV drip every 12 hours for 5–10 days, then maintenance 10,000 IU SC every 8–12 hours to keep PTT 1.5–2.5 times control.

Complications

Hemorrhage, osteoporosis, thrombocytopenia, skin necrosis.

Antidote

1% protamine sulphate solution 10 mg.

🚨 Danger
Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — platelet counts should be monitored periodically. A >50% fall from baseline mandates immediate switch to non-heparin anticoagulant.

LMWH (Enoxaparin/Clexane)

Advantages over UFH

  • Better bioavailability. Longer plasma half-life.
  • Lower risk of hemorrhagic complications.

Dose

  • Prophylactic: 40 mg daily.
  • Therapeutic: 1 mg/kg body weight every 12 hours.
  • Treatment should continue for 6–12 weeks after delivery.
💎 Pearl
Enoxaparin does not cross the placenta and is not teratogenic. Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic, crosses placenta). UFH and LMWH are the only safe options.

Pulmonary Embolism

Clinical Picture

Symptoms
  • Dyspnea. Cough. Chest pain.
  • Frothy blood-stained sputum. Hemoptysis.
Signs
  • Mild pyrexia. Tachycardia. Tachypnea.
  • Cyanosis. Raised JVP. Pleural friction. Pleural effusion.

Investigations

  • Arterial blood gases (hypoxia and hypercapnia).
  • ECG. Ventilation/perfusion imaging.
  • Chest spiral CT. Pulmonary angiography.
  • MRA. Investigations for DVT.

Treatment

  1. Stabilization of the patient.
  2. Start immediately anticoagulant (IV unfractionated heparin preferred).
  3. IVC filter.
  4. Thrombolysis.
  5. Pulmonary embolectomy.
🚨 Danger
Massive PE is a clinical diagnosis — do not delay empirical anticoagulation while awaiting definitive imaging. Thrombolysis and pulmonary embolectomy are last-resort interventions for hemodynamic collapse.

13. Rh Incompatibility

Erythroblastosis Fetalis (Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn) — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition & Etiology

📝 Definition
An immunological disorder resulting in destruction of fetal red blood cells by antibodies which pass through placenta from maternal blood.

Rh Genetics

  • There are 5 main Rh genes (C, D, E, c, and e); the most important is D.
  • About 85% of the population are Rh positive and about 15% are Rh negative.

When Incompatibility Occurs

  • Rh-negative mother pregnant from Rh-positive husband.
  • Homozygous father (2D): 100% of babies will be Rh positive.
  • Heterozygous father (Dd): 50% Rh positive and 50% Rh negative.

Sensitization Triggers

During Pregnancy
  • Placental separation (most important).
  • Abortion. Ectopic pregnancy.
  • Vesicular mole. Amniocentesis.
  • External cephalic version. APH.
Before Pregnancy
  • Previous blood transfusion of Rh-positive blood.
  • In utero from her Rh-positive mother (grandmother syndrome).

Pathogenesis & Clinical Varieties

When maternal antibodies pass into fetal circulation they cause hemolysis of fetal RBCs and jaundice.

Pathogenesis Cascade

Attachment of IgG to fetal RBCs → hemolysis leading to:

  1. Anemia → anemic heart failure and generalized edema.
  2. Extramedullary hematopoiesis → hepatosplenomegaly → portal hypertension, ascites, hypoproteinemia.
  3. Excess bilirubin production.

Clinical Varieties

Hydrops fetalis (most severe)

IUFD due to anemic heart failure. Macrosomic baby. Severe hemolytic anemia (cord Hb < 8 gm/dL), hepatosplenomegaly, generalized edema, ascites, scalp edema, hydrothorax, large edematous placenta.

Icterus gravis neonatorum (commonest)

Occurs after delivery. Nausea, vomiting, anemia (Hb 8-12 gm/dL), jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly. Severe: bilirubin crosses immature BBB → kernicterus (extrapyramidal effects and convulsions). Yellow staining of amniotic fluid, cord and vernix.

Congenital hemolytic anemia (mildest)

Mild anemia at birth (Hb < 14 gm/dL), mild pallor and transient jaundice.

Investigations

Screening

  • Assessment of maternal and paternal blood group and Rh.
  • If mother Rh-negative and husband Rh-positive: indirect Coomb's test.
  • Critical titer: > 1/16 → significant possibility of sensitization & stillbirth.

Diagnosis of the Affected Fetus

During pregnancy: amniocentesis, cordocentesis, ultrasound.

After delivery: cord blood sampling.

Fetal/neonatal blood examined for:
  1. ABO group & Rh typing.
  2. Hemoglobin & Hct value.
  3. Reticulocytic count.
  4. Direct Coomb's test.
  5. Bilirubin level.
  6. Serum protein level.

Ultrasound

  • Signs of hydrops fetalis: scalp edema, increased amniotic fluid, placental thickening, hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Doppler on middle cerebral artery (MCA) to predict fetal anemia by measuring peak systolic velocity.

Serial Amniocentesis

Indications

High maternal antibody titer. Past history of hydrops fetalis or exchange transfusion.

Value

Indirect assessment of fetal RBC hemolysis by measuring amniotic fluid bilirubin by spectrophotometry plotted against gestational age on the Liley chart.

Timing
  • Before 22-24 weeks if high antibody titer.
  • Otherwise between 30-32 weeks.
  • If past history of IUFD: 10 weeks earlier than expected time of that event.

Management

Prophylactic Management

  • No Rh-positive blood is given to Rh-negative mother.
  • Anti-D immunoglobulin should be given to Rh-negative mothers after labor or abortion.

During Pregnancy

  • If titer < 1/16 (unsensitized): no intervention required. At 28 weeks, standard dose of 300 µg anti-D immunoglobulin.
  • If indirect Coomb's test above critical value: do amniocentesis and put results on Liley curve:
    • Zone 1 (A): Minimal fetal affection. Deliver at term.
    • Zone 2 (B): Moderate affection. Allow pregnancy until AF bilirubin levels increase or until 32 weeks.
    • Zone 3 (C): Severe fetal affection. Risk of IUFD within 7-10 days. Must be delivered or transfused.
💎 Modern Update
MCA Doppler as a noninvasive test has eliminated the need for amniocentesis in many centers. Done every 1-2 weeks starting at 16 to 18 weeks.

Maternal Plasmapheresis

To wash antibodies from maternal circulation. Indicated for severe cases before 24 weeks when intrauterine transfusion cannot be done.

Intrauterine Fetal Transfusion

  • Timing: 24-32 weeks gestation.
  • Blood type: Group O, Rh-negative.
  • Routes: Intraperitoneal or intravascular (into umbilical vein).
  • Amount: (Gestational age in weeks - 20) × 10 mL.

Obstetric & Postpartum Management

  • Vaginal delivery preferred.
  • Cord immediately clamped to avoid further passage of antibodies.
  • Divided 2-3 inches from umbilicus to facilitate exchange transfusion.
  • Manual removal of placenta should be avoided.

Postpartum Anti-D Schedule

Neonate Rh D-negative

Anti-D immunoglobulin is not necessary.

Neonate Rh D-positive + mother antibody negative

Standard dose of anti-D immunoglobulin in 1st 72 hours.

Neonate Rh D-positive + mother antibody positive

No anti-D immunoglobulin. Mother's next pregnancy managed as Rh sensitized.

Neonate — Exchange Transfusion & Phototherapy

Exchange Transfusion — Indications

  1. Cord blood bilirubin > 5 mg%.
  2. Cord hemoglobin < 10 gm.
  3. Rapidly rising bilirubin in first 24 hours.
  4. Clinical jaundice in first 12 hours.
  5. Past history of severely affected baby.

Aims

  1. Correction of neonatal anemia.
  2. Removal of excess bilirubin.

Phototherapy

Blue or blue-green light converts unconjugated bilirubin into non-toxic, water-soluble isomers excreted in bile and urine.

Phenobarbitone

Enzyme inducer that increases activity of glucuronyl transferase enzyme, leading to increased bilirubin conjugation and excretion.

ABO Incompatibility

📝 Note
Differs from Rh incompatibility: Can affect the first baby because most group O women have Anti-A and Anti-B. Most are IgM type so cannot cross the placenta. No need for amniocentesis or preterm delivery. Mild condition treated by phototherapy.

Causes of Neonatal Jaundice

  1. Physiological jaundice.
  2. Rh incompatibility.
  3. ABO incompatibility.
  4. Congenital biliary atresia.
  5. Breast milk jaundice.
  6. Infections: hepatitis, STORCH.
  7. Congenital spherocytosis.
  8. Infant of diabetic mother.
  9. Drug induced.

Hydrops Fetalis

📝 Definition
Accumulation of fluid in the interstitial tissue (skin) and two or more body cavities (pleural, pericardial effusions or ascites).
Immune hydrops fetalis

Due to Rh and ABO isoimmunization.

Non-immune hydrops fetalis — Causes

Genetic: Aneuploidy syndromes. Cardiovascular: Structural AVSD, hypoplastic Rt, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia. Pulmonary: lymphangiectasia. Infections: CMV, Toxoplasma. Renal/GI: Congenital nephrosis, gut duplication. Hematological: Alpha thalassemia. Placental: AV fistula, chorioangiomas. Others: twin-to-twin transfusion, liver cirrhosis, true knots of cord.
