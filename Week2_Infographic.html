Antepartum Hemorrhage

Vaginal bleeding from 20 weeks' gestation to delivery — covering placenta previa, abruptio placentae, vasa previa, ruptured uterus, and the bedside tests that distinguish fetal from maternal blood.

Premium Medical-Reference Infographic · Source: Book Obstetrics 2025-2026-008

1 ILOs of this chapter

By the end of this chapter the student should be able to:

  • To understand the etiological classification of APH
  • To be able to differentiate clinically between APH due to placenta previa or abruptio placentae
  • To be able to describe the management of placenta previa and abruptio placentae
  • To be able the explain the management of both ruptured uterus and vasa previa
  • To realize the possible complications of placenta previa and abruptio placentae

2 Definition

Bleeding from the vagina occurring at any time after 20th week of pregnancy and before the child birth.

Bleeding before 20 weeks (early pregnancy bleeding) is conventionally classified as a threatened/missed/inevitable abortion and is covered in Chapter 05. Bleeding after 20 weeks — APH — is a separate clinical entity because the fetus is potentially viable, the bleeding is more likely to be severe, and the differential diagnosis is dominated by placental causes.

PearlThe 20-week cut-off corresponds approximately to the lower limit of fetal viability — it is the threshold at which neonatal intensive care can offer a chance of survival and at which obstetric management shifts from "preserve the pregnancy" to "preserve the mother and the fetus."

3 Etiology (Classification)

Four etiological categories — two placental, one fetal, one extra-placental.

1 · Placenta Previa

Hemorrhage due to partial separation of abnormally situated placenta in the lower uterine segment.

2 · Accidental Hemorrhage / Abruptio Placenta

Hemorrhage due to partial separation of normally situated placenta (in the upper uterine segment).

3 · Vasa Previa

Fetal hemorrhage (bleeding of fetal origin) due to laceration of abnormally situated umbilical (fetal vessels) vessels.

4 · Extraplacental Bleeding (Incidental Hemorrhage)

Rupture uterus, hemorrhage due to lesions of the cervix or vagina as erosion, polyp or carcinoma.

4 Placenta Previa — Definition & Incidence

Antepartum hemorrhage (APH) due to premature separation of abnormally situated placenta (in the lower uterine segment).

1 / 200
Pregnancies Affected by Placenta Previa
WarnThe lower uterine segment is a relatively passive, poorly contractile area. When the placenta implants here, the vessels that anchor it cannot constrict after separation — leading to profuse, sometimes catastrophic, bleeding.

5 Placenta Previa — Degrees (Types I–IV)

Four degrees are recognised, based on the relationship of the placental edge to the internal cervical os.

TypeNameDescription
I Placenta previa lateralis The placenta is only partially attached to the lower uterine segment, which may be anterior or posterior.
II Placenta previa marginalis A great part of the placenta is attached to the lower uterine segment so that its lower margin reaches down to the internal os.
III Incomplete placenta previa centralis The placenta covers the internal os when it is closed or partially dilated but not when it is fully dilated.
IV Complete placenta previa centralis The placenta covers the internal os completely whether the cervix is partially or fully dilated.

6 Placenta Previa — Clinical Picture

Symptoms

  • It usually presents as painless causeless recurrent vaginal bleeding in third trimester.
  • Mean gestational age at diagnosis is 32 weeks.
  • Associated with increased risk of congenital abnormalities and intra-uterine growth restriction.

Abdominal findings

  1. Abdomen is lax, no tenderness or rigidity.
  2. Fundal level equals the period of amenorrhea.
  3. Fetal parts are easily felt.
  4. FHS usually normal.
  5. Malpresentation & non-engagement: common.

PearlThe classic bedside triad for placenta previa is (1) painless vaginal bleeding, (2) soft lax abdomen, (3) malpresentation/non-engagement. Any one should prompt ultrasound before digital vaginal examination.

7 Placenta Previa — Investigations

  • Ultrasound is the diagnostic technique of choice — especially transvaginal ultrasound.
  • Magnetic resonant imaging (MRI).
WarnNever perform a digital vaginal examination in suspected placenta previa — it can provoke catastrophic hemorrhage. The diagnosis is made by imaging (transvaginal ultrasound), not by palpation.

8 Placenta Previa — Management

Initial resuscitation

  • High intensity care area.
  • Intravenous access.
  • Blood typing and saving.
  • Serial hematocrit.
  • Vital signs observation.
  • Ultrasound scanning.

Remote from term — Expectant management

Provided that the maternal and fetal hemodynamics are stable:

  • If there is preterm contractions magnesium sulfate can be used as a tocolytic.
  • Replace blood loss to keep hematocrit greater than 30%.
  • Steroids to enhance fetal lung maturity from 24–34 weeks.
  • Serial ultrasound examinations every 2 weeks.
  • Bed rest in hospital.

Home care — only under ideal circumstances

  1. Highly motivated patient.
  2. Full understanding of risks.
  3. Ability to maintain bed rest.
  4. Location near hospital.
  5. 24-hour transportation is available.

Delivery

  • Vaginal delivery is allowed when the placental edge is more than 2 cm from the internal os. If it is less than 2 cm, delivery is by cesarean section.
  • In incomplete and complete centralis, the ideal is a well-planned elective cesarean section at 36–37 weeks.
DangerFailure to keep a fully cross-matched unit of blood available for a major placenta previa is a recognised cause of maternal mortality — the lower-segment bleeding can be torrential within minutes.

9 Abruptio Placentae — Definition, Incidence & Classification

A.P.H. due to premature separation of a normally situated placenta (upper uterine segment).

1 / 100
Pregnancies Affected by Abruptio Placentae

Classification

  • Concealed accidental hemorrhage — the blood is retained behind the placenta, inside the uterus (retro-placental hematoma).
  • Revealed (visible) accidental hemorrhage — the blood escapes and appears externally as vaginal bleeding.
  • Combined or mixed type — part of blood is retained inside the uterus and part escapes as vaginal bleeding.

PearlConcealed abruptio is the more dangerous form: less bleeding is seen externally, but the retroplacental hematoma can be large — the abdomen becomes tense, the uterus "board-like," and coagulopathy (DIC) can develop rapidly from consumption of clotting factors.

10 Abruptio Placentae — Etiology

  • Hypertensive disorders with pregnancy — especially pre-eclampsia.
  • Trauma to pregnant woman.
  • Thrombophilia with pregnancy.
  • Submucous fibroid with pregnancy.
  • Premature rupture of membrane on top of polyhydramnios.
  • Idiopathic.
WarnPre-eclampsia is the single most important risk factor for abruptio placentae — abruption is several-fold more common in women with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

11 Abruptio Placentae — Clinical Picture

  • Vaginal bleeding (80% of patients).
  • Blood remains concealed (20% of patients).
  • Abdominal pain from uterine contractions.
  • Uterine tenderness.
  • Board-like abdomen, tenderness & rigidity.
  • Fundal level > period of amenorrhea (blood accumulation).
  • Fetal parts are difficult to palpate due to rigidity.
  • F.H.S distress or death.
Source Image
Sonographic diagnosis of abruptio placentae — retroplacental hematoma at the abrupted edge.
Source: Book Obstetrics 2025-2026-008, p. 46 (Figure: Ultrasound picture of abruptio placenta).
DangerA "board-like" rigid tender uterus with fetal distress is an obstetric emergency — immediate resuscitation and delivery are required regardless of gestational age.

12 Abruptio Placentae — Investigations

  • Blood grouping.
  • CBC.
  • Coagulation profile — prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time, bleeding time and coagulation time, fibrinogen level and fibrinogen degradation products.
  • Ultrasonography — it can show retroplacental hematoma.
WarnUltrasound has limited sensitivity for abruptio placentae (a normal scan does not exclude it) — the diagnosis is largely clinical (painful bleeding + tense uterus + fetal distress).

13 Abruptio Placentae — Complications

Maternal

  • Hemorrhage, shock.
  • Rupture uterus.
  • Acute renal failure.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Sheehan syndrome.
  • Amniotic fluid embolism.
  • Consumptive coagulopathy (DIC).

Fetal

  • IUFD (intra-uterine fetal death).
  • Prematurity.
DangerConsumptive coagulopathy (DIC) is the most feared complication — coagulation factors and fibrinogen are consumed by the retroplacental clot, producing a profound bleeding diathesis that can become uncontrollable.

14 Abruptio Placentae — Management

Termination of pregnancy

If fetal distress is present or the mother is unstable regardless of gestational age while resuscitation is going on. Termination is either by:

  • Artificial rupture of membranes and induction of labor, or
  • Cesarean section.

Conservative treatment

If both fetal and maternal hemodynamics are stable with preterm gestational age.

15 Ruptured Uterus — Definition & Incidence

Disruption of the uterine wall during pregnancy or labor.

1–4 / 1 000
Pregnancies (95% in multipara)
95%
Occur in Multiparous Women
PearlThe "95% in multipara" figure reflects the cumulative weakening of the uterine wall from repeated pregnancies and labors — the primigravid uterus is rarely affected except in the presence of a congenital anomaly, trauma, or invasive procedure.

16 Ruptured Uterus — Etiology

1. During pregnancy

Spontaneous rupture
  • Rupture of uterine scar (due to weak scar caused by infection of the wound, erosion by chorionic villi, USCS, uterine overdistension, repeated vaginal deliveries after CS).
  • Abruptio placenta with severe concealed hge.
  • Invasive trophoplastic disease.
  • Rupture of angular pregnancy or pregnancy in rudimentary horn.
Traumatic rupture
  • Perforation during evacuation.
  • External trauma (external cephalic version), stab wound, kick.

2. During labor

Spontaneous rupture
  • Obstructed labor (contracted pelvis, neglected shoulder, hydrocephalus, malposition, macrosomia).
  • Rupture of uterine scar.
  • Maluse of ecbolics.
  • Multiparity — due to:
    • Weak uterine wall from previous deliveries.
    • Increased incidence of malpresentation, macrosomia, prolonged labor and abnormal uterine action.
    • False sense of security due to previous spontaneous vaginal delivery.
Traumatic rupture

Intrauterine manipulation leading to uterine rupture:

  • Internal version (after drainage of liquor).
  • Manual separation of the placenta.
  • Breech extraction.
  • Destructive operations.
  • Manual dilatation of the cervix.
  • Forceps or Ventouse application.

17 Ruptured Uterus — Risk Factors

  • Excessive uterine stimulation.
  • History of previous C/S.
  • Trauma.
  • Prior rupture.
  • Previous uterine surgery.
  • Multiparity.
  • Non-vertex fetal presentation.
  • Shoulder dystocia.
  • Forceps delivery.

18 Ruptured Uterus — Types, Site & Severity

Types

1 · Complete rupture

Tearing of the uterine wall including the peritoneum.

2 · Incomplete rupture

Tearing of the uterine wall without the peritoneum.

Site of rupture

It depends on the cause of rupture:

  1. Lower segment rupture — in obstructed labor.
  2. At the site of scar.
  3. Extension of cervical tear into lower segment.

Severity of bleeding

  1. Severe bleeding — if uterine artery is torn.
  2. Mild bleeding — if fetus and placenta escape into the peritoneal cavity. The empty uterus retracts so bleeding is mild.
DangerA "mild bleeding" rupture is a trap — although external bleeding is minimal, fetal death, broad-ligament hematoma, and consumptive coagulopathy can still develop rapidly. The absence of brisk bleeding does not exclude a catastrophic rupture.

19 Ruptured Uterus — Clinical Picture

  • Vaginal bleeding.
  • Pain.
  • Cessation of contractions.
  • Absence / deterioration of fetal heart rate.
  • Loss of station of the fetal head from the birth canal.
  • Easily palpable fetal parts.
  • Profound maternal tachycardia and hypotension.

20 Ruptured Uterus — Management

Prophylactic

  • Proper antenatal care (early detection of causes of obstructed labor). Grand multipara should deliver in hospital.
  • Proper intranatal care (ecbolics, version, forceps, ventouse, exploration of birth canal after difficult or instrumental labor).

Curative

  • Emergent laparotomy.
  • Then either:
    • Repair, or
    • Hysterectomy.
  • Internal iliac artery ligation in cases of broad ligament hematoma.

21 Vasa Previa — Definition & Diagnosis

Fetal hemorrhage (bleeding of fetal origin) due to laceration of abnormally situated umbilical (fetal vessels) vessels.

Diagnosis

  • Antenatally: by Doppler ultrasonography.
  • During labor: if there is sudden fetal distress with minimal amount of vaginal bleeding (highly suspect it).
DangerFetal blood volume is small — even 100 mL of fetal blood loss represents a major fraction of total fetal blood volume and can cause hypovolemic shock and death within minutes.

22 Vasa Previa — Causes & Risk Factors

Causes

There are three causes typically noted for vasa previa:

  • Bi-lobed placenta.
  • Velamentous insertion of the umbilical cord.
  • Succenturiate (Accessory) lobe.

Risk Factors

  • Bilobed and succenturiate placentas.
  • Velamentous insertion of the cord.
  • Low-lying placenta.
  • Multiple gestation.
  • Pregnancies resulting from in vitro fertilization.

23 Vasa Previa — Management

When vasa previa is diagnosed prior to labor, elective C.S. is the delivery method of choice.

PearlUnlike placenta previa (where trial of labor may be considered for minor types), vasa previa mandates cesarean delivery whenever it is antenatally diagnosed — the fetal vessels are unsupported and can rupture at the onset of labor with catastrophic fetal exsanguination.

24 Kleihauer–Betke Test

  • A blood test used to measure the amount of fetal hemoglobin transferred from a fetus to the mother's blood stream.
  • Used to determine the required dose of Rh immune globulin.
  • Used for detecting fetomaternal hemorrhage.
PearlThe Kleihauer–Betke test exploits the fact that fetal hemoglobin resists acid elution, whereas adult hemoglobin does not — fetal cells remain pink after acid treatment, adult cells become "ghost" outlines.

25 Apt Test

The test allows the clinician to determine whether the source of blood is fetal (vasa previa) or maternal.

  1. Place 5 mL water in each of 2 test tubes.
  2. Add 5 drops of vaginal blood to one test tube.
  3. Add 5 drops of maternal (adult) blood to the other tube.
  4. Add 6 drops 10% NaOH to each tube.
  5. Observe for 2 minutes.

Result: Maternal (adult) blood turns yellow-green-brown while fetal blood stays pink.

If fetal blood → rapid delivery.

WarnThe Apt test is a rapid bedside test using alkali denaturation of adult hemoglobin — it does not require a lab and is the test of choice in acute vasa-previa suspicion in the labor ward.

By the end of this chapter the student should be able to:

30 Causes of Vomiting in Pregnancy

Vomiting in pregnancy is not always a single disease — five etiological categories must be considered before attributing the symptom to pregnancy itself.

1 · Pregnancy-related (NVP)

Emesis gravidarum, hyperemesis gravidarum.

2 · Complications of Pregnancy

Severe pre-eclampsia, vesicular mole.

3 · Gynaecological Complications

Red degeneration of fibroid, torsion of ovarian cyst.

4 · Medical Causes

Food poisoning, gastritis, cholecystitis, pyelonephritis.

5 · Surgical Causes

Appendicitis, gall stones, peptic ulcer.

PearlAlways consider the non-pregnancy causes first in any patient with severe vomiting — missing appendicitis or pyelonephritis in pregnancy is a recognised cause of maternal mortality.

31 Emesis Gravidarum vs. Hyperemesis Gravidarum

The same symptom on a spectrum — mild and self-limiting, or severe enough to threaten the mother's life.

Emesis Gravidarum

Nausea & vomiting on getting up in the morning, which starts early in pregnancy about the 12th week. It does not affect the general condition.

Hyperemesis Gravidarum (HG)

A condition of severe nausea and vomiting during pregnancy leading to fluid, electrolyte and acid-base imbalance, nutritional deficiency and weight loss.

PearlTime of onset is the bedside clue: emesis gravidarum classically begins around the 12th week, peaks, and resolves — while hyperemesis begins earlier, persists beyond the 1st trimester, and produces measurable metabolic derangement.

32 Etiology and Pathogenesis

The cause is unknown but may be related to six overlapping mechanisms.

a
Hormonal factors

(NVP) are worse in pregnant women with conditions associated with elevated Human chorionic gonadotropins (hCG) levels (molar pregnancies, multiple gestations, and Down's syndrome). Progesterone decreases smooth muscle contractility and may alter gastric emptying and lead to increased nausea and vomiting. Thyroid dysfunction has also been studied as a possible mechanism for NVP and HG development due to cross reactivity between hCG and the thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor.

b
Immune system dysregulation

Immune mechanisms are implicated in NVP and HG development.

c
Gastrointestinal dysmotility

Alterations in lower esophageal sphincter (LES) resting pressure and esophageal peristalsis.

d
Psychosocial factors

Early studies proposed that NVP may be a psychosomatic illness.

e
Infection

About 90% of cases are infected with helicobacter pylori.

f
Allergic theory

The patient is allergic to corpus luteum of pregnancy, so some patients improve on antihistaminics and after the 1st trimester when the corpus luteum degenerates.

PearlThe allergic theory explains two otherwise puzzling clinical observations — that antihistamines help, and that symptoms often resolve in the 2nd trimester when the corpus luteum involutes and placental progesterone takes over.

33 Prevention

  • Multivitamin with folic acid in early pregnancy.
  • Treating heartburn and acid reflux.

34 Complications of Hyperemesis Gravidarum

  • Wernicke encephalopathy from deficiency of vitamin B1.
  • Bleeding diseases or embryopathy from vitamin K deficiency.
  • Malnutrition (immunosuppression, poor wound healing, muscle wasting).
  • Esophageal tears (Mallory–Weiss) and esophageal rupture.
  • Hepatic insufficiency, venous thrombosis, and acute tubular necrosis.
  • Placental dysfunction (preeclampsia, abruption, SGA).
DangerWernicke encephalopathy is the most feared complication of HG — it is preventable with thiamine before any glucose is given. Glucose without thiamine precipitates the encephalopathy by exhausting the last remaining thiamine stores in oxidative metabolism.

35 Investigations

Laboratory

Kidney & liver functions, CBC, hematocrit & electrolytes & blood gases.

Urine

Ketonuria, oliguria, proteinuria, urine culture & sensitivity.

Fundus examination

Fundus examination of the eyes of the mother.

Ultrasonography

To exclude multiple pregnancy & vesicular mole.

WarnUltrasound is essential — molar pregnancy and multiple gestation are two of the most common correctable causes of severe HG, and the scan is the only reliable way to exclude them early.

36 Management — Without Hypovolemia

Step-up approach for the ambulant, non-dehydrated patient: dietary adjustment → trigger avoidance → oral pharmacotherapy.

  1. Dietary changes (Section 9)
  2. Trigger avoidance (Section 10, part B)
  3. Pharmacotherapy (Section 10, part C → Section 11)

37 Dietary Changes

  • Avoid empty or fully stomach.
  • Meals and snacks should be eaten slowly and in small amounts every one to two hours.
  • Eliminating coffee and spicy, odorous, high-fat, acidic, or very sweet foods, and instead consuming protein, salty, low-fat, bland, and/or dry food.
  • Avoid iron supplements which cause gastric irritation and can provoke nausea and vomiting.
  • Fluids: fluids at least 30 minutes before or after solid food to minimize the effect of a full stomach. Fluids are better tolerated if cold, clear, and carbonated.
PearlSuspending iron supplementation until vomiting settles is a simple change that often produces a dramatic improvement — iron tablets are among the most common avoidable triggers of NVP.

38 Trigger Avoidance & Pharmacotherapy

B. Trigger avoidance

Avoidance of environmental triggers as odors, heat, humidity, noise or physical motion.

C. Pharmacotherapy

See the dose reference table in Section 11 for the complete regimen. The order below mirrors the source — start with the safest agent (pyridoxine) and step up to the more potent antiemetics as needed.

  1. Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) — first-line, antiemetic and safe in pregnancy.
  2. Antihistamine (H1 antagonists) — decreases stimulation of the vomiting center.
  3. Doxylamine — sedating antihistamine, often combined with pyridoxine.
  4. Diphenhydramine and Meclizine — for vestibular-component nausea.
  5. Dopamine antagonist — stimulate gastric motility and emptying.
  6. Metoclopramide — prokinetic + central antiemetic.
  7. Promethazine — phenothiazine, broad antiemetic.
  8. Serotonin antagonist — Ondansetron — for refractory cases.
  9. Acid-reducing agents — antacids, H2 blockers, proton pump inhibitors.

40 Management — With Hypovolemia (Hyperemesis Gravidarum)

Diagnosis

Symptoms of hypovolemia
  • Lassitude.
  • Postural dizziness.
  • Thirst.
Signs
  • Manifestation of dehydration as loss of weight.
  • Dry skin and tongue.
  • Tachycardia.
  • Hypotension.
  • Oliguria.

Treatment

  1. Hospitalization.
  2. Replacement fluid therapy and nutrition: most patients respond to intravenous hydration (Saline and Ringer lactate) and a short period of gut rest, followed by reintroduction of oral intake and pharmacologic therapy.
  3. Thiamine supplementation (100 mg daily intramuscularly or intravenously) is given prior to administration of glucose to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy.
  4. Diet: after a short period of gut rest, begin with a diet consisting of bananas, rice, and then advance the diet as tolerated.
  5. Pharmacotherapy: non-oral routes of administration are necessary for patients with persistent vomiting. Antihistamine (H1 antagonists), dopamine antagonists, and serotonin antagonists.
    • Glucocorticoids for refractory cases: Methylprednisolone (16 mg) IV every 8 hours or hydrocortisone 100 mg IV twice daily.
  6. Tube feeding and parenteral nutrition.
DangerThiamine (100 mg IM/IV) MUST be given before glucose in any patient with persistent vomiting — administering glucose first risks precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy in a thiamine-depleted patient.

41 Indications for Termination of Pregnancy

When the mother's life is at risk despite maximal supportive therapy, termination becomes the only remaining option.

Termination is indicated if any of the following develop:

  • Appearance of jaundice.
  • Proteinuria & progressive oliguria.
  • Creatinine rise.
  • Deterioration of the general condition despite treatment.
  • Encephalopathy.
  • Fundus changes.
DangerThese six signs indicate that conservative management is failing — the patient is developing end-organ damage (liver, kidney, brain, retina). Delaying termination beyond this point risks irreversible multi-organ failure.

Anemia with Pregnancy

The single most common medical disorder of pregnancy — covering classification, normal hemoglobin cutoffs, maternal and fetal effects, and the four major anemias: iron-deficiency, megaloblastic (folate / B12), and the thalassemias.

Premium Medical-Reference Infographic · Source: Book Obstetrics 2025-2026-010

44 ILOs of this chapter

By the end of this chapter the student should be able to:

  • To be able to classify different types of anemia with pregnancy
  • To be able to describe etiology and clinical features of each type of anemia
  • To be able to explain the management of anemia with pregnancy

45 Definition

Decrease in the total red blood cells (RBCs) or hemoglobin in the blood during pregnancy leading to decrease in oxygen carrying capacity.

46 Normal Hemoglobin Values in Pregnancy

Trimester-specific hemoglobin cutoffs define who is anemic and who needs treatment. Note that the second-trimester cutoff is lower than the first and third trimesters because of physiologic plasma-volume expansion.

1st Trimester > 11 g/dL
2nd Trimester > 10.5 g/dL
3rd Trimester > 11 g/dL
Postpartum > 10 g/dL
PearlThe 2nd-trimester cutoff of 10.5 g/dL (not 11) is the most commonly forgotten — any value below this in the middle three months is anemia, even though 10.6 g/dL would be acceptable in early pregnancy.

47 Signs and Symptoms

1 · Headache, fatigue and lethargy

Low oxygen delivery to the brain and skeletal muscle — the most common presenting complaints.

2 · Tachycardia and tachypnea

Compensatory cardiovascular response to maintain tissue oxygenation.

3 · Paresthesia and pallor

Tingling/numbness and the classic pale mucous membranes, palms and nail beds.

5 · Tongue glossitis

Smooth, beefy-red, sore tongue — a classical finding in iron and B-vitamin deficiency anemias.

DangerIn severe cases, congestive heart failure can occur. Always auscultate the heart and check for a flow murmur / gallop rhythm in any pregnant patient with profound anemia.

48 Causes — Mechanism-Based Classification

Anemia results from impaired production of red blood cells, increased destruction of red blood cells or blood loss.

1 · Impaired Production

Iron deficiency, B12 / folate deficiency, marrow failure, chronic disease.

2 · Increased Destruction

Hemolysis — sickle cell, thalassemia, G6PD deficiency, autoimmune hemolysis.

3 · Blood Loss

Antepartum hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleeding, hookworm.

Anemia can also be classified by inheritance pattern:

  • Congenital — sickle cell anemia and thalassemia.
  • Acquired — iron-deficiency anemia.

49 Physiologic (Dilutional) Anemia

Why Hb "drops" in a normal pregnancy

There is an increase in overall blood volume during pregnancy, there is an increase in plasma more than red blood cell mass. The red cell mass rises, but plasma rises more — the resulting hemodilution lowers the hemoglobin concentration without any true RBC deficit.

PearlDilutional anemia is physiologic, not pathologic — the body's way of improving placental perfusion by reducing blood viscosity. The mid-pregnancy Hb nadir (~10.5 g/dL) is when it is most pronounced.

50 Effects of Anemia on Pregnancy

Maternal anemia endangers both mother and fetus at every stage — pregnancy, labor, puerperium, and the fetus itself.

Maternal Effects
During pregnancy
  • Pregnancy induced hypertension.
  • Accidental hemorrhage.
  • Preterm labor.
During labor
  • Prolonged labor and inertia.
During puerperium
  • Atony and postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Puerperal sepsis.
Fetal Effects
  • Abortion.
  • IUGR (Intrauterine Growth Restriction).
  • IUFD (Intrauterine Fetal Death).
WarnUterine inertia and postpartum atony share the same mechanism — the myometrium is a working muscle that fails in hypoxia. Anemic mothers bleed more, contract less, and tolerate blood loss worse than non-anemic mothers.

51 Effect of Pregnancy on Anemia

  • Aggravation of pre-existing anemia.
PearlPregnancy is a stress test for the hematopoietic system. Iron stores that comfortably carry a non-pregnant woman become inadequate when the red-cell mass must expand by ~30% — unmasking latent deficiencies.

52 Iron Deficiency Anemia

The single most common cause of anemia in pregnancy worldwide. This can occur from the increased production of red blood cells, which requires a lot of iron and also from inadequate intake of iron during pregnancy.

Diagnosis

TestFinding
Blood filmMicrocytic hypochromic anemia
Serum iron concentrationDecreased (n = 60–170 µmg/dL)
Iron binding capacityIncreased (n = 200–450 µg/dL)
Serum ferritin(Diagnostic) less than 30 ng/mL

Treatment

Prophylactic
  • Eating iron-rich foods. This includes dark green leafy vegetables, eggs, meat, fish, dried beans, and fortified grains.
  • Supplemental oral iron 27–30 mg daily.
Therapeutic
  • Oral iron (ferrous sulphate, ferrous gluconate) ranges from 40–200 mg elemental iron daily. This should be on an empty stomach, 1 hour before meals, with a source of vitamin C (ascorbic acid) such as orange juice to maximize absorption.
  • Adverse effects in some cases may include gastrointestinal side effects, nausea, diarrhea, and constipation.

Factors Influencing the Absorption of Iron

Factors that inhibit iron absorption

Foods rich in calcium, Tannins in tea and Phytates in cereals.

Factors that enhance iron absorption

Heme iron, Ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) and Ascorbic acid.

Parenteral Iron

For those with iron deficiency that cannot be managed with oral therapy because of lack of compliance, severe gastrointestinal side effects, malabsorption, or continuing significant blood loss.

RouteAgentNotes
Intramuscular (IM) Low molecular weight iron dextran However, injections tend to be painful and there is a significant risk of permanent skin staining.
Intravenous (IV) Iron hydroxide sucrose complex Can be used in cases of severe iron deficiency anemia.
Contraindications for IV iron
  • History of anaphylaxis or reactions to parenteral iron therapy
  • First trimester of pregnancy
  • Active acute or chronic infection
  • Chronic liver disease

Blood Transfusion

Reserved for those with imminent cardiac compromise or low levels of iron immediately before delivery at 37 weeks of pregnancy.

PearlThe 1-hour-before-meals rule plus vitamin C co-administration can double oral iron absorption — the cheapest intervention in iron-deficiency anemia, often missed.
DangerAnaphylaxis to IV iron dextran, while uncommon, is the reason it is contraindicated in the 1st trimester — every maternal life lost this way is preventable by using the safer sucrose complex in the 2nd/3rd trimesters only.

53 Megaloblastic Anemia — Folic Acid Deficiency

The second most common nutritional anemia of pregnancy. Without folate, DNA synthesis stalls in the bone marrow — producing the characteristic oversized, immature red cell precursors (megaloblasts).

Daily Requirement

The daily requirement during pregnancy is 200–300 μg/day.

Etiology

  1. Inadequate intake.
  2. Defective absorption.
  3. Increased demand e.g. pregnancy.
  4. Drugs: folic acid antagonists as anti-epileptic drugs.

Clinical Picture

  • General symptoms of anemia.
  • GIT manifestations in the form of:
    • Dyspepsia.
    • Anorexia.
    • Nausea.
    • Vomiting.
    • Diarrhea.

Investigations

  1. Blood film:
    • Macrocytic hyperchromic RBCs.
    • Hypersegmented neutrophilic nuclei (>5 lobes).
  2. Serum folate level: is low measured by radioimmunoassay.
  3. Bone marrow: abnormal red cell precursors (megaloblasts).

Treatment

  • Diet rich in folic acid as liver, kidney and meat.
  • Folic acid 5–15 mg/day orally.
PearlHypersegmented neutrophils (>5 lobes) on a routine blood film are often the earliest sign of folate deficiency — they appear before the macrocytosis and before the hemoglobin falls.

54 Vitamin B12 Deficiency Anemia

Rarer in pregnancy than folate deficiency — but uniquely dangerous because of the irreversible neurologic damage it can cause if missed.

Etiology

  1. Inadequate intake (rare).
  2. Deficient intrinsic factor as in atrophic gastritis or gastrectomy.
  3. Malabsorption syndrome.
  4. Increased demand e.g. pregnancy.

Clinical Picture

  • General symptoms of anemia.
  • GIT manifestations: as folic acid deficiency.
  • Nervous manifestations:
    • Subacute combined degeneration.
    • Peripheral neuritis.

Investigations

As folic acid deficiency + decreased serum vit. B12.

Treatment

Vit. B12 IM injection.

WarnSubacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (dorsal columns + corticospinal tracts) is the neurologic hallmark of B12 deficiency — numbness, paresthesia, ataxia, and spastic weakness. It is potentially irreversible if treatment is delayed, distinguishing B12 deficiency from the more easily corrected folate deficiency.

55 Thalassemia — Definition and Inheritance

An autosomal inherited disorder resulted from failure of production of either α chain (α-thalassemia) or β chain (β-thalassemia) of the hemoglobin molecule and their replacement with other polypeptide chains.
PearlThalassemia is a quantitative disorder — the globin chains are made in the wrong numbers — whereas sickle cell disease is a qualitative disorder — the chains are made but defective. Both are autosomal, but the underlying molecular pathology is different.

56 α-Thalassemia — Major and Minor

(I) α-Thalassemia Major (homozygous)
  • The fetus with this disorder is affected in utero showing polyhydramnios, erythroblastosis, anemia and hydrops.
  • The fetus does not survive due to inability of oxygen transfer as the α-chain is responsible for O2 carrying capacity.
(I) α-Thalassemia Minor (heterozygous)
  • Patient develops mild progressive anemia during pregnancy.
Dangerα-Thalassemia major is incompatible with extrauterine life — the fetus cannot make any functional hemoglobin (HbF still requires α-chains). The obstetric implication is severe fetal hydrops and stillbirth, and the family must be offered genetic counseling.

57 β-Thalassemia — Major and Minor

(II) β-Thalassemia Major (homozygous)
  • The disorder starts in childhood leading to death of the patient.
(II) β-Thalassemia Minor (heterozygous)
  • As α-thalassemia minor. — mild progressive anemia during pregnancy.
PearlUnlike α-thalassemia major, β-thalassemia major is not a fetal disease — the fetus survives on HbF (γ-chains) and only becomes symptomatic in the first year of life as HbF declines. Hence the source's note "starts in childhood."

58 Thalassemia — Investigations and Treatment

Effect on Pregnancy

Anemia becomes severe in the second trimester and heart failure may occur.

Investigations

TestFinding
Blood filmShows microcytic hypochromic anemia
ElectrophoresisDetect type of hemoglobin

Treatment

  • Blood transfusion: in acute attacks.
  • Folic acid: may be indicated.
  • Mode of delivery: CS.
WarnThalassemia and iron-deficiency anemia share a microcytic hypochromic blood film. The discriminating test is not the MCV or the MCH — it is serum ferritin (low in IDA, normal/high in thalassemia trait) and hemoglobin electrophoresis (diagnostic in thalassemia).

62 Cardiovascular Physiology During Pregnancy

Pregnancy is a high-output, low-resistance state — the cardiovascular system is remodeled to perfuse the placenta. These adaptations mimic disease in the unwary examiner.

Key hemodynamic changes
  • Increase in cardiac output due to increased stroke volume and increase in heart rate.
  • Reduced systemic vascular resistance.
PearlThe cardiac output rises ~30–50% by the third trimester, peaking at delivery. This is why cardiac decompensation most often occurs at 28–32 weeks, during labor, and immediately postpartum — when blood volume and venous return are at their highest.

63 Incidence and Etiology

Cardiac disease complicates 1–4% of pregnancies in most populations. Two etiological groups account for nearly all cases.

Incidence: 1–4 %

Congenital

The most common congenital heart disease are ASD, VSD and PDA.

Acquired

Rheumatic heart (85%, the commonest is mitral stenosis), ischemic heart, cardiomyopathies.

DangerRheumatic heart disease accounts for 85% of acquired cardiac disease in pregnancy — and mitral stenosis is the most common valve lesion. In any pregnant patient with new dyspnea + opening snap + diastolic rumble, the diagnosis is mitral stenosis until proven otherwise.

64 NYHA Functional Classification

The single most important bedside tool. It is the language that cardiologists, obstetricians, and anesthetists all use to communicate severity.

CLASS I

No symptoms & no limitation in ordinary physical activity.

CLASS II

Mild symptoms & slight limitation during ordinary physical activity.

CLASS III

Marked limitation in activity, even during less than ordinary activity. Only comfortable at rest.

CLASS IV

Severe limitations, symptoms even at rest.

PearlNYHA class is dynamic in pregnancy — a Class II patient can deteriorate to Class III as blood volume rises, then improve again postpartum. Re-classify at every antenatal visit, not just at booking.

65 Diagnosis — History

  • Rheumatic fever
  • Heart lesion
  • Dyspnea
  • Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
  • Orthopnea
  • Hemoptysis
PearlParoxysmal nocturnal dyspnea and orthopnea are the two symptoms that point away from "physiologic pregnancy" and toward true left-sided heart failure — ask about them in every antenatal cardiac review.

66 Diagnosis — Examination

  • Murmur
  • Accentuated heart sound
  • Arrhythmia
  • Central cyanosis
  • Displaced apex beat
  • Manifestations of left side heart failure e.g. gallop rhythm, crepitations over lung bases and pleural effusion.
  • Manifestations of right side heart failure e.g. congested neck veins, enlarged tender liver, ascitis and oedema lower limbs.
WarnThe classic triad of decompensation is tachycardia >100/min + crepitations at lung bases + displaced apex. This is the "earliest evidence of decompensation" the source flags — admit to hospital immediately when it appears.

67 Diagnosis — Investigations

  • Chest X-ray: may show cardiac enlargement, pulmonary congestion or pleural effusion.
  • Electrocardiography (ECG).
  • Echocardiography: for cardiac structure and function.
PearlEchocardiography is the investigation of choice in pregnancy — it is non-ionizing, gives structural and functional information, and is safe to repeat serially. Reserve CXR and CT for cases where the echo is inconclusive.

68 Misleading Findings in Pregnancy

Three classes of "abnormality" that are normal in pregnancy. Knowing them prevents over-investigation and unnecessary admission.

1 · Dyspnea and tachycardia
2 · Increased neck (jugular) venous pressure

During normal pregnancy up to +5 cm is not uncommon due to high cardiac output.

3 · Auscultation changes due to hyperkinetic circulation
  • Systolic ejection murmur.
  • Splitting of the first heart sound.
  • Early diastolic murmur due to increased velocity of the blood passing through the aortic and pulmonary valves.
  • Presence of third heart sound.
WarnA systolic ejection murmur and a third heart sound are physiologic in most pregnancies. Do not admit every pregnant woman with a "new murmur" — but do an echo to confirm, because missing a true MS or AS is far more dangerous than over-imaging.

69 Effect of Pregnancy on Heart Disease

  • Deteriorate the patient one functional class.
  • May precipitate heart failure mostly at 28–32 weeks, 2nd, 3rd, 4th stage of labor due to high blood volume which increase cardiac output.
  • Increase risk of subacute bacterial endocarditis, thromboembolism and rheumatic attacks.
DangerThree high-risk windows for decompensation: 28–32 weeks (peak blood volume), the 2nd stage of labor (Valsalva + tachycardia), and the immediate postpartum (autotransfusion from the contracted uterus). Plan extra monitoring in all three.

70 Effect of Heart Disease on Pregnancy

Maternal / Obstetric Effects
  • Polyhydramnios.
  • Preterm labor.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Abortion.
  • Excess weight gain during pregnancy.
  • Preeclampsia.
  • Placental abruption.
  • Gestational diabetes.
  • Progressive heart failure.
  • Maternal or fetal death.
Fetal Effects
  • Increase incidence of congenital malformation (CFMF).
  • IUGR and IUFD.
DangerCongenital heart disease in the mother is associated with an increased incidence of congenital malformation (CFMF) in the fetus — preconception counseling and a detailed anatomy scan at 20–22 weeks are mandatory in this group.

71 Management — During Pregnancy

1 · Frequent antenatal visits
2 · Rest
3 · Prevent and treat anemia

As it may induce heart failure.

4 · Hospitalisation on decompensation

If signs of decompensation occur, the earliest evidence is tachycardia exceeding 100 beats/minute and crepitations at the lung bases. Rest in a hospital is better in the last 2 weeks of pregnancy.

5 · Heparin

Is indicated in patients with artificial valves or atrial fibrillation.

6 · Digoxin and diuretics

In cases of heart failure.

PearlThe simplest, cheapest, and most evidence-based intervention: prevent and treat anemia. Volume overload of pregnancy is poorly tolerated when every extra gram of hemoglobin is already missing.
WarnWarfarin is teratogenic in the 1st trimester (warfarin embryopathy) — switch to heparin before conception or as soon as pregnancy is confirmed in patients with mechanical valves.

72 Management — During Delivery

Delivery of a cardiac patient is a planned, multidisciplinary event — not an emergency unless it has to be.

Team & Setting

  • Should be done in high risk unit, multidisciplinary team (senior obstetrician, cardiologist, anesthesiologist).

Route of Delivery

Vaginal delivery is allowed except in patients with restricted cardiac output as:

  • Tight MS
  • Severe AS
  • Primary pulmonary hypertension
  • Eisenmenger syndrome
  • Marfan's syndrome with aortic root > 4 cm

Timing of Delivery

Usually at term except if heart failure happened which may need preterm termination of pregnancy. Patient is not allowed to go postterm.

DangerThe "no postterm" rule matters — every additional day of pregnancy adds blood volume, cardiac output, and risk of decompensation. Induce at 38–39 weeks in compensated cardiac patients; do not wait for spontaneous labor.

73 Management — Intrapartum (Stages 1–3)

During 1st Stage
  • Semi-setting position.
  • O2 mask-sedation.
  • Control fluid intake.
  • Infective endocarditis (IE) prophylaxis.
During 2nd Stage
  • Sedation.
  • Shorten 2nd stage.
  • Close observation.
During 3rd Stage
  • Avoid ergot alkaloids.
  • Diuretics can be given to decrease blood volume and cardiac output.
WarnAvoid ergot alkaloids (methylergometrine) at the 3rd stage — they cause vasoconstriction and hypertension that can precipitate acute decompensation. Use oxytocin infusion and controlled cord traction instead.
Pearl"Shorten the 2nd stage" is a euphemism for instrumental delivery (forceps or ventouse) at the right station — a long, exhausting 2nd stage is the single most common precipitant of acute pulmonary edema in labor.

74 Puerperium & Contraception

Puerperium

  • Postpartum observation for 48 hours is essential as the risk of heart failure is high in this period.
  • Breast feeding is allowed unless there is heart failure.

Contraception

  • Progesterone only methods can be used.
  • Sterilization is recommended if decompensation occurred in this pregnancy.
DangerThe 48-hour postpartum window is when autotransfusion from the contracted uterus can throw a borderline cardiac patient into frank pulmonary edema. Continuous monitoring for 48 hours postpartum is not optional — it is the standard of care.
PearlAvoid estrogen-containing contraceptives in any patient with a high thromboembolic risk (mechanical valves, atrial fibrillation, Eisenmenger, Fontan). Progesterone-only methods (DMPA, implant, levonorgestrel IUD) are safe.

78 Background & the TORCH Acronym

Why this chapter matters

Viral infections in pregnancy are major causes of maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality. Infections can develop in the neonate:

  • Trans-placentally.
  • Perinatally (from vaginal secretions or blood).
  • Postnatally (from breast milk or other sources).

TORCH — infections known to produce congenital defects

TToxoplasma

T. gondii — obligate intracellular protozoan. Cats are the definitive hosts. Congenital severity is highest in the 1st trimester.

OOthers

Parvovirus B19 (B19V), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), measles virus, enteroviruses, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).

RRubella

RNA virus. Droplet infection. 2–3 week incubation. Congenital rubella syndrome is one of the most severe teratogenic infections.

CCytomegalovirus

Double-stranded DNA herpes virus. The most common congenital viral infection. Saliva, urine, body fluids, transplacental, breast milk.

HHerpes simplex

Double-stranded DNA virus. Sexual contact, ascending infection, transplacental. 50 % vertical transmission in primary HSV in labor — C-section is the prevention of choice.

Mnemonic

TORCH → Toxoplasma, Others, Rubella, CMV, Herpes — "the light of pregnancy". Each pathogen crosses the placenta and produces a characteristic congenital syndrome.

PearlThe classic TORCH screen (a single IgM / IgG panel) has been replaced in modern practice by pathogen-specific testing — maternal IgM + IgG with IgG avidity for CMV and toxoplasmosis, PCR on amniotic fluid for definitive fetal diagnosis, and viral PCR for HSV in active lesions. The acronym is still useful for memorization and clinical reasoning, but treatment decisions should be pathogen-specific.
NoteModern additions to the "Others" category include Zika virus (associated with microcephaly and other CNS defects), hepatitis B and C (vertical transmission at delivery, not classically teratogenic), and syphilis (congenital syphilis can produce a wide spectrum of findings — the "great imitator"). Not all of these are in the source's "Others" list; the visual retains the source's original list for source-faithfulness.

79 Toxoplasmosis (T. gondii)

Pathogen & transmission

Caused by T. gondii, an obligate intracellular protozoan. Cats are the definitive hosts. Maternal infection is acquired by eating undercooked meat.

Congenital transmission

The severity of infection decreases with gestational age — most severe infections occur in the 1st trimester.

Clinical picture

Maternal
Acute maternal infection

Usually asymptomatic. Some patients may present with:

  • Posterior cervical lymphadenopathy.
  • Fatigue.
  • Fever.
  • Maculopapular rash.
Fetal — Classic Triad
Congenital toxoplasmosis triad
1

Hydrocephalus

2

Intracranial calcifications

3

Chorioretinitis

Investigations

Maternal

By serology for detection of the specific IgM.

Fetal — PCR

Isolation of toxoplasma in the amniotic fluid by PCR.

Fetal — US

Ultrasound may be useful for detecting fetal anomalies as: hydrocephalus and intracranial calcifications.

Treatment

The drug of choice during pregnancy is Spiramycin, which can reduce the risk of congenital transmission.

PearlToxoplasmosis has an inverse relationship between transmission rate and severity: 1st-trimester transmission is rare but severe (classic triad), while 3rd-trimester transmission is common but subclinical at birth — the same principle that explains why rubella is severe in the 1st trimester (active organogenesis) but mild later. Spiramycin is used for primary maternal infection before fetal transmission is documented; once fetal infection is confirmed by amniotic PCR at ≥ 18 weeks, the regimen is typically switched to pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + folinic acid (with the triple regimen usually deferred until after the 1st trimester due to teratogenicity risk).
NoteThe source notes that "Primary infection causes a life-long immunity and usually prevents re-infection." This is preserved verbatim in §3 lead. Clinically this means pre-pregnancy IgG seropositivity is protective — only primary infection in pregnancy is teratogenic. Reactivation in immunocompromised patients is rare but documented.

Pathogen
RNA virus — teratogenic

Clinical picture

  • Maculopapular rash that persists for 3 days.
  • Generalized lymphadenopathy (especially postauricular and occipital), which may precede the rash.
  • Transient arthritis, malaise, and headache.

Diagnosis

Maternal

If there is suspicion of maternal exposure, both IgM and IgG should be done and repeated after 3 weeks.

  • IgM negative + IgG did not rise → no acute infection.
  • IgM negative + IgG level rise → infection may have occurred but reduced risk of congenital rubella.
  • IgM positive in any sample → acute rubella infection.
Fetal

By detecting viral RNA by PCR from chorionic villi, fetal blood, or amniotic sample.

Prevention

MMR vaccine should be offered to all women in childbearing age. It should not be given to pregnant women.

Treatment

  • Induction of abortion if infection occurs in the first trimester.
  • Immunoglobulin.
DangerFirst-trimester rubella infection has a ~ 85 % risk of congenital rubella syndrome. The combination of the source's "induction of abortion" recommendation with the severity of CRS (IUGR, microcephaly, cataracts, PDA, neuro disease) makes first-trimester primary rubella infection one of the few infections where termination of pregnancy is a medically and ethically legitimate discussion. The MMR vaccine remains the cornerstone of prevention — it is a live attenuated vaccine and is contraindicated in pregnancy (the source's "It should not be given to pregnant women").
PearlThe diagnostic algorithm in the source ("IgM negative + IgG did not rise → no acute infection", etc.) is a classic example of paired-serology interpretation — a single IgM is unreliable because of false positives and persistent IgM after vaccination. The gold standard is a ≥ 4-fold rise in IgG titer between acute and convalescent samples drawn 2–3 weeks apart. The source's 3-week repeat interval matches this standard.

82 Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)

Pathogen & vertical transmission

It is a double stranded DNA virus. Neonatal herpes is acquired from passage through an infected birth canal. The risk of vertical transmission is:

  • 50 % for primary HSV infection in labor.
  • 0–4 % in women with recurrent disease.

Mode of infection

  • Direct contact with mucous membranes or skin infected with the virus, commonly through sexual contact.
  • Fetal infection with HSV can occur transplacentally, as an ascending infection from the cervix.

Clinical picture

  • Painful, vesicular lesions and ulcers.
  • Congenital infections are very rare and may produce disseminated or CNS disease.

Diagnosis & prevention

Diagnosis — Maternal

PCR for detection of IgM and IgG antibodies.

Prevention — C-section

Cesarean section delivery is recommended for all pregnancies complicated by primary genital HSV in labor.

Management

In pregnancy: Acyclovir. In labor: cesarean delivery.

DangerThe 50 % vs 0–4 % vertical transmission differential is the single most clinically important number in this section. It is the rationale for the source's "Cesarean section delivery is recommended for all pregnancies complicated by primary genital HSV in labor" — the dramatic risk difference between primary and recurrent HSV reflects the absence of protective maternal IgG in primary infection. In recurrent HSV, maternal IgG crosses the placenta and provides partial fetal protection, reducing vertical transmission to single-digit percentages.
PearlAcyclovir is safe in pregnancy — it is a category B drug with extensive safety data in pregnancy registries. Suppressive acyclovir therapy from 36 weeks gestation is commonly used in women with a history of recurrent genital HSV to reduce the risk of recurrence at delivery and the need for cesarean delivery. The visual preserves the source's "In pregnancy: Acyclovir" recommendation and pairs it with the "In labor: cesarean delivery" decision-making rule.

Shoulder Dystocia

An unpredictable and unpreventable obstetric emergency (0.2-0.3% of deliveries) that occurs when the fetal head is delivered but the fetal shoulder becomes impacted behind the maternal pubic symphysis, requiring additional maneuvers to deliver. 2Definition & Incidence

📋 Definition

Arrested delivery of fetal shoulder after delivery of fetal head and the need for additional maneuver to deliver fetal shoulder.

📊 Incidence

Unpredictable and unpreventable obstetric complication (0.2-0.3% of deliveries).

🚨 DANGER — "Unpredictable and unpreventable"

The two most important adjectives in the definition are unpredictable and unpreventable. Unpredictable means: even with all known risk factors present (macrosomia, diabetes, previous shoulder dystocia, etc.), the event cannot be reliably forecast; conversely, shoulder dystocia can occur in deliveries with no risk factors at all. Unpreventable means: there is no prophylactic intervention (e.g., elective cesarean for suspected macrosomia) that reliably prevents it; the only effective response is to recognize it quickly and execute the management algorithm. Note: this is why all delivery personnel — obstetrician, midwife, anesthetist, pediatric team — must be familiar with the maneuvers in §6-§7 even if they have never personally seen a case.

3Figure 1 · Turtle-neck sign (clinical diagnosis)

The source chapter's only intrapartum diagnostic finding: the "turtle sign" — the fetal head emerges but then retracts tightly back against the perineum, resembling a turtle withdrawing into its shell.

Source Image · img-000
Figure 1 — Turtle-neck sign: Fetal head emerges and retracts against the perineum. The composite source image shows a real obstetric delivery photograph (fetal head emerging but held back by the impacted anterior shoulder) on the left, paired with a yellow diamond turtle-crossing road sign on the right as a visual mnemonic. The clinical sign gets its name from the resemblance: just as a turtle pulls its head back into its shell, the fetal head is pulled back against the perineum when the anterior shoulder is trapped behind the pubic symphysis. Recognition of this sign is the first diagnostic step in shoulder dystocia — the moment it appears, the operator must stop pulling on the head and call for help.
💡 CLINICAL PEARL — Why you must stop pulling on the head

The natural instinct when the head is delivered is to keep pulling. Resist this urge. Continued axial traction on the head stretches the brachial plexus against the pubic symphysis and dramatically increases the risk of Erb's palsy and other nerve injuries. Once the turtle sign is recognized, the correct sequence is: (1) call for help (§6 step 1), (2) stop pulling, (3) ask the mother to stop pushing, (4) start with McRobert's maneuver (§6 step 3) — these four steps together typically resolve 90% of cases without any internal manipulation.

4Figure 2 · Mechanism (anterior shoulder impaction)

Source Image · img-002
Figure 2 — Mechanism of shoulder dystocia: anterior fetal shoulder impacted behind the maternal pubic symphysis. A sagittal (side-view) cross-section of the mother in the second stage of labor. The fetal head has already been delivered and is visible exiting the birth canal, but the anterior fetal shoulder is wedged behind the pubic bone (the bold black arrow points to the exact site of obstruction). The bisacromial diameter (shoulder-to-shoulder distance) of the baby is too large to navigate the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic inlet in this orientation — the shoulder cannot rotate to the wider oblique diameter on its own, hence the need for the maneuvers in §6-§7.
🧠 MECHANISM — Why McRobert's works

Looking at Figure 2, you can see that the obstruction is the pubic symphysis catching the anterior shoulder. The McRobert's maneuver (next section) works because hyperflexing the maternal hips rotates the pubic symphysis cephalad (upward, away from the shoulder), effectively shortening the distance the shoulder needs to travel. Suprapubic pressure (also next section) then pushes the anterior shoulder down and laterally so it slips under the symphysis. This is why these two maneuvers together — and not internal rotation — are the first-line approach.

5Risk factors

The source lists 10 risk factors split into two groups — 5 Antepartum (identifiable before labor) and 5 Intrapartum (develop during labor). Remember: shoulder dystocia is unpredictable and unpreventable, so risk factors are useful for awareness and preparation, not for prevention.

📋 Antepartum risk factors (5)

1. Previous shoulder dystocia

Recurrence rate ~10% in subsequent pregnancies.

2. Macrosomia

Birth weight > 4,000 g (or > 4,500 g in diabetic mothers) — the single most common risk factor.

3. Diabetes mellitus

Fetal hyperinsulinemia → increased shoulder and chest girth relative to head.

4. Maternal BMI > 30 kg/m²

Maternal obesity is associated with fetal macrosomia + soft-tissue dystocia.

5. Induction of labor

Induction may be performed for macrosomia or other indications, increasing shoulder-dystocia risk if fetus is large.

📋 Intrapartum risk factors (5)

6. Prolonged first stage of labor

Slow cervical dilation may indicate relative cephalopelvic disproportion.

7. Secondary arrest

Cervix dilated to a point, then no further progress in the active phase.

8. Prolonged second stage of labor

Pushing > 2h (or > 3h with epidural) — head may be malpositioned, increasing shoulder-dystocia risk.

9. Oxytocin augmentation

Strong contractions may drive the head past the shoulders before the shoulders have rotated to the oblique diameter.

10. Assisted vaginal delivery

Vacuum or forceps — the operator may pull the head past the shoulders.

🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — "5+5 = 10 Risk factors"

The 10 risk factors split as "5 A's" (Antepartum) + "5 P's" (Intrapartum):

Antepartum (5): Previous SD · Macrosomia · Diabetes mellitus · Obesity (BMI > 30) · Induction of labor — (mnemonic: PMDOI = "Primed for Macrosomia, Diabetes, Obesity, Induction")

Intrapartum (5): Prolonged 1st stage · Secondary arrest · Prolonged 2nd stage · Oxytocin augmentation · Assisted vaginal delivery — (mnemonic: PSPOA = "Prolonged-Slow-Pushed-Out-Assisted")

Unifying principle: every risk factor — antepartum or intrapartum — is a factor that increases the size of the fetus (macrosomia, DM, obesity), changes the dynamics of labor (induction, prolonged stages, oxytocin, arrest), or increases the operator's force (assisted delivery, previous SD). All three mechanisms ultimately converge on the same anatomic event shown in Figure 2: the anterior shoulder is too big to fit under the pubic symphysis.

6Management · Steps 1-5 (call for help → posterior arm)

The first 5 steps of the 11-step algorithm: call for help, episiotomy, McRobert's, suprapubic pressure, and delivery of the posterior arm. McRobert's + suprapubic pressure together resolve ~90% of cases — so these are the most important to memorize.

📋 Step 1 — Call for help (HALT the natural impulse to pull)

🚨 Immediately after recognition of shoulder dystocia, extra help should be called

Senior obstetrician, a pediatric resuscitation team, and an anesthetist should be present.

Why this step is the first: the operator alone cannot manage shoulder dystocia — McRobert's requires an assistant, suprapubic pressure requires a second pair of hands, the pediatric team must be ready for a possibly hypoxic or injured neonate, and the anesthetist may be needed for uterine relaxation if the Zavanelli maneuver (§7) is required.

📋 Step 2 — Episiotomy

✂ Episiotomy: to facilitate different maneuvers.

Enlarges the vaginal opening to allow internal manipulation (steps 4-5, and the rotational maneuvers in §7). Note: episiotomy alone does not resolve shoulder dystocia — the obstruction is at the pelvic brim (Figure 2), not at the introitus — but it gives the operator room to work.

📋 Step 3 — McRobert's maneuver (the most effective single intervention)

🦵 McRobert's maneuver

Flexion and abduction of the maternal hips positioning the maternal thighs on her abdomen. It is the most effective intervention and should be performed first (resolve 90% of cases).

If delivery is not achieved, add suprapubic pressure (step 4).

💡 CLINICAL PEARL — Why "resolve 90% of cases" applies to the McRobert's + suprapubic combination, not McRobert's alone

The source text says McRobert's is the "most effective intervention and should be performed first" and "resolve 90% of cases" — but then immediately notes "If delivery is not achieved, added suprapubic pressure." This is the source's way of saying: McRobert's alone is not 90% effective; the combination of McRobert's + suprapubic pressure is. The two maneuvers are applied together by default — the operator flexes the maternal hips while an assistant applies suprapubic pressure. Internal maneuvers (steps 4-5 and §7) are reserved for the ~10% of cases that do not resolve with this combination.

👊 Suprapubic pressure

Pressure applied by an assistant just above the maternal pubic bone, directed downward and laterally toward the fetal back. Aims to adduct the anterior shoulder and rotate it under the pubic symphysis. Typically combined with McRobert's (step 3) — see Figure 3.

📋 Step 5 — Delivery of the posterior arm

🤚 Delivery of the posterior arm (3 sub-steps)

When McRobert's + suprapubic pressure fail, the next maneuver is to deliver the posterior arm:

  1. Insert a hand in the vagina and flex the elbow. The operator's hand reaches into the vagina along the curve of the sacrum, locates the posterior fetal arm, and flexes the elbow by pressing on the antecubital fossa.
  2. Sweep arm over the chest. Once the elbow is flexed, the forearm is swept across the fetal chest and out of the vagina.
  3. Deliver the posterior arm. With the posterior arm delivered, the bisacromial diameter is reduced (one shoulder is no longer trapped), and the anterior shoulder can usually be delivered with gentle downward traction on the head.
⚠ CLINICAL NOTE — Posterior arm delivery can fracture the humerus or clavicle

This is a more aggressive maneuver than McRobert's + suprapubic pressure, and it carries a higher risk of fracturing the humerus or clavicle of the posterior arm. The fractures usually heal well in newborns, but they should be acknowledged in the documentation. If the posterior arm cannot be reached easily, do not pull hard — proceed to the rotational maneuvers in §7 (Rubin, Wood's corkscrew) or delivery of the posterior shoulder instead.

🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — "R-W-G-C-S-Z" (Steps 6-11 mnemonic)

A simple way to remember the 6 advanced maneuvers in order: Rubin · Wood corkscrew · Gaskin · Cleidotomy · Symphysiotomy · Zavanelli. The progression goes from internal rotation (R, W) → positional (G) → destructive surgery (C, S) → reversal + CS (Z). In a real case, the operator rarely gets past step 8 — by that point the case is in the hands of a senior obstetrician, an anesthetist, and a pediatric team.

🚨 DANGER — Why the source preserves the typo "Vaginal hand" in Wood corkscrew

The source's Wood corkscrew step says "with a Vaginal hand" (with a capital V and using "Vaginal" as a noun). The corrected form is "with a vaginal hand" (lowercase, used as an adjective). The source's odd capitalization is preserved verbatim here per the source-fidelity rule. It does not change the meaning — both refer to the operator's hand placed inside the vagina to rotate the posterior fetal shoulder through 180°.

8Complications · Fetal & Maternal

Shoulder dystocia complications split into fetal (5 items in the source) and maternal (4 items in the source). Fetal complications are usually more severe and more visible; maternal complications are usually less severe but more common.

🍼 Fetal Complications (5)

  • Brachial plexus injury. Stretching of the nerve network supplying the arm — most common after excessive lateral traction on the head.
  • Erb's palsy. A specific brachial plexus injury (C5-C6 roots) causing "waiter's tip" deformity (arm adducted, internally rotated, forearm pronated).
  • Bone fractures (humerus and clavicle). Most common after delivery of the posterior arm (§6 step 5) or excessive traction on the shoulder. Usually heal well.
  • Hypoxic ischemic brain injury. From prolonged cord compression / head compression during the dystocia. The time-pressure to deliver is to prevent this.
  • Fetal death. The worst outcome; most often from prolonged hypoxic injury when all maneuvers fail.

🤰 Maternal Complications (4)

  • Post-partum hemorrhage. From uterine atony (the exhausted uterus after prolonged labor) or from perineal trauma.
  • Vaginal lacerations and anal tears. From operative delivery, episiotomy, or the maneuvers themselves.
  • Uterine rupture. Rare but possible, especially if oxytocin was used for augmentation (§5 risk factor 9).
  • Maternal psychological stress. PTSD, fear of future pregnancies, postpartum depression — often under-recognized but a real complication of any emergency delivery.
⚠ CLINICAL NOTE — Documentation is part of management

Every shoulder dystocia case must be documented in detail — the maneuvers used, the order they were used, the time from recognition to delivery, the neonatal assessment (Apgar, cord pH, evidence of injury), and the maternal assessment (blood loss, perineal trauma). This documentation serves three purposes: (1) clinical handover to the pediatric and postnatal teams, (2) medico-legal record (shoulder dystocia is one of the most litigated obstetric events), and (3) counseling for future pregnancies (a mother who had shoulder dystocia has a ~10% recurrence risk — §5 risk factor 1).

A high-risk pregnancy with more than one fetus inside the uterus. 2Definition & key terminology

📋 Definition

A pregnancy with more than 1 fetus inside the uterus.

The chapter then defines the 5 most important terms used in multiple pregnancy — these are the vocabulary that the rest of the chapter builds on, so they must be memorized first.

💡 CLINICAL PEARL — "Zygote" vs "Chorion" vs "Amnion"

These three words refer to three different layers of biology and three different diagnostic questions:

  • Zygote = the fertilized egg before it divides. Zygosity asks: "Did the twins come from one zygote or two?" → monozygotic (= one zygote → identical) vs dizygotic (= two zygotes → fraternal).
  • Chorion = the outer fetal membrane that becomes the placenta. Chorionicity asks: "How many placentas do the twins share?" → dichorionic (= 2 placentas) vs monochorionic (= 1 shared placenta).
  • Amnion = the inner fetal membrane that holds the amniotic fluid. Amnionicity asks: "How many amniotic sacs do the twins have?" → diamniotic (= 2 sacs) vs monoamniotic (= 1 shared sac).

The timing of the monozygotic embryo's cleavage (see §5 + Figure 2) determines the chorionicity and amnionicity — the later the cleavage, the more structures the twins share. The clinical importance of chorionicity is that monochorionic twins share a placenta and can therefore develop twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS) — see §12.

4Monozygotic vs Dizygotic twins

Twins are classified by zygosity (genetic origin) into two main groups — monozygotic ("identical", from 1 zygote) and dizygotic ("fraternal", from 2 zygotes). The chapter gives the incidence, 6 risk factors (one applies only to dizygotic), and the classification by chorionicity + amnionicity.

📊 Incidence

Twins

1 in 80 pregnancies.

Triplets

1 in 80² = 1 in 6,400 pregnancies.

📋 Risk factors (6 — one applies only to dizygotic)

1. Prior history of multiple pregnancies

Recurrence risk in subsequent pregnancies.

2. History of twins in the maternal family

Hereditary predisposition, particularly for dizygotic twinning.

3. Maternal weight and height

Women with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² and ≥ 165 cm are at ↑ risk for dizygotic twin births (the only risk factor the source flags as specific to dizygotic).

4. ↑ Maternal age

Older mothers have higher FSH levels, increasing the chance of multiple ovulation.

5. ↑ Parity

Multiparous women have higher rates of multiple pregnancy.

6. Racial and ethnic variation

Certain ethnic groups have higher baseline dizygotic twinning rates (e.g., West African populations have the highest rates worldwide; East Asian populations have among the lowest).

📋 Classification

Dizygotic twins

Always dichorionic-diamniotic.

Two separate eggs + two separate sperm → two separate zygotes → each develops its own placenta and amniotic sac. There is no monochorionic form of dizygotic twinning.

Monozygotic twins

Classified based on the timing of cleavage after fertilization.

The timing of cleavage determines chorionicity and amnionicity — the 4 stages are in §5 + Figure 2.

🚨 DANGER — BMI ≥ 30 AND height ≥ 165 cm is a SINGLE risk factor (not two)

The source lists "Maternal weight and height" as a single risk factor, with the specific quantitative criterion BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² AND ≥ 165 cm applying to dizygotic twin births only. It is easy to misread this as two separate risk factors (one for weight, one for height), but the source treats it as a single combined criterion — both the BMI threshold and the height threshold must be met for the increased dizygotic risk to apply.

📋 The 4 stages of monozygotic cleavage (verbatim from source)

Stage 1 · In 1st 3 days

→ Dichorionic Diamniotic.

Earliest cleavage. The morula splits before the chorion forms → each twin gets its own chorion and amnion, and its own placenta. The most separate form of identical twinning.

Stage 2 · 4-8 days

→ Monochorionic Diamniotic.

The blastocyst splits after the chorion has formed but before the amnion forms → twins share one chorion (one placenta) but each has its own amnion. The most common form of identical twinning. Risk of TTTS (see §12).

Stage 3 · 8-12 days

→ Monochorionic Monoamniotic.

The implanted blastocyst splits after the amnion has formed → twins share one chorion and one amnion (no intertwin membrane). Rare, and high-risk because of cord entanglement.

Stage 4 · After 12 days

→ Conjoined twins.

The embryonic disc splits only partially after it has formed → the twins are physically fused at varying body sites. The rarest and most severe form of monozygotic twinning.

💡 CLINICAL PEARL — "The later, the more shared" — the unifying rule

Notice the pattern in the 4 stages: as the cleavage day number goes up (1-3 → 4-8 → 8-12 → >12), the number of shared structures goes up (dichorionic-diamniotic → monochorionic-diamniotic → monochorionic-monoamniotic → conjoined). The biological reason is intuitive: the earlier the split, the more time each half-embryo has to develop its own membranes before the next membrane forms around it. Clinically, the more structures the twins share, the higher the risk of complications — so monochorionic-monoamniotic twins are higher-risk than monochorionic-diamniotic twins, which in turn are higher-risk than dichorionic-diamniotic twins.

6Diagnosis (signs, abdominal exam, ultrasound)

The chapter divides the diagnosis of multiple pregnancy into three streams: signs and symptoms (subjective/observed), abdominal examination (clinical bedside — inspection, palpation, auscultation), and ultrasound findings (definitive imaging). Ultrasound is the gold standard.

📋 Signs and symptoms

  • Exaggerated early pregnancy symptoms (e.g. hyperemesis gravidarum). The higher β-hCG of a multiple pregnancy worsens nausea and vomiting in the first trimester.
  • Symphyseal-fundal height is greater than expected for gestational age. Two fetuses fill the uterus faster than one, so the fundal height is disproportionately large.
  • Abdominal overdistension. Visible abdominal distension beyond what is expected for dates.

📋 Abdominal examination

I. Inspection

Oversized uterus. The abdomen is visibly larger than expected for gestational age.

II. Palpation

  • Fundal level: higher than the period of amenorrhea (i.e. the fundus sits higher than the date would suggest).
  • Fundal, umbilical and first pelvic grips: can detect multiple fetal poles (at least 3 poles should be palpated). Fetal limbs are felt as multiple knobs.

III. Auscultation

  • Fetal heart sounds are heard simultaneously by 2 observers with a minimum difference of 10 beats/minute. Two observers, two stethoscopes, two fetuses — the two heart rates differ by ≥ 10 bpm, confirming two distinct fetuses.
  • Arnaux sign: The superimposition of 2 fetal heart sounds produce a gallop sound. (A "gallop" sound is a triphasic rhythm that emerges when two distinct but overlapping heart sounds fuse into one another at certain positions on the abdomen.)

📋 Ultrasound findings

Sonographic diagnosis (gold standard)

  • ≥ 2 fetuses and ≥ 2 heart activities. Direct visualization of two or more fetuses, each with its own cardiac activity.
  • Diagnosis of congenital anomalies and placenta previa. Ultrasound also allows early detection of structural anomalies and abnormal placental position.
🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — "3 poles, 2 rates, 1 gallop"

A simple way to remember the three bedside findings that distinguish a twin from a singleton pregnancy:

  • 3 poles on palpation (instead of the usual 2 poles — head + breech) — at least 3 fetal poles must be palpable.
  • 2 rates on auscultation — two observers hearing two distinct heart rates that differ by ≥ 10 bpm.
  • 1 gallop on auscultation — Arnaux sign, the superimposition of two heart sounds producing a triphasic gallop rhythm.

When all three findings are present, multiple pregnancy is highly likely at the bedside — and ultrasound is the next step to confirm and to determine chorionicity (see §7).

📋 The 3 chorionicity patterns on ultrasound

🟢 Dichorionic-Diamniotic (DCDA)

  • Lambda sign: a thick, triangular protrusion of tissue leading up to the intertwin membrane. (The "lambda" of the Greek letter λ — the wedge of chorionic tissue between the two amniotic sacs.)
  • Thick intertwin membrane.
  • 2 separate placentae.

🔵 Monochorionic-Diamniotic (MCDA)

  • T sign: the interface between 2 amniotic membranes. (The "T" of the letter T — two thin amniotic membranes meeting the placenta at a right angle, with no wedge of chorion between them.)
  • Thin intertwin membrane.
  • Single placenta.

🟠 Monochorionic-Monoamniotic (MCMA)

  • No intertwin membrane. (Because both fetuses share the same amniotic sac, there is no membrane separating them on ultrasound — this is the diagnostic feature.)
💡 CLINICAL PEARL — Lambda vs T sign in one line

Lambda (λ) = thick wedge = dichorionic (two placentas, the wedge is the chorion between them). T = thin interface = monochorionic (one placenta, the T is where the two amniotic membranes meet the shared placenta). This is the single most important ultrasound distinction in multiple pregnancy, because monochorionic = shared placenta = risk of TTTS (see §12).

8Management · Antenatal care

The opening of the management section: multiple pregnancy is considered a high-risk pregnancy and the antenatal care schedule is intensified accordingly. The source lists 7 specific adjustments.

📋 Antenatal care adjustments (7 — verbatim from source)

  1. Multiple pregnancy is considered a high-risk pregnancy. This is the rationale for all the adjustments that follow.
  2. More frequent prenatal visits. Standard prenatal visit frequency is insufficient for a multiple pregnancy.
  3. More frequent ultrasounds to monitor fetal growth. Twin pregnancies have higher rates of growth restriction and discordant growth; serial growth scans are required.
  4. Adequate maternal nutrition. Two fetuses require more calories, protein, iron, and folate than one.
  5. Monitoring for maternal complications (e.g. gestational diabetes and preeclampsia). Both GDM and preeclampsia are more common in multiple pregnancies (see §10 complications).
  6. Monitoring for fetal complications (e.g. congenital anomalies). The higher anomaly rate in multiple pregnancies (especially monozygotic) requires targeted sonographic surveillance.
  7. Prevention of preterm delivery. Preterm labor is the most common fetal complication of multiple pregnancy (see §11), and proactive measures (cervical-length screening, progesterone in selected cases, lifestyle modification) are part of standard care.
🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — "7 Antenatal care adjustments" (the rule of "more")

Notice that the theme running through all 7 adjustments is "more" — more visits, more ultrasounds, more nutrition, more monitoring (for both maternal and fetal complications), and more active prevention (of preterm delivery). The principle is: a multiple pregnancy concentrates the risks of a singleton pregnancy and adds new risks of its own, so antenatal care is intensified proportionally.

9Management · Obstetric care (route + timing + 3rd stage)

The obstetric management of multiple pregnancy has three components: route of delivery (4 presentation-based rules), timing of delivery (3 chorionicity-based windows), and third-stage precautions (5 specific steps to reduce PPH and sepsis risk).

📋 Route of delivery

🚨 Cesarean section (CS) — mandatory in two situations

Multiple pregnancies in which the presenting fetus is not vertex and for all higher-order multiple pregnancies (triplets) → Cesarean section.

Why: a non-vertex presenting twin (breech or transverse) cannot be safely delivered vaginally because the second twin is at high risk of head entrapment or cord prolapse. Triplets (≥ 3 fetuses) are also delivered by CS by convention — the logistical complexity of monitoring and delivering three fetuses in sequence makes vaginal delivery unsafe.

🚨 Cesarean section — also mandatory for monochorionic-monoamniotic twins

Monochorionic-monoamniotic twins → Cesarean section.

Why: MCMA twins share one amniotic sac with no intertwin membrane, so their umbilical cords can become entangled — vaginal delivery would risk sudden fetal death from cord compression during labor.

✅ Vaginal delivery — vertex/vertex presentation

Vertex/vertex presentation → vaginal delivery.

Both twins in cephalic presentation — the safest configuration for vaginal delivery.

🟠 Vaginal delivery of 1st twin + maneuver for 2nd twin — vertex/nonvertex

Vertex/nonvertex presentation → vaginal delivery of the 1st twin. Then, external cephalic version or internal podalic version and breech extraction of 2nd twin.

The first twin is delivered vaginally in cephalic presentation. The second twin (non-vertex) is then either turned to cephalic by external version, or delivered breech by internal podalic version followed by breech extraction. This combined approach is the standard of care for vertex/nonvertex twins.

📋 Timing of delivery in uncomplicated cases

The source's rule: depending on chorionicity and amnionicity. The later the chorionicity in the cleavage timing (i.e. the more structures shared), the earlier the delivery — because the risk of shared-placenta complications (TTTS, cord entanglement) rises with gestational age.

🟢 Dichorionic-Diamniotic

38 0/7 weeks – 38 6/7 weeks.

Lowest-risk chorionicity → can safely go to early term (38 weeks).

🔵 Monochorionic-Diamniotic

34 0/7 weeks – 37 6/7 weeks.

Shared placenta → higher late-pregnancy risk of TTTS and selective growth restriction → deliver before 38 weeks.

🟠 Monochorionic-Monoamniotic

32 0/7 weeks – 34 6/7 weeks.

Highest late-pregnancy risk (cord entanglement + TTTS) → deliver even earlier, around 32-34 weeks.

📋 In the third stage of labor

5 precautions — the source lists them as 5 bullet points under "In the third stage":

  1. Examine the placenta and the membranes for zygosity. After delivery, the placenta and membranes are inspected — chorionicity can be confirmed anatomically, and zygosity can be inferred (monochorionic = monozygotic with rare exceptions; dichorionic = either dizygotic or early-cleavage monozygotic).
  2. If retained, manual removal may be performed. The overdistended uterus is at higher risk of retained products; manual removal is performed if needed.
  3. Exploration of genital tract for retained products and lacerations. Multiple pregnancy + operative delivery (in vertex/nonvertex) increases the risk of genital-tract trauma.
  4. Guard against postpartum hemorrhage by uterine massage & ecbolics. Uterine atony is a leading cause of PPH in multiple pregnancy — the overdistended uterus contracts poorly. Active management with uterine massage and ecbolic drugs (oxytocin, methylergometrine, misoprostol) is standard.
  5. Guard against puerperal sepsis. Multiple pregnancy is associated with higher rates of chorioamnionitis and postpartum infection — antibiotic prophylaxis and close monitoring are required.

The correct spelling is "amnionicity" (the property of having one or more amnions — parallel to "chorionicity"). The source's typo is preserved verbatim in the §9 heading below, and this inline DANGER callout explains the correction.

10Complications · Maternal

The source lists 9 maternal complications of multiple pregnancy. Most are conditions that are also seen in singleton pregnancy but at higher frequency in multiples (GDM, preeclampsia, anemia, PPH) — and a few are multiples-specific in mechanism (postpartum hemorrhage from uterine overdistension, increased cesarean rate from malpresentation).

🤰 Maternal Complications (9 — verbatim from source)

  • Hyperemesis gravidarum. Exaggerated nausea + vomiting in early pregnancy, driven by higher β-hCG.
  • Gestational diabetes mellitus. Higher placental mass → more insulin resistance.
  • Hypertensive pregnancy disorders (e.g. preeclampsia). Larger placental mass + greater cardiovascular demand.
  • Anemia. Greater iron + folate demand from two fetuses; iron-deficiency anemia is common.
  • Excessive weight gain. Two fetuses + extra placental tissue + extra amniotic fluid + extra maternal blood volume.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage (due to uterine atony or lacerations). Overdistended uterus contracts poorly (atony) and the higher rate of operative delivery increases genital-tract lacerations.
  • Miscarriages. Higher first-trimester pregnancy loss rate, particularly vanishing-twin phenomenon (one twin resorbs, the other continues).
  • Placental abnormalities (e.g., placenta previa). Larger placental footprint increases the chance that the placenta covers the cervical os.
  • Increased risk for cesarean delivery. Higher rate of non-vertex presentation, fetal distress, and CS-mandatory configurations (triplets, MCMA, non-vertex presenting twin).
🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — The "3-fold higher" rule for maternal complications

A useful clinical rule of thumb: most of the complications in the list above (GDM, preeclampsia, anemia, PPH, preterm labor) occur 2-3 times more often in twin pregnancies than in singleton pregnancies. The biological reason is that two fetuses effectively double the placental mass, the blood volume demand, the iron demand, the mechanical load on the uterus, and the cardiovascular workload — so every complication that scales with placental mass or cardiovascular demand is amplified proportionally.

11Complications · Fetal

The source lists 5 fetal complications. Twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS) is the most distinctive — it is unique to monochorionic twins and is covered in detail in §12.

🍼 Fetal Complications (5 — verbatim from source)

  • Preterm labor and birth. The most common fetal complication; overdistended uterus triggers earlier labor. Mean gestational age at delivery for twins is ~36 weeks, and ~50% of twin pregnancies deliver before 37 weeks.
  • Congenital anomalies. Higher anomaly rate in multiples, especially monozygotic (cardiac, neural tube, and midline defects are all more common). Note: the same fetus cannot have two structurally identical anomalies from one set of genetic material, but monozygotic twins are at higher risk of structural defects than dizygotic twins.
  • Low birth weight. Each twin tends to be smaller than a singleton of the same gestational age — and many twins deliver preterm, compounding the LBW risk.
  • Discordant growth. Significant (> 20%) difference in birth weight between the two twins. Discordant growth is associated with higher perinatal mortality, particularly when due to TTTS or selective fetal growth restriction (sFGR) in MCDA twins.
  • Neonatal death. The most severe fetal outcome; driven by the combination of preterm delivery, LBW, anomalies, and TTTS-related complications.
🚨 DANGER — "Neonatal death" is the only fatal complication on the list

Of the 5 fetal complications, 4 (preterm labor, anomalies, LBW, discordant growth) are morbidities that may resolve with appropriate neonatal care — but neonatal death is a mortality, and it is what all the upstream interventions (intensified antenatal care, chorionicity-determined delivery timing, TTTS surveillance) are designed to prevent. Every other complication on the list is a risk factor for this one.

12Twin-to-Twin Transfusion Syndrome (TTTS)

The chapter's most distinctive fetal complication: twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome, a disorder unique to monochorionic twins (twins sharing one placenta). The syndrome is driven by imbalanced blood flow through placental vascular anastomoses between the two fetuses.

🚨 TTTS — definition (verbatim from source)

Occurs in 10%-15% of monochorionic twins.
Due to arterio-venous anastomosis with imbalanced blood flow.
Blood flows in a fixed direction from 1 fetus (donor) to another (recipient).

📋 Donor twin vs Recipient twin — the asymmetric pathophysiology

🩸 Donor twin — gives blood away

  • Anemia (low red-cell mass from losing blood to the recipient).
  • Growth restriction (poor perfusion of fetal tissues → IUGR).
  • Oligohydramnios (low amniotic fluid — the donor's sac becomes visibly "stuck" against the uterine wall on ultrasound, the "stuck twin" sign).

💧 Recipient twin — receives excess blood

  • Polycythemia (high red-cell mass from receiving excess blood).
  • Macrosomia (oversized from excess perfusion and nutrients).
  • Polyhydramnios (high amniotic fluid — the recipient's sac becomes visibly overdistended, the "polyhydramnios" sign).

📋 Management of TTTS

  1. Amnioreduction. Needle drainage of excess amniotic fluid from the recipient twin's sac. Relieves the polyhydramnios temporarily and reduces the risk of preterm labor from uterine overdistension. Often performed serially.
  2. Fetoscopic laser occlusion of placental vessels. Definitive treatment for TTTS — a fetoscope is inserted into the uterus, and the vascular anastomoses on the surface of the shared placenta are identified and laser-ablated. This separates the two fetal circulations and stops the imbalanced blood flow. The preferred treatment for severe TTTS (Quintero stage II-IV).
💡 CLINICAL PEARL — "Donor anemia/oligo, recipient poly/macro"

A simple way to remember the asymmetric picture of TTTS: the donor is "deficient" (anemia, growth restriction, oligohydramnios — all the "-penia" and "-restriction" words), and the recipient is "excessive" (polycythemia, macrosomia, polyhydramnios — all the "poly-" and "macro-" words). The same imbalanced blood flow that causes the donor's deficiency causes the recipient's excess — they are two sides of the same coin.

🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — "Anastomosis = imbalance = Donor vs Recipient"

Three nested facts to remember about TTTS: (1) Anastomosis in the shared placenta is the anatomic prerequisite. (2) When the anastomoses are imbalanced (blood flows in one direction), the result is TTTS. (3) The clinical picture is always asymmetric — one twin (the donor) becomes deficient, the other (the recipient) becomes excessive. Fetoscopic laser occlusion is the definitive treatment because it eliminates the anatomic prerequisite by ablating the anastomoses themselves.

Preterm Labor

Regular uterine contractions associated with cervical change occurring between 20 and 37 weeks' gestation — the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide. This chapter covers the definition (preterm labor vs premature infant), the maternal and fetal causes/risk factors (with idiopathic as the most common), clinical diagnosis (symptoms & signs + ultrasound/TUS + fetal fibronectin), prophylaxis (smoking cessation, cerclage, progesterone), curative management (bed rest, hydration, antenatal corticosteroids, tocolytics, antibiotics), the 5 classes of tocolytic drugs with doses and contraindications, GBS antibiotic prophylaxis, and mode of delivery (cephalic → vaginal; breech → vaginal only if > 34 weeks, else cesarean).

2Definition (preterm labor + premature infant)

📋 Preterm Labor (verbatim from source)

"Regular uterine contractions associated with cervical change occurring between 20-37 weeks' gestation."

It means onset of labor (regular painful uterine contractions associated with effacement and the dilatation of the cervix) prior to completion of 37 weeks.

Premature infant: infant born before 37 completed weeks of gestation.

🔑 KEY DEFINITION — memorize this

The 20-week lower bound distinguishes preterm labor from late miscarriage. The 37-week upper bound distinguishes preterm labor from term labor. Effacement + dilatation distinguishes true labor from Braxton-Hicks contractions.

3Causes & risk factors — Maternal

⚠️ MEMORY ANCHOR — Most common cause

Most common cause of PTL is idiopathic — i.e., when no specific risk factor is identified, idiopathic remains the leading cause.

Previous preterm laborA history of preterm delivery in a prior pregnancy is a major recurrent risk factor.
Local or systemic infectionIncludes UTI, bacterial vaginosis, chorioamnionitis, periodontal disease, and any systemic infection that may trigger prostaglandin release.
Congenital anomalies of uterus & incompetent osSeptate uterus, bicornuate uterus, diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure, and cervical incompetence (incompetent os).
Smoking, obesity, diabetes, low SESLifestyle and metabolic risk factors that cluster together in low-socioeconomic-status populations.
Uterine overdistensionMultiple gestations (twins, triplets) and polyhydramnios mechanically stretch the myometrium.
Maternal age extremesAge < 18 years or > 35 years — both ends of the reproductive spectrum carry elevated risk.
Second- or third-trimester bleedingPlacenta previa or placental abruption — bleeding triggers uterine irritability and prostaglandin release.

4Causes & risk factors — Fetal

Fetal anomaliesCongenital structural malformations (e.g., neural tube defects, cardiac anomalies) can be associated with preterm labor.
IUGRIntrauterine growth restriction — a compromised fetus may trigger earlier delivery.

5Diagnosis — Symptoms & signs

Symptoms & signs (verbatim from source)

🤰 Clinical features of preterm labor

  • Uterine contractions occurring at a frequency of 4 in 20 minutes or 8 in 60 minutes.
  • Lower back pain — often dull, rhythmic, radiating to the lower abdomen.
  • Passage of blood-stained vaginal discharge (show) — release of the cervical mucus plug, often blood-tinged.
  • Sensation of pelvic pressure — feeling that the fetus is pushing down.
  • Bulging membranes / rupture of membranes — visible bulging of the forewaters or frank ROM (PROM).
💎 CLINICAL PEARL — "4 in 20 / 8 in 60" rule

This contraction-frequency threshold is the textbook definition of active preterm labor — memorize it because it is the trigger to start tocolysis + corticosteroids. Below this frequency, Braxton-Hicks contractions or false labor are more likely.

6Diagnosis — Investigations

1. Ultrasound (obstetric)

🩺 What ultrasound evaluates in suspected PTL

  • Identify obvious fetal malformations.
  • Identify abnormalities in fetal growth.
  • Identify abnormalities in amniotic fluid volume (oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios).
  • Confirm fetal presentation to aid in planning mode of delivery.
  • Evaluate the placenta and its location.

A thorough obstetrical ultrasound is helpful in evaluating the etiology of preterm labour.

2. Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS)

🔬 Transvaginal cervical assessment

  • Dilatation of cervix > 2 cm.
  • Shape of cervix on USG: there is bulging of fetal membranes into a widened internal os in case of PTL.
  • Cervical shape progression: the normal shape of cervix is T shape and it changes to Y shape then to V shape and finally to U shape.
📝 MEMORY ANCHOR — Cervical shape progression

T → Y → V → U — the cervix progressively shortens and opens as labor approaches. A U-shaped cervix on TVUS = imminent delivery.

3. Fetal fibronectin test

🧪 Fetal fibronectin assay

Presence of fibronectin glycoprotein produced by fetal amnion in the cervico-vaginal discharge between 24 and 34 weeks is a predictor of PTL.

💎 CLINICAL PEARL — Fibronectin window

The test is useful only between 24 and 34 weeks — before 24 weeks, fibronectin may normally be present; after 34 weeks, its presence is no longer predictive of preterm labor because term delivery is imminent regardless.

7Management — Prophylactic

Prophylactic measures (verbatim from source)

  1. Stop smoking.
  2. Cerclage surgery if cervical incompetence is present.
  3. Progesterone.
📝 Note on cerclage

Cerclage (e.g., McDonald or Shirodkar suture) is placed prophylactically in women with a history of cervical insufficiency, typically at 12–14 weeks, and removed at 36–37 weeks. It mechanically keeps the cervical os closed.

8Management — Curative

I. Bed rest and hydration

Hydration, both orally and intravenously. Bed rest reduces the gravitational and mechanical stimulus on the cervix.

II. Drug therapy

  • Antenatal corticosteroids.
  • Tocolytic therapy.
  • Antibiotic therapy.
⚠️ Sequence of drug therapy

In established preterm labor, all three drug-therapy pillars are often combined: corticosteroids (for fetal lung maturity), tocolytics (to delay delivery 48 hours — the time window corticosteroids need to act), and antibiotics (for GBS prophylaxis).

9Antenatal Corticosteroids

💊 Purpose

Corticosteroids decrease the chance of respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) and other prematurity complications (IVH, NEC, neonatal mortality).

Dose of corticosteroids

Betamethasone

2 doses of 12 mg, intramuscularly, 24 hours apart.

Betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2 (24 h apart)

Dexamethasone

4 doses of 6 mg, intramuscularly, 12 hours apart.

Dexamethasone 6 mg IM × 4 (12 h apart)
💎 CLINICAL PEARL — Why 48 hours?

Corticosteroids need ~48 hours to complete their effect on fetal lung surfactant production. This is also why tocolytics are given — to buy those 48 hours even when labor is actively established.

10Tocolytic drugs & contraindications

Drugs used for tocolysis (5 classes)

  1. Prostaglandin synthetase inhibitor: Indomethacin.
  2. Calcium channel blocker: Nifedipine.
  3. Oxytocin antagonist: Atosiban.
  4. Magnesium sulfate.
  5. Nitric oxide donor: Glyceral trinitrite.
  6. Beta agonist: Ritodrine, Terbutalin.

Contraindications of tocolytics

🚫 Absolute contraindications (tocolysis must NOT be used)

  • Chorioamnionitis.
  • Advanced labor.
  • Pregnancy > 34 weeks.
  • Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia.
  • Abruptio placenta.
  • Fetal demise and distress.
  • Congenital anomaly of fetus not compatible with life.
🚨 DANGER — when NOT to use tocolytics

In each of the 7 conditions above, attempting to delay delivery would harm the mother or fetus (e.g., infection would worsen, abruption would hemorrhage, lethal fetal anomaly would prolong suffering). Always rule out these conditions before starting tocolysis.

11Tocolytic dosing details

Calcium channel blocker — Nifedipine (Nifidipine in source)

  • Initial: 20 mg orally, followed by another 20 mg after 30 minutes.
  • Maintenance: 20 mg every 4-8 hours (maximal 160 mg/day).
  • Side effects: Low incidence — hypotension, dizziness & flushing.
Nifedipine 20 mg PO → 20 mg PO @ 30 min → 20 mg PO q4-8h (max 160 mg/day)

Prostaglandin synthetase inhibitor — Indomethacin

  • Loading: 50-100 mg twice daily orally or rectally.
  • Maintenance: 25-50 mg orally every 6 hours for 48 hours.
  • Limit: Not used after 30 weeks' gestation (risk of premature closure of ductus arteriosus).
Indomethacin 50-100 mg PO/PR → 25-50 mg PO q6h × 48h (stop @ 30 wk)

Beta agonists — Ritodrine

  • Dangerous complications — heart failure (and other β-adrenergic side effects: tachycardia, pulmonary edema, hyperglycemia).
  • Historical use only; largely abandoned because of maternal cardiotoxicity.
⚠️ Use with extreme caution — risk of heart failure

Oxytocin antagonist — Atosiban

  • Bolus: 6.75 mg IV.
  • Infusion: 300 μg/min IV for 3 hours, then 100 μg/min.
  • Drawback: Very expensive.
Atosiban 6.75 mg IV bolus → 300 μg/min × 3h → 100 μg/min

Magnesium sulphate

  • Loading dose: 4-6 g IV.
  • Maintenance: 1-2 g IV for 24-48 hours.
  • Also provides fetal neuroprotection (reduces risk of cerebral palsy in preterm infants < 32 weeks).
MgSO4 4-6 g IV load → 1-2 g IV × 24-48 h (monitor reflexes + RR + urine output)
⚠️ Note on the source spelling "Nifidipine"

The source text spells the calcium channel blocker as "Nifidipine" — this is a typographic error. The correct INN (international nonproprietary name) is Nifedipine. The corrected spelling is used in the dosing card; the source spelling is preserved verbatim in the references block for transparency.

12Antibiotic therapy (GBS) + Mode of delivery

Antibiotic therapy for prevention of group B streptococci infection

🦠 GBS prophylaxis in established PTL

Benzyl penicillin or ampicillin should be given for an established PTL as a prophylaxis against early onset of neonatal sepsis due to group B streptococcus.

In cases of penicillin allergy, clindamycin can be used.

Mode of delivery

Cephalic presentation

With cephalic presentation, the preferred mode is vaginal delivery even if delivery at < 34 weeks.

Breech presentation

Vaginal delivery is allowed if more than 34 weeks, but if < 34 weeks, cesarean section is done.

💎 CLINICAL PEARL — 34-week rule for breech PTL

Before 34 weeks, the preterm fetal head and body are disproportionately small and fragile — vaginal breech delivery carries higher risk of head entrapment, intracranial hemorrhage, and birth trauma. Cesarean is the safer route. After 34 weeks, fetal size is large enough for safe vaginal breech delivery.

Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM)

A spontaneous rupture of the fetal membranes before the onset of labor — either term PROM (after 37 completed weeks) or preterm PROM / PPROM (before 37 completed weeks). This chapter covers the definition, the 5-item etiology (intrauterine infection, prior PPROM, trauma, amniocentesis, polyhydramnios), clinical presentation (sudden gush of clear or pale-yellow fluid), the 5-step diagnostic workup (speculum exam, nitrazine test, fern test, ultrasonography, fetal fibronectin), the 4 effects of preterm PROM, the gestational-age–stratified management (before 26 weeks: termination; 26–34 weeks: conservative with hospitalization + observation + antibiotics + corticosteroids + tocolysis for 48 h; after 34 weeks: delivery), and a complete chorioamnionitis sub-chapter (definition, 4-item etiology, fever > 38 °C + 7 signs diagnosis, definitive-treatment-with-antibiotics management).

2Definition (term vs preterm PROM)

📋 PROM — verbatim from source

"A spontaneous rupture of membranes before the onset of labor. It could be term after 37 completed weeks of gestation or preterm before 37 completed weeks."

Term PROM

Spontaneous rupture of membranes after 37 completed weeks of gestation (i.e., at term).

Preterm PROM (PPROM)

Spontaneous rupture of membranes before 37 completed weeks of gestation — the high-risk variant, associated with neonatal morbidity from prematurity.

🔑 KEY DEFINITION — memorize this

"PROM" = Premature Rupture of Membranes, in which the rupture occurs before the onset of labor. When the rupture occurs before 37 weeks it is called preterm PROM (PPROM); when it occurs after 37 weeks it is called term PROM. The phrase "before the onset of labor" is what distinguishes PROM from the normal rupture of membranes that occurs during labor.

3Etiology (5 risk factors)

Intrauterine infection(chorioamnionitis, subclinical deciduitis) — the most clinically important cause; infection weakens the chorioamniotic membranes and predisposes them to rupture.
Prior history of PPROMA previous preterm premature rupture of membranes is a major recurrent risk factor in subsequent pregnancies.
TraumaAbdominal trauma, motor-vehicle accidents, falls, or any event that mechanically stresses the membranes.
AmniocentesisIatrogenic rupture from invasive prenatal diagnostic procedures (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling) can cause fluid leakage.
PolyhydramniosExcess amniotic fluid mechanically over-distends the uterus, increasing wall tension and membrane stress.
📝 MEMORY ANCHOR — 5 etiologies

INFECTION · PRIOR PPROM · TRAUMA · AMNIOCENTESIS · POLYHYDRAMNIOS — the classic 5-item etiology list. Intrauterine infection is the most important clinically because it is both a cause and a complication of PROM (chorioamnionitis, §11).

4Presentation (sudden gush of fluid)

💧 Typical clinical presentation (verbatim from source)

Patients present with a typical history of sudden gush of clear or pale yellow fluid leaking from vagina.

💎 CLINICAL PEARL — what to ask

A history of a sudden, single, large gush followed by continued leakage distinguishes PROM from urinary incontinence (continuous, no gush) or excessive vaginal discharge (gradual, no gush). The color — clear or pale yellow — is also characteristic: meconium-stained (green/brown) fluid suggests fetal distress, while foul-smelling fluid suggests chorioamnionitis (see §11).

5Diagnosis — 5 modalities

Diagnosis of PROM uses a stepwise approach: speculum examination is the first step, followed by the nitrazine test, fern test, ultrasonography, and (when the diagnosis remains uncertain) fetal fibronectin.

1. Speculum examination — the first step

🔍 Speculum examination

Speculum examination is the first step in the diagnosis of PROM — it is used to directly demonstrate leaking of fluid from the cervical os.

2. Nitrazine test — pH-based

🧪 Nitrazine test (pH principle)

  • The pH of amniotic fluid = 7 to 7.5 (alkaline).
  • The pH of vaginal discharge = 3.5 to 4.5 (acidic).
  • Procedure: yellow nitrazine paper is introduced in the vagina.
  • If it turns to blue → alkaline amniotic fluid is present → diagnosis of PROM confirmed.
  • If it remains yellow → amniotic fluid is not present.
💎 CLINICAL PEARL — Nitrazine color rule

Yellow → blue = +ve (amniotic fluid present). A ~3-log difference in pH (4 → 7) makes the test highly sensitive. False positives occur with blood, semen, bacterial vaginosis, and certain antiseptic solutions — all of which can also raise vaginal pH.

3. Fern test — arborization pattern

🌿 Fern test (crystallization pattern)

Fluid from the posterior vaginal fornix is swabbed on a glass slide and allowed to dry for 10 minutes. If on drying a fern pattern (arborization under microscopy) appears, then amniotic fluid is present (PROM).

📝 Note on the fern test

The fern pattern is created by the unique estrogen + electrolyte + protein composition of amniotic fluid. It is highly specific when a clean posterior-fornix sample is used. Blood and cervical mucus can sometimes produce atypical patterns — these are recognized as atypical ferning and should not be confused with the true amniotic-fluid pattern.

4. Ultrasonography

🖥️ Ultrasound finding

  • Oligohydramnios (low amniotic-fluid volume) — supports the diagnosis when history is suggestive but speculum is non-diagnostic.
  • Anhydramnios (absent amniotic fluid) — strongly suggests prolonged ROM with complete fluid loss.

5. Fetal fibronectin — biochemical confirmation

🧬 Fetal fibronectin protein

It is a glycoprotein present in large amounts in amniotic fluid. It can be detected in 39% of females with PROM by means of an ELISA test.

⚠️ WARN — sensitivity of fetal fibronectin for PROM

Fetal fibronectin is detected in only 39% of females with PROM — its sensitivity is limited. It is more useful as a rule-out test than a rule-in test. A negative fetal fibronectin result does not exclude PROM.

6Effects of preterm PROM (4 consequences)

Preterm laborThe most immediate and most common consequence — once the membranes rupture, uterine contractions typically begin within hours to days.
Pulmonary hypoplasiaDue to severe oligohydramnios — the fetus needs amniotic fluid for normal lung development; severe fluid loss impairs alveolar formation.
Skeletal & joint deformitiesDue to compression — without the cushioning amniotic fluid, the uterine wall compresses the fetus, leading to deformities such as clubfoot (Potter sequence).
ChorioamnionitisAscending infection through the ruptured membranes — see §11–§12 for full clinical picture and management.
🚨 DANGER — Pulmonary hypoplasia is irreversible

Pulmonary hypoplasia from prolonged severe oligohydramnios is not reversible after birth. This is why very early PPROM (< 24 weeks) carries a very high neonatal mortality — the lungs never develop adequately. This is also why before-26-weeks management (§8) leans toward termination: the fetal lung is too immature to develop outside, and severe oligohydramnios will prevent lung maturation in utero.

7Management — General (bed rest + sterile pads)

🛏️ Bed rest

Bed rest reduces the gravitational and mechanical stimulus on the cervix, may slow ongoing fluid loss, and facilitates close maternal and fetal monitoring.

🩹 Sterile pads for inspection

Sterile pads are used for inspection for meconium staining and signs of infection. Pads are changed regularly and inspected for: (a) meconium staining (greenish-brown — suggests fetal distress), (b) foul smell (suggests chorioamnionitis), (c) blood (suggests placental abruption or vasa previa).

8Management — Before 26 weeks (termination)

🚨 DANGER — Before 26 weeks

The risk of developing chorioamnionitis is very high, so termination of pregnancy is better.

Why termination before 26 weeks?

  • Very high chorioamnionitis risk — prolonged membrane rupture before 26 weeks almost always leads to ascending infection.
  • Pulmonary hypoplasia is likely — the lungs are not yet developed enough for survival outside the uterus, and severe oligohydramnios prevents further in-utero lung development.
  • Skeletal deformities are likely — compression-related deformities (Potter sequence) from prolonged severe oligohydramnios.
  • Fetal viability is below 24–26 weeks — most units cannot provide intensive care for a fetus delivered below this threshold.

9Management — 26–34 weeks (conservative, antibiotics, steroids, tocolysis)

🏥 Conservative treatment for 26–34 weeks' gestation (5 pillars)

Between 26 and 34 weeks, the goal is to delay delivery long enough for corticosteroids to mature the fetal lungs, while preventing/maternal-fetal infection. The 5 pillars are:

Pillar 1 — Hospitalization

🏥 Hospitalization

Admit the patient for close monitoring, bed rest, and rapid intervention if labor, infection, or fetal distress develops.

Pillar 2 — Maternal observation

👩 Maternal observation (vital signs, contractions, FBC, CRP)

Monitor: vital signs (for fever > 38 °C → chorioamnionitis, §11), uterine contractions (for preterm labor), full blood count (for leukocytosis), and C-reactive protein (for inflammation).

Pillar 3 — Fetal observation

👶 Fetal observation by cardiotocography (CTG)

Continuous or intermittent CTG monitoring to detect fetal tachycardia (> 160 bpm, a sign of chorioamnionitis) and fetal distress.

Pillar 4 — Antibiotics (IV → oral step-down)

💊 Antibiotic regimen — IV ampicillin + erythromycin, then oral step-down

IV phase (48 hours):

  • IV ampicillin 2 g
  • + Erythromycin 250 mg / 6 hours
  • Given together for the first 48 hours.

Oral step-down (next 5 days):

  • Oral amoxicillin 250 mg / 8 hours
  • + Erythromycin 250 mg / 6 hours
  • Given for 5 additional days.

Total antibiotic course: 7 days (2 days IV + 5 days oral).

Ampicillin 2 g IV + Erythromycin 250 mg/6h × 48h → Amoxicillin 250 mg/8h + Erythromycin 250 mg/6h × 5 d

Pillar 5 — Corticosteroids and tocolysis for 48 hours

💉 Corticosteroids and tocolysis for 48 hours

Corticosteroids (e.g., betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2, 24 h apart, or dexamethasone 6 mg IM × 4, 12 h apart) to accelerate fetal lung maturity, and tocolysis to delay delivery for the 48-hour window the corticosteroids need to act.

💎 CLINICAL PEARL — Why 48 hours?

Corticosteroids need ~48 hours to complete their effect on fetal lung surfactant production. Tocolysis is given to buy those 48 hours even when labor is actively established. After 48 hours, tocolysis is usually discontinued (its benefit beyond 48 h is unproven) but corticosteroids may be repeated only once, ≥ 14 days after the first course.

10Management — After 34 weeks (delivery)

🚼 After 34 weeks' gestation: delivery

After 34 weeks, the fetus is sufficiently mature that the risk of conservative management (chorioamnionitis, cord prolapse) outweighs the risk of prematurity. Delivery is indicated.

📝 Note on the 26-34 vs >34-week cutoff

The 34-week cutoff is the same as in the management of preterm labor (Chapter 31) and is anchored on the lung-maturation data for antenatal corticosteroids: by 34 weeks, most fetuses have produced enough endogenous surfactant that additional benefit from corticosteroids is minimal, and the risk of ongoing oligohydramnios + ascending infection becomes the dominant clinical concern.

11Chorioamnionitis — Definition, Etiology, Diagnosis

A. Definition

📋 Chorioamnionitis — verbatim from source

"Inflammation of fetal membranes which means intrauterine infection."

B. Etiology (4 risk factors)

Prolonged PROMThe single most important risk factor — ascending infection through the ruptured membranes.
Prolonged laborMultiple vaginal examinations during prolonged labor increase the risk of ascending infection.
Prenatal diagnostic proceduresAmniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling (CVS), and other invasive prenatal procedures can introduce pathogens.
Bacterial vaginosisDisruption of normal vaginal flora — most commonly Gardnerella vaginalis overgrowth — predisposes to ascending infection.

C. Diagnosis — fever > 38 °C + any of 7 signs

🚨 DANGER — Diagnostic rule

"Diagnosis: if there is fever > 38 °C with any of the following signs or investigations." The diagnosis is made when fever is present PLUS at least one of the 7 items below.

  1. Maternal tachycardia.
  2. Fetal tachycardia.
  3. Uterine tenderness.
  4. Foul smelling amniotic fluid.
  5. Maternal leukocytosis > 16,000/cc.
  6. Raised C-reactive protein > 2.5.
  7. High vaginal swab culture and stain positive.
⚠️ WARN — the > 2.5 CRP cutoff

The source's CRP cutoff of > 2.5 is presented verbatim. In modern clinical practice the upper limit varies by laboratory; many labs report normal CRP as < 5 mg/L and consider > 10 mg/L suspicious. The source's > 2.5 is preserved here as a relative elevation in the context of pregnancy (where baseline CRP may be slightly higher).

12Chorioamnionitis — Management (ampicillin + gentamicin ± clindamycin)

🚨 DANGER — Definitive treatment is delivery

"Definitive treatment is delivery and evacuation of the uterine contents." Antibiotics alone are not curative while the infected focus (the amniotic cavity) remains in place. Delivery removes the source of infection.

Mainstay of management — Antibiotics until delivery

💊 Antibiotic regimen — ampicillin + gentamicin (± clindamycin if CS)

Regimen until delivery:

  • Ampicillin 2 g IV every 6 hours.
  • + Gentamicin sulfate 2 mg/kg IV as a loading dose, then 1.5 mg/kg IV every 8 hours.
  • Continue until delivery.
  • If cesarean delivery is performed, clindamycin or metronidazole is added for anaerobic coverage.
Ampicillin 2 g IV q6h + Gentamicin 2 mg/kg IV load → 1.5 mg/kg IV q8h until delivery (± Clindamycin/Metronidazole if CS)
💎 CLINICAL PEARL — Why ± clindamycin only at cesarean?

Ampicillin + gentamicin already cover the most common ascending pathogens (Group B Strep, E. coli, other gram-negatives). Anaerobic coverage is added only at cesarean because the surgical incision breaches the skin and exposes deep pelvic tissues to anaerobic flora from the vagina. After a vaginal delivery, the same anaerobes have not been introduced to deep tissues, so additional anaerobic coverage is not required.

Definition

KEY DEFINITION A fetus with a fetal abdominal circumference (AC) or estimated fetal weight (EFW) less than the 10th centile for its gestational age.

The fetus may be constitutionally small without being at increased risk of complications. Fetuses with IUGR have a 2- to 6-fold increase in perinatal morbidity and mortality.

WARN IUGR is not simply "small for gestational age" (SGA). A constitutionally small fetus has normal growth velocity and Doppler studies. IUGR implies pathological growth restriction with abnormal Doppler findings.
3
Types
1 — Asymmetric IUGR

Disproportionate growth restriction with a greater decrease in fetal body and limbs compared to head circumference.

  • Caused by extrinsic factors such as placental insufficiency.
  • Oxygen and nutrients are directed towards vital organs (brain and heart) by passing other organs (e.g. liver, muscle, and fat tissue).
  • Affects the fetus later in gestation.
2 — Symmetric IUGR

Proportional growth restriction in all parts of the fetus.

  • Caused by intrinsic factors such as genetic abnormalities and intrauterine infections.
  • Affects the fetus early in gestation.
MEMORY ANCHOR A-S-L-E: Asymmetric = Spare the head, Later in gestation, Extrinsic (placental). Symmetric = Small everywhere, Early in gestation, Intrinsic (genetic/infection).
CLINICAL PEARL Asymmetric IUGR shows "brain-sparing" — the fetus preferentially shunts blood to the brain and heart. This produces a high head circumference-to-abdominal circumference ratio.

Etiology — Maternal Risk Factors
1 — Maternal Risk Factors

Risk factors for IUGR can be divided into maternal, uteroplacental and fetal risk factors.

  • Maternal medical conditions: pre-existing diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome, sickle cell disease and severe anemia.
  • Substance use: tobacco, alcohol, cocaine, or narcotics.
  • Exposure to teratogenic drugs: ACE-inhibitors, warfarin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, cyclophosphamide or valproic acid.
  • Previous pregnancy with IUGR.
MEMORY ANCHOR M-S-T-P: Medical conditions (DM, HTN, pre-eclampsia, SLE, APS, SCD, anemia) → Substance use (tobacco, alcohol, cocaine, narcotics) → Teratogenic drugs (ACE-i, warfarin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, cyclophosphamide, valproic acid) → Previous IUGR.

Etiology — Placental & Fetal Risk Factors
2 — Placental Risk Factors
  • Placental insufficiency.
  • Placenta previa or placental abruption.
  • Multiple gestation.
3 — Fetal Risk Factors
  • Congenital/early intrauterine infections: toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus infection, varicella, herpes infections, HIV infection, syphilis, or malaria.
  • Genetic abnormalities: aneuploidy.
  • Congenital anomalies: cyanotic congenital heart disease.
DANGER TORCH infections (Toxoplasmosis, Other [syphilis, varicella, parvovirus], Rubella, CMV, Herpes) are a major fetal cause of symmetric IUGR — early screening and treatment can prevent severe growth restriction.
7
Diagnosis — History & Clinical Examination
History

A thorough obstetric history is crucial to identify any risk factors for IUGR.

Clinical Examination
  • Symphysial-fundal height (SFH) is decreased compared to gestational age (at least 3 cm less than gestational age in weeks).
  • Fetus is small for gestational age.
  • Fetal movements are reduced or absent.
WARN SFH lag of ≥ 3 cm below gestational age in weeks is the classic clinical sign. However, SFH measurement has poor sensitivity — serial measurements and ultrasound are more reliable.
CLINICAL PEARL Reduced or absent fetal movements in the context of a small-for-dates fetus should raise immediate suspicion of IUGR with fetal compromise — this is an obstetric emergency.
8
Investigations
Serial Ultrasound Scans

To assess fetal biometry and estimated fetal weight. It also provides detailed information on fetal anatomy, placental morphology, and amniotic fluid volume.

Findings may include:

  • Decreased fetal growth and fetal weight
  • Small placenta
  • Oligohydramnios
Umbilical Artery Doppler

Shows abnormalities such as reduced or reversed diastolic flow.

Biophysical Profile (BPP)

A non-invasive test that examines many parameters to assess fetal well-being.

Typical findings in IUGR include:

  • Oligohydramnios
  • Absent fetal breathing movements
  • Decreased fetal movements and tone
DANGER A BPP score of ≤ 4 indicates the need for delivery.
MEMORY ANCHOR U-A-B: Umbilical Doppler (reduced/reversed diastolic flow) → Amniotic fluid (oligohydramnios) → BPP (≤ 4 = deliver). These three investigations form the IUGR assessment triad.

Complications
Perinatal Complications
  • Preterm labor and delivery.
  • Stillbirth.
  • Perinatal asphyxia.
  • Necrotising enterocolitis.
  • Cognitive delay and behavioral issues.
  • Adult-onset diseases (e.g. diabetes mellitus, obesity, coronary artery disease, hypertension).
  • Motor and neurological disabilities.
  • Intrauterine/neonatal death.
DANGER IUGR is not just a perinatal problem — it programmes the fetus for adult-onset diseases (Barker hypothesis). Adults who were IUGR fetuses have increased risks of diabetes, obesity, coronary artery disease, and hypertension.
CLINICAL PEARL The Barker hypothesis (thrifty phenotype): fetal undernutrition leads to permanent metabolic adaptations that increase risk of chronic disease in adulthood — IUGR is a lifelong condition, not just a delivery concern.

Management — Antenatal · Intranatal · Postnatal
A — Antenatal
  1. Treatment of the underlying cause and no smoking.
  2. Monitoring of fetal wellbeing.
  3. Good diet.
B — Intranatal
  1. Mode of delivery: Cesarean section is the rule as the fetus does not tolerate the reduced oxygen supply and the birth trauma encountered during vaginal delivery.
  2. Continuous intranatal monitoring.
WARN Cesarean section is the rule — the IUGR fetus has limited oxygen reserves and cannot tolerate the hemodynamic stress of labor and vaginal delivery.

Identification and management of problems of dysmaturity.

2
Definition

KEY DEFINITION A pregnancy continuing beyond 42 weeks. Pregnancy between 41 to 42 weeks is called prolonged pregnancy.

MEMORY ANCHOR 41–42 = Prolonged · > 42 = Postterm. Do not confuse the two — management differs. Prolonged pregnancy is monitored; postterm pregnancy requires intervention.
3
Causes
Most Common Cause

Most common cause of post term pregnancy is wrong date so a careful review of menstrual history is important in all such cases.

Maternal Factors
  • Primipara.
  • History of previous prolonged pregnancy.
  • Sedentary life.
Fetal Factors
  • Anencephaly.
  • Adrenal hypoplasia (diminished fetal cortisol response).
CLINICAL PEARL Anencephaly causes postterm pregnancy because the fetal hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis is disrupted — without fetal cortisol, labor is not initiated. This is why dating must be confirmed before diagnosing postterm pregnancy.
4
Diagnosis — Antenatal
(A) Antenatal
  • History: calculation of gestational age.
  • Examination: larger baby size.
  • Ultrasonography:
    • Biparietal diameter more than 9.6 cm.
    • Increased fetal weight.
    • Oligohydramnios.
    • Increased placental calcification.
    • Doppler to diagnose placental insufficiency.
WARN Increased placental calcification on US is a hallmark of placental aging — it reflects the pathophysiology of postterm pregnancy where the placenta becomes insufficient.
DANGER Oligohydramnios + placental insufficiency in a postterm pregnancy = high risk for cord compression, fetal hypoxia, and meconium aspiration. Delivery should not be delayed.
5
Diagnosis — Postnatal
(B) Postnatal
  • Baby length: more than 54 cm.
  • Baby weight: more than 4.5 kg.
  • Skull: well ossified with smaller fontanelles.
  • Finger nails: project beyond finger tips.
MEMORY ANCHOR Postterm postnatal signs: L-W-S-F: Length > 54 cm · Weight > 4.5 kg · Skull well ossified / small fontanelles · Finger nails beyond tips.
6
Complications
Postterm Complications
  • Aging of placenta lead to placental insufficiency, fetal hypoxia, acidosis, and stillbirth.
  • Oligohydramnios leading to cord compression and meconium aspiration.
  • Fetal macrosomia leading to shoulder dystocia, increase the chances of birth trauma (intracranial hemorrhage) and increase the chances of operative delivery.
  • Postpartum hge and infection.
  • Increased operative delivery.
DANGER The cascade of postterm complications: placental aging → hypoxia → acidosis → stillbirth. Each day past 42 weeks increases this risk — this is why timely delivery is essential.
WARN "Postpartum hge" — source abbreviation for "hemorrhage". Macrosomic postterm babies (> 4.5 kg) carry high risk of shoulder dystocia and birth trauma.
7
Management
Termination of Pregnancy

Termination of pregnancy.

NOTE The source is brief: management of postterm pregnancy is termination of pregnancy — induction of labor or cesarean section depending on cervical readiness and fetal status.

102 Definition

Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD): It is death of the fetus in-utero after 20 weeks of pregnancy or if the fetus weight is > 500 gm.

103 Causes

Maternal

  • Diabetes (poorly controlled)
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome
  • Infection
  • Hypertension
  • Preeclampsia
  • Eclampsia
  • Uterine rupture
  • Maternal trauma or death
  • Thrombophilias

Fetal

  • Intrauterine growth restriction
  • Congenital abnormality
  • Genetic abnormality
  • Infection (i.e. Parvovirus B19, CMV, Listeria)
  • Hydrops

Placental

  • Cord accident
  • Abruption
  • Premature rupture of membranes
  • Vasa previa
  • Feto-maternal hemorrhage
  • Placental insufficiency

104 Diagnosis

Symptoms

  • History of the cause.
  • Gradual disappearance of pregnancy symptoms.
  • Breast milk secretion may start.
  • Failure of abdominal enlargement.
  • Cessation of fetal movements.

Signs

  • Uterus < the period of amenorrhea.
  • Difficult palpation of fetal parts due to absent muscle tone.
  • Inaudible FHS by Doppler.

105 Investigations

  • Spalding sign: overlapping of fetal skull bones due to shrinkage of cerebrum after fetal death.
  • Robert sign: presence of gas in fetal large vessels.
  • Ball sign: hyperflexion of fetal spine.
X-ray signs recall: Spalding (skull overlap), Robert (gas), Ball (spine). SRB — scan the bones last when IUFD is suspected.

106 Complications

  • Intrauterine infection.
  • DIC and hypofibrinogenemia (after 3 weeks).
DIC time bomb: Once fetal death has persisted for > 3 weeks, the risk of maternal DIC and hypofibrinogenemia rises sharply. This is why delivery is usually offered — either to prevent DIC or, if it has already begun, to remove the antigenic stimulus. Back-to-back clotting screens plus fibrinogen are essential if expectant management is chosen.

107 Management

First principle: Termination of pregnancy should be offered after diagnosis.

1. Mode of Delivery

Mode of delivery will depend on the gestational age and obstetric history.

2. Induction of Labor

Induction of labor can be accomplished with preinduction cervical ripening followed by intravenous oxytocin.

3. Failed Induction

If failed induction, cesarean section is done.

Expectant management is conditional: Most modern protocols offer delivery within 1–2 weeks of confirmed IUFD to minimise DIC risk. Expectant management beyond 3–4 weeks without close surveillance is dangerous and rarely practiced.

1ILOs & Fetal movement counting

📘 KEY DEFINITION · Intended Learning Outcome

To be able to describe various fetal well-being tests regarding the indications, procedure steps and its interpretation.

I. Fetal movement counting

Timing: After 30 weeks' gestation.

Method: The mother counts the fetal kicks in 3 hours during the period of 12 hours, from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m. for one hour at the beginning, one hour at the middle and one hour at the end of this period. The count is multiplied by 4 to get fetal kicks in 12 hours. If it is less than 10 kicks, this needs further testing with non-stress test (NST).

2Non-stress Test (NST)

📘 KEY DEFINITION · Non-stress Test (NST)

The non-stress test is used to describe fetal heart rate acceleration in response to fetal movement as a sign of fetal health typically after 32 weeks' gestation.

Method

This test involves the use of Doppler-detected fetal heart rate acceleration coincident with fetal movements perceived by the mother.

Results

Normal (reactive) NST

The presence of two or more fetal heart rate accelerations that peak at 15 beat per minute (bpm) or more above the baseline, each lasting 15 seconds or more, and all occurring within 20 minutes of beginning the test.

Abnormal NST

A fetal heart rate baseline that oscillated less than 5 beats per minute.

Frequency of testing

The interval between tests for fetal well-being, was set at 7 days. But, more frequent testing is recommended for women with post-term pregnancy, type 1 diabetes mellitus, fetal growth restriction, or maternal hypertension.

3Contraction Stress Test (CST)

📘 KEY DEFINITION · Contraction Stress Test (CST)

The CST evaluates fetal heart rate in response to uterine contractions induced by breast stimulation or oxytocin infusion. Once 3 contractions occur in a 10-minute period, the fetal heart rate is observed for reactivity and the occurrence of late or variable decelerations.

Interpretation

Result Definition
Negative No late or significant variable decelerations.
Positive Late decelerations following 50% or more of contractions, even if the contraction frequency is fewer than three in 10 min.
Equivocal Intermittent late decelerations or significant variable suspicious decelerations.
Equivocal Fetal heart rate decelerations that occur in the presence of hyperstimulatory contractions more frequent than every 2 min or lasting longer than 90 seconds.
Unsatisfactory Fewer than three contractions in 10 min or an uninterpretable tracing.

4Cardiotocography (CTG)

📘 KEY DEFINITION · Cardiotocography (CTG)

Cardiotocography (CTG) is a technical means of recording the fetal heart beat (cardio-) and the uterine contractions (-toco-) during pregnancy, typically in the third trimester. Simultaneous recordings are performed by two separate transducers, one for the measurement of the fetal heart rate and a second one for the uterine contractions.

Five components of CTG interpretation

  1. Uterine activity (contractions).
  2. Baseline fetal heart rate.
  3. Baseline FHR variability.
  4. Presence of accelerations.
  5. Periodic or episodic decelerations.

Uterine activity

Normal

≤ to 5 contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over a 30-minute window.

Tachysystole

>5 contractions in 10 minutes, averaged over a 30 minute window.

6Fetal Biophysical Profile (BPP)

📘 KEY DEFINITION · Fetal Biophysical Profile (BPP)

The combined use of five fetal components gives accurate method for assessing fetal health than any one used alone. Typically, these tests require 30 minutes of examiner time.

Parameter Score 2 (Normal) Score 0 (Abnormal)
Fetal heart rate reactivity Presence of reactivity, without or without presence of decelerations. Absence of reactivity.
Fetal tone — Extremity extension/flexion At least one episode of fetal extremity extension and flexion within 30 minutes. No episodes of fetal extremity extension/flexion.
Fetal movement At least three gross body movements observed within 30 minutes. Fewer than three gross body movements within 30 minutes.
Fetal breathing Sustained episode of breathing >30 s in duration, occurring at least once during a 30-min period. Absence of sustained episode of fetal breathing.
Amniotic fluid index At least one pocket of fluid >2.0 cm or an amniotic fluid index >5.0 cm. Less fluid.
💡 CLINICAL PEARL · BPP score interpretation

Scores of 8 or 10 indicates good fetal well-being and associated with normal pH. An equivocal test result (a score of 6), needs to be repeated. A score of 4 or less is predictor of abnormal outcome.

7Umbilical Artery Doppler Velocimetry

📘 KEY DEFINITION · Umbilical Artery Doppler Velocimetry

Doppler ultrasonography is a non-invasive technique to assess blood flow in the umbilical artery.

S/D ratio — most commonly used index

The umbilical artery systolic diastolic (S/D) ratio, the most commonly used index, is considered abnormal if it is above the 95th percentile for gestational age.

🚨 DANGER · Absent or reversed end-diastolic flow

Absent or reversed end-diastolic flow indicates increased resistance to umbilical artery blood flow. It is reported to result from poorly vascularized placental villi and is seen in the most extreme cases of fetal growth restriction.
