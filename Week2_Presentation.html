1. Antepartum Hemorrhage (APH)

Placenta Previa — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

Bleeding from the vagina occurring at any time after 20th week of pregnancy and before childbirth.

Etiological Classification

Placenta Previa

Hemorrhage due to partial separation of abnormally situated placenta in the lower uterine segment.

Abruptio Placentae

Hemorrhage due to partial separation of normally situated placenta (upper uterine segment).

Vasa Previa

Fetal hemorrhage due to laceration of abnormally situated umbilical vessels.

Extraplacental Bleeding

Ruptured uterus, lesions of cervix or vagina (erosion, polyp, carcinoma).

Placenta Previa — Types I–IV

TypeNameDescription
IPlacenta previa lateralisPlacenta partially attached to lower uterine segment
IIPlacenta previa marginalisLower margin reaches the internal os
IIIIncomplete centralisCovers internal os when closed, not when fully dilated
IVComplete centralisCovers internal os completely

Abruptio Placentae

APH due to premature separation of normally situated placenta (upper uterine segment). Incidence: 1/100 pregnancies.

Classification

  • Concealed — blood retained behind placenta (retroplacental hematoma)
  • Revealed — blood escapes as vaginal bleeding
  • Combined — part retained, part escapes

Vasa Previa

Fetal hemorrhage due to laceration of abnormally situated umbilical vessels. Causes: bi-lobed placenta, velamentous insertion, succenturiate lobe.

⚠️ Warning
Fetal blood volume is small — even 100 mL of fetal blood loss represents a major fraction of total fetal blood volume.

Kleihauer-Betke Test

Measures fetal hemoglobin transferred to mother's blood stream. Used to determine Rh immune globulin dose.

Apt Test

  1. Place 5 mL water in 2 test tubes
  2. Add 5 drops vaginal blood to one, maternal blood to other
  3. Add 6 drops 10% NaOH to each
  4. Observe for 2 minutes

Result: Maternal blood turns yellow-green-brown; fetal blood stays pink.

📋 Clinical Pearl
Painless vs. painful bleeding is the single most useful bedside distinction between placenta previa and abruptio placentae.

2. Vomiting with Pregnancy

Pregnancy — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Causes of Vomiting in Pregnancy

  • Pregnancy-related: Emesis gravidarum, hyperemesis gravidarum
  • Complications of pregnancy: Severe pre-eclampsia, vesicular mole
  • Gynaecological: Red degeneration of fibroid, torsion of ovarian cyst
  • Medical: Food poisoning, gastritis, cholecystitis, pyelonephritis
  • Surgical: Appendicitis, gall stones, peptic ulcer

Emesis Gravidarum vs Hyperemesis Gravidarum

Emesis Gravidarum

Nausea & vomiting on getting up, starts about 12th week. Does not affect general condition.

Hyperemesis Gravidarum

Severe nausea and vomiting leading to fluid, electrolyte and acid-base imbalance, nutritional deficiency and weight loss.

Etiology (6 mechanisms)

  1. Hormonal factors — elevated hCG, progesterone, thyroid dysfunction
  2. Immune system dysregulation
  3. Gastrointestinal dysmotility
  4. Psychosocial factors
  5. Infection — 90% infected with H. pylori
  6. Allergic theory — allergy to corpus luteum

Complications

  • Wernicke encephalopathy — vitamin B1 deficiency
  • Bleeding diseases/embryopathy — vitamin K deficiency
  • Malnutrition
  • Esophageal tears (Mallory-Weiss) and rupture
⚠️ Warning
Wernicke encephalopathy is preventable with thiamine BEFORE any glucose is given.

Management — With Hypovolemia

  1. Hospitalization
  2. IV fluid therapy (Saline + Ringer lactate)
  3. Thiamine 100 mg IM/IV BEFORE glucose
  4. Diet — bananas, rice, advance as tolerated
  5. Pharmacotherapy — antihistamines, dopamine antagonists, serotonin antagonists
  6. Glucocorticoids for refractory cases

Indications for Termination

  • Jaundice
  • Proteinuria & progressive oliguria
  • Creatinine rise
  • Deterioration despite treatment
  • Encephalopathy
  • Fundus changes

Iron Deficiency Anemia

Most common cause of anemia in pregnancy worldwide.

TestFinding
Blood filmMicrocytic hypochromic anemia
Serum ironDecreased (n = 60-170 µg/dL)
Iron binding capacityIncreased (n = 200-450 µg/dL)
Serum ferritin< 30 ng/mL (diagnostic)

Treatment

  • Prophylactic: Iron-rich foods + oral iron 27-30 mg daily
  • Therapeutic: Oral iron 40-200 mg elemental iron daily, 1 hour before meals with vitamin C

Megaloblastic Anemia — Folic Acid Deficiency

Second most common nutritional anemia. Daily requirement: 200-300 µg/day.

  • Blood film: Macrocytic hyperchromic RBCs, hypersegmented neutrophils (>5 lobes)
  • Treatment: Folic acid 5-15 mg/day orally

Vitamin B12 Deficiency

Rarer but uniquely dangerous — irreversible neurologic damage if missed.

  • Nervous manifestations: Subacute combined degeneration, peripheral neuritis
  • Treatment: Vitamin B12 IM injection

Thalassemia

Autosomal inherited disorder — failure of production of α or β chains of hemoglobin.

α-Thalassemia

Major: fetus does not survive. Minor: mild progressive anemia.

β-Thalassemia

Major: starts in childhood, death. Minor: mild progressive anemia.

4. Cardiac Diseases with Pregnancy

Heart Anatomy — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Cardiovascular Physiology During Pregnancy

  • Increase in cardiac output (stroke volume + heart rate)
  • Reduced systemic vascular resistance
📋 Clinical Pearl
Cardiac output rises ~30-50% by third trimester. Decompensation most often at 28-32 weeks, during labor, and immediately postpartum.

Incidence and Etiology

Incidence: 1-4%

Congenital

ASD, VSD and PDA are most common.

Acquired

Rheumatic heart (85%) — most common is mitral stenosis.

NYHA Functional Classification

ClassDescription
INo symptoms, no limitation in ordinary activity
IIMild symptoms, slight limitation during ordinary activity
IIIMarked limitation, only comfortable at rest
IVSevere limitations, symptoms even at rest

Misleading Findings in Pregnancy

  • Dyspnea and tachycardia (physiologic)
  • Increased JVP up to +5 cm
  • Systolic ejection murmur, splitting of S1, S3 heart sound

Management During Pregnancy

  • Frequent antenatal visits
  • Rest
  • Prevent and treat anemia
  • Hospitalization on decompensation (HR >100 + crepitations)
  • Heparin for artificial valves/AF
  • Digoxin and diuretics for heart failure

Management During Delivery

  • Vaginal delivery allowed EXCEPT tight MS, severe AS, pulmonary hypertension, Eisenmenger, Marfan with aortic root >4cm
  • No postterm — induce at 38-39 weeks

Puerperium & Contraception

  • Postpartum observation for 48 hours
  • Progesterone-only contraception recommended
  • Sterilization if decompensation occurred

Toxoplasmosis

Congenital triad: Hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications, chorioretinitis.

Drug of choice: Spiramycin.

Rubella

RNA virus. Droplet infection. 2-3 week incubation.

Congenital Rubella Syndrome: IUGR, intracranial calcifications, microcephaly, cataracts, cardiac defects (PDA).

⚠️ Warning
First-trimester rubella has ~85% risk of CRS. MMR vaccine is contraindicated in pregnancy.

CMV

Most common congenital viral infection. Double-stranded DNA herpes virus.

Fetal effects: IUGR, sensorineural hearing loss, intracranial calcifications, microcephaly.

7. Shoulder Dystocia

Fetal Positions — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

Arrested delivery of fetal shoulder after delivery of fetal head and need for additional maneuver. Unpredictable and unpreventable (0.2-0.3% of deliveries).

Risk Factors

Antepartum (5)
  1. Previous shoulder dystocia
  2. Macrosomia (>4000g)
  3. Diabetes mellitus
  4. Maternal BMI >30
  5. Induction of labor
Intrapartum (5)
  1. Prolonged 1st stage
  2. Secondary arrest
  3. Prolonged 2nd stage
  4. Oxytocin augmentation
  5. Assisted vaginal delivery

Management — 11-Step Algorithm

  1. Call for help — senior obstetrician, pediatric team, anesthetist
  2. Episiotomy — to facilitate maneuvers
  3. McRobert's maneuver — flexion/abduction of maternal hips (resolves ~90% with suprapubic pressure)
  4. Suprapubic pressure — push anterior shoulder under symphysis
  5. Delivery of posterior arm — insert hand, flex elbow, sweep over chest
  6. Rubin maneuver — rotate anterior shoulder obliquely
  7. Wood corkscrew — rotate posterior shoulder 180°
  8. Gaskin maneuver — turn to all fours
  9. Cleidotomy — cutting of clavicle
  10. Symphysiotomy — incise symphysis pubis
  11. Zavanelli maneuver — push head back, cesarean section

Complications

Fetal (5)
  • Brachial plexus injury
  • Erb's palsy
  • Bone fractures
  • Hypoxic brain injury
  • Fetal death
Maternal (4)
  • Postpartum hemorrhage
  • Vaginal lacerations
  • Uterine rupture
  • Psychological stress

9. Preterm Labor

Premature Infant in Incubator — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

Regular uterine contractions associated with cervical change occurring between 20-37 weeks' gestation.

Risk Factors

Maternal
  • Previous preterm labor
  • Local/systemic infection
  • Congenital uterine anomalies
  • Smoking, obesity, diabetes
  • Uterine overdistension
  • Maternal age extremes
  • 2nd/3rd trimester bleeding
Fetal
  • Fetal anomalies
  • IUGR

Diagnosis

  • Contractions: 4 in 20 minutes or 8 in 60 minutes
  • Lower back pain, blood-stained show, pelvic pressure
  • TVUS: cervical dilatation >2cm, shape progression T→Y→V→U
  • Fetal fibronectin: useful between 24-34 weeks

Corticosteroids

Betamethasone

12 mg IM × 2 doses, 24 hours apart

Dexamethasone

6 mg IM × 4 doses, 12 hours apart

Tocolytic Drugs (5 classes)

ClassDrugDose
Ca channel blockerNifedipine20 mg PO → 20 mg @30min → 20 mg q4-8h (max 160mg/day)
PG synthetase inhibitorIndomethacin50-100 mg → 25-50 mg q6h × 48h (stop @30 wk)
Oxytocin antagonistAtosiban6.75 mg IV bolus → 300 µg/min × 3h → 100 µg/min
Magnesium sulphateMgSO44-6 g IV load → 1-2 g × 24-48h
Beta agonistRitodrine⚠️ Risk of heart failure

Contraindications to Tocolytics

  • Chorioamnionitis
  • Advanced labor
  • Pregnancy >34 weeks
  • Pre-eclampsia/eclampsia
  • Abruptio placentae
  • Fetal demise/distress
  • Congenital anomaly incompatible with life

Mode of Delivery

  • Cephalic → vaginal (even <34 weeks)
  • Breech → vaginal if >34 weeks; C/S if <34 weeks

10. Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM)

Definition

Spontaneous rupture of membranes before the onset of labor.

Term PROM

After 37 completed weeks

Preterm PROM (PPROM)

Before 37 completed weeks

Etiology (5 risk factors)

  • Intrauterine infection (most important)
  • Prior history of PPROM
  • Trauma
  • Amniocentesis
  • Polyhydramnios

Diagnosis — 5 Modalities

  1. Speculum examination — first step
  2. Nitrazine test — pH 7-7.5 (alkaline) = blue → PROM confirmed
  3. Fern test — arborization pattern on dried slide
  4. Ultrasonography — oligohydramnios/anhydramnios
  5. Fetal fibronectin — detected in 39% of PROM cases

Management by Gestational Age

GAManagement
<26 weeksTermination (high chorioamnionitis risk, pulmonary hypoplasia likely)
26-34 weeksConservative: hospitalization + observation + antibiotics + corticosteroids + tocolysis 48h
>34 weeksDelivery

Antibiotic Regimen (26-34 weeks)

IV phase (48h): Ampicillin 2g + Erythromycin 250mg/6h
Oral phase (5 days): Amoxicillin 250mg/8h + Erythromycin 250mg/6h
Total: 7 days

Chorioamnionitis

Diagnosis: Fever >38°C + any of: maternal tachycardia, fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, foul-smelling fluid, WBC >16,000, CRP >2.5, positive culture.

Antibiotics: Ampicillin 2g IV q6h + Gentamicin 2mg/kg IV load → 1.5mg/kg q8h until delivery.

11. Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR)

IUGR Growth Restriction — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

A fetus with abdominal circumference (AC) or estimated fetal weight (EFW) less than the 10th centile for gestational age. 2-6 fold increase in perinatal morbidity/mortality.

Types

Asymmetric IUGR

Spare the head, later in gestation, extrinsic (placental insufficiency). Oxygen directed to brain/heart.

Symmetric IUGR

Small everywhere, earlier in gestation, intrinsic (genetic/infection).

Etiology

Maternal
  • DM, chronic HTN, pre-eclampsia, SLE, APS, sickle cell, severe anemia
  • Tobacco, alcohol, cocaine
  • Teratogenic drugs (ACE-i, warfarin)
  • Previous IUGR
Placental/Fetal
  • Placental insufficiency, previa, abruption
  • Multiple gestation
  • TORCH infections
  • Genetic abnormalities

Diagnosis

  • SFH decreased (≥3cm less than GA in weeks)
  • Fetal movements reduced/absent
  • Serial ultrasound: decreased growth, small placenta, oligohydramnios
  • Umbilical artery Doppler: reduced/reversed diastolic flow
  • BPP ≤ 4 = delivery needed

Management

  • Antenatal: Treat cause, no smoking, good diet, monitor fetal wellbeing
  • Intranatal: Cesarean section is the rule — IUGR fetus cannot tolerate labor
  • Postnatal: Management of dysmaturity problems

12. Postterm Pregnancy

Definition

Pregnancy beyond 42 weeks. 41-42 weeks = prolonged pregnancy.

Causes

  • Most common: wrong date — review menstrual history
  • Maternal: primipara, previous prolonged pregnancy, sedentary life
  • Fetal: anencephaly, adrenal hypoplasia

Antenatal Diagnosis

  • BPD >9.6 cm on US
  • Increased fetal weight
  • Oligohydramnios
  • Increased placental calcification
  • Doppler for placental insufficiency

Postnatal Signs

  • Length >54 cm, Weight >4.5 kg
  • Skull well ossified, small fontanelles
  • Finger nails project beyond tips

Complications

  • Placental insufficiency → fetal hypoxia → acidosis → stillbirth
  • Oligohydramnios → cord compression → meconium aspiration
  • Fetal macrosomia → shoulder dystocia → birth trauma
  • Postpartum hemorrhage and infection

Management

Termination of pregnancy — induction or cesarean section.

13. Macrosomia

Definition

Fetal weight > 4,000 grams.

Risk Factors

  • History of fetal macrosomia
  • Maternal obesity
  • Excessive weight gain during pregnancy
  • Previous pregnancies (risk increases with each)
  • Male infants
  • Maternal age >35

Causes

  • Genetic factors
  • Maternal diabetes
  • Multiparity
  • Postterm pregnancy

Complications

  • Prolonged and obstructed labor
  • Shoulder dystocia
  • Fetal injuries
  • Meconium aspiration syndrome
  • Genital tract lacerations
  • Postpartum hemorrhage
  • Uterine rupture

Treatment

  1. Proper antenatal care
  2. Cesarean section — safe for both mother and fetus
  3. Postnatal: proper neonatal examination

14. Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD)

Definition

Death of fetus in-utero after 20 weeks or >500g.

Causes

Maternal
  • Poorly controlled DM
  • SLE, APS
  • Infection
  • Hypertension, pre/eclampsia
  • Uterine rupture
  • Maternal trauma/death
  • Thrombophilias
Fetal
  • IUGR
  • Congenital/genetic abnormality
  • Infection (Parvovirus B19, CMV, Listeria)
  • Hydrops
Placental
  • Cord accident, abruption
  • PROM, vasa previa
  • Feto-maternal hemorrhage
  • Placental insufficiency

Diagnosis

Symptoms

  • Gradual disappearance of pregnancy symptoms
  • Cessation of fetal movements
  • Failure of abdominal enlargement

Signs

  • Uterus < period of amenorrhea
  • Difficult palpation of fetal parts
  • Inaudible FHS by Doppler

Investigations

  • Spalding sign: overlapping fetal skull bones
  • Robert sign: gas in fetal large vessels
  • Ball sign: hyperflexion of fetal spine

Complications

  • Intrauterine infection
  • DIC and hypofibrinogenemia (after 3 weeks)
⚠️ Warning
DIC time bomb: After >3 weeks, risk of maternal DIC rises sharply.

Management

Termination of pregnancy should be offered after diagnosis.

  • Induction with cervical ripening + oxytocin
  • If failed induction → cesarean section

15. Assessment of Fetal Well-being

Cardiotocography (CTG) — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Fetal Movement Counting

After 30 weeks: Count kicks in 3 hours (1h at start, middle, end of 12h period). Multiply by 4. If <10 kicks → NST needed.

Non-Stress Test (NST)

Normal (reactive): ≥2 accelerations ≥15bpm above baseline, each ≥15 seconds, within 20 minutes.

Abnormal: Baseline oscillating <5 bpm.

Contraction Stress Test (CST)

ResultDefinition
NegativeNo late or significant variable decelerations
PositiveLate decelerations following ≥50% of contractions
EquivocalIntermittent late or significant variable decelerations
Unsatisfactory<3 contractions in 10 min or uninterpretable tracing

Cardiotocography (CTG)

Five Components

  1. Uterine activity (contractions)
  2. Baseline fetal heart rate
  3. Baseline FHR variability
  4. Presence of accelerations
  5. Periodic or episodic decelerations

Umbilical Artery Doppler

Absent or reversed end-diastolic flow indicates increased resistance — seen in extreme IUGR.
