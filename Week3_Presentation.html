1. Normal Labor

Stages of Cervical Changes During Labor — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition of Normal Labor

Normal labor is the process of spontaneous delivery of a single full-term viable fetus presented by the vertex through the birth canal within reasonable time without interference or complications to the mother or the fetus.

Causes of Onset of Labor

1. Hormonal Factors

↑ Estrogen, ↓ Progesterone, ↑ Prostaglandin F2α and ↑ Oxytocin → uterine contraction.

2. Mechanical Factors

Uterine distension and stretch of the lower uterine segment → uterine contraction.

The Four Functional Stages of Labor

1st
Onset → Full Dilatation (12h PR, 6h M)
2nd
Full Dilatation → Fetus Born (1-2h PR, 30min M)
3rd
Fetus Born → Placenta (~15min)
4th
1 Hour Observation Post-Placenta

True vs False Labor Pains

FeatureTrue LaborFalse Labor
Cervical changesPresent (dilatation & effacement)Absent
FrequencyRegular, gradually increaseIrregular
Pain radiationLower abdomen & back → thighsLower abdomen only
ShowPresentAbsent
Relief with sedationNoYes

Friedman Curve

The pattern of cervical dilatation during labor is a sigmoid curve (Friedman curve) with two phases:

  • Latent phase: cervix 0–3/5 cm, mainly effacement (12h PR, 8h M)
  • Active phase: cervix 3–10 cm, mainly dilatation
    • Acceleration phase: 3–4 cm
    • Phase of maximum slope: 4–9 cm
    • Deceleration phase: 9–10 cm

Mechanism of Labor (7 Cardinal Movements)

  1. Descent — caused by uterine contractions
  2. Engagement — biparietal diameter passes through pelvic brim
  3. Increased flexion — chin approaches chest
  4. Internal rotation — occiput rotates to anterior
  5. Extension — head extends under symphysis
  6. Restitution — head rotates back 45° to realign with shoulders
  7. External rotation — shoulders rotate to complete delivery

Abnormalities of Active Phase

FeatureCervical DilatationDescent of Head
Nulliparous (normal)1.2 cm/hr1 cm/hr
Multiparous (normal)1.5 cm/hr2 cm/hr
⚠️ Warning
Arrest of dilatation: cessation for ≥ 2 hours. Arrest of descent: cessation for ≥ 1 hour. Causes: excessive sedation, epidural analgesia, fetal malposition, CPD.

Signs of Placental Separation (6 Signs)

  1. Uterus becomes globular, hard and mobile
  2. Sudden gush of blood
  3. Suprapubic bulge in upper vagina
  4. Rise of fundal level above umbilicus
  5. Lengthening of the umbilical cord
  6. Absence of pulsation in the umbilical stump
💎 Clinical Pearl
Active management of third stage (oxytocin + cord traction + uterine massage) is preferred over expectant management — it cuts third-stage duration from 15 min to 5 min and significantly lowers PPH risk and maternal mortality.

2. Female Pelvis

Female Pelvis Anatomy — Anterior View — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Anatomy of the Bony Pelvis — Three Compartments

1. Pelvic Inlet (Pelvic Brim)

The superior opening into the true pelvis. Bounded by the linea terminalis.

2. Pelvic Cavity

Space between inlet and outlet where the fetus negotiates the birth canal.

3. Pelvic Outlet

Anatomical outlet: lozenge-shaped (symphysis, pubic arch, ischial tuberosities, sacrotuberous ligaments, coccyx). Obstetric outlet: a segment bounded by the plane of least pelvic dimension above and anatomical outlet below.

Diagonal conjugate12.5 cmSacral promontory → lower border symphysis (only measurable clinically)

Obstetric transverse12 cmBisects the true conjugate
Oblique diameters12 cmRight/left sacroiliac joint → opposite iliopectineal eminence
Sacro-cotyloid9.5 cmSacral promontory → iliopectineal eminence

Pelvic Outlet Diameters

DiameterValueLandmarks
Obstetric AP of outlet13 cmTip of sacrum → lower border symphysis (coccyx moves back in 2nd stage)
Bituberous11 cmInner aspects of ischial tuberosities
Interspinous10.5 cmTips of ischial spines (smallest pelvic dimension — plane of arrest)

Caldwell-Moloy Classification — 4 Pelvic Types

🟢 Gynecoid (50%)

The classic female pelvis. Round inlet, wide forepelvis, straight side walls. Optimally suited for vaginal delivery.

🔵 Android (20%)

The typical male pelvis. Heart-shaped inlet, narrow forepelvis. Increases risk of malposition and operative delivery.

🟠 Anthropoid (25%)

AP diameter > transverse — ape-like inlet. Long oval shape.

🟡 Platypelloid (5%)

Flat pelvis — transverse diameter > AP. Kidney-bean shaped inlet.

💎 Clinical Pearl
Mnemonic "GAAAP": Gynecoid 50, Android 20, Anthropoid 25, Platypelloid 5 (relative incidence). Only the gynecoid pelvis is optimally suited for vaginal delivery. The diagonal conjugate ≥ 11.5 cm suggests adequate pelvic capacity.

The Obstetric Axis (Curve of Carus)

An imaginary J-shaped line representing the path the head passes during labor — downwards and backwards along the inlet axis till the ischial spines, then downwards and forwards along the outlet axis.

3. Fetal Skull

Fetal Skull — Bones, Sutures & Fontanelles — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Three Parts of the Fetal Skull

1. Base

From chin to foramen magnum. Rigid, non-compressible (bones fused).

2. Face

From chin to root of nose and suborbital margin.

3. Vault

Consists of 5 flat bones joined by fibrous sutures. Compressible — allows moulding during labor.

Mento-vertical (13.5 cm) exceeds the largest pelvic inlet diameter (anatomical transverse = 13 cm). In brow presentation, the head CANNOT enter the pelvis — this is why brow presentation requires cesarean section.

4. Occipitoposterior Position

Occiput Anterior vs Occiput Posterior — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

The most common fetal malposition. It is vertex presentation in which the fetal back is directed posteriorly — also called "sunny-side up."

Etiology

Fetal Causes (7)
  • Marked deflexion of fetal head
  • Prematurity
  • Macrosomia
  • Multiple pregnancy
  • Polyhydramnios
  • Congenital anomalies (anencephaly, hydrocephalus)
  • Cord around the neck
Maternal Causes (6)
  • Android and anthropoid pelvis
  • Pendulous abdomen
  • Placenta previa
  • Pelvic tumors
  • Uterine anomalies (bicornuate, septate)
  • Abnormal uterine contractions

Four Positions of OP

Left O.P.

Occiput over left sacroiliac joint.

Right O.P. (more common)

Occiput over right sacroiliac joint. More common due to dextrorotation and sigmoid colon on left.

Direct O.P.

Occiput over the sacrum.

Occipito-transverse

Occiput lies transverse in pelvis.

Mechanism of Labor — 4 Outcomes

OutcomeIncidenceDescription
Long anterior rotation90%Head rotates 135° to direct OA → vaginal delivery
Direct OP (face-to-pubis)6%Delivers in OP position — episiotomy needed
Persistent OP3%No rotation → obstructed labor
Deep transverse arrest1%Short rotation 45° → obstructed labor

Management

First Stage

  • Avoid unnecessary PV examinations
  • Avoid uterine inertia (encourage adequate contractions)
  • Evacuate bladder and rectum
  • Close maternal and fetal observation

Second Stage

  • Wait for descent and long anterior rotation (90% of cases)
  • CS if: prolonged labor, poor progress, no rotation, fetal distress
  • Episiotomy for direct OP (face-to-pubis) to avoid perineal laceration

Complications

Maternal
  • Early PROM, cord prolapse, prolonged/obstructed labor, vaginal/cervical tears, rupture uterus
Fetal
  • Fetal hypoxia, skull fracture (left parietal bone impinges on ischial spine)
⚠️ Danger
Persistent OP with obstructed labor is a leading cause of rupture uterus — a maternal life-threatening emergency.

5. Face Presentation

Face Presentation — Mentoanterior Position — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

A rare cephalic malpresentation (~0.1–0.2%) in which the fetal head is completely hyperextended — the occiput touches the fetal back and the chin (mentum) becomes the presenting part. The denominator is the mentum.

Causes

  • All causes of OP position (deflexion, prematurity, macrosomia, polyhydramnios, anomalies, cord around neck, android pelvis, pendulous abdomen, etc.)
  • Specific to face: neck tumors, cystic hygroma, large fetal thyroid gland — all prevent flexion

Three Position Groups

🟢 Mentoanterior (favorable)

Chin directed anteriorly (symphysis). Direct MA, R MA, L MA. Vaginal delivery possible.

🟡 Mentoposterior (unfavorable)

Chin directed posteriorly (sacrum). Direct MP, R MP, L MP. Usually requires CS.

🔵 Mentotransverse

Chin lies transverse. R MT, L MT.

Diagnosis

PV Examination

The mouth, nose, orbital bones and chin are felt. Key: mouth and malar eminences form a triangle (vs ischial tuberosities and anus in a straight line).

Mechanism — Mentoanterior

  1. Descent
  2. Engagement by submento-bregmatic diameter (9.5 cm)
  3. Increased extension
  4. Internal rotation of chin 1/8 cycle anteriorly
  5. Head delivered by flexion
  6. Restitution
  7. External rotation

Mechanism — Mentoposterior

OutcomeDescription
Long anterior rotationChin rotates to anterior → delivered as MA (safe)
Deep transverse arrestShort anterior rotation → chin arrested transverse
Persistent mentoposteriorNo rotation → chin remains posterior
Direct mentoposteriorShort posterior rotation → chin moves posteriorly

Management — Do's and Don'ts

❌ Don'ts
  • Don't convert face to vertex (risk: uterine rupture, cord prolapse)
  • Don't apply vacuum extractor
  • Don't apply internal scalp electrode
✅ Do's
  • Wait for spontaneous rotation (high chance of anterior rotation)
  • Large episiotomy if vaginal delivery
  • Epidural analgesia for prolonged labor
  • Forceps in mentoanterior (chin anterior, head low)
  • CS for: persistent MP, deep transverse arrest, direct MP

6. Brow Presentation

Brow Presentation — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

A rare cephalic malpresentation in which the fetal head is midway between flexion and extension — the brow is the presenting part and the frontal bone is the denominator.

Causes

Same as occipito-posterior position (see Topic 4).

Positions

Frontoanterior

Right and Left. Frontal bone oriented anteriorly.

Frontoposterior

Right and Left. Frontal bone oriented posteriorly.

Diagnosis

During Pregnancy

  • Occiput and sinciput felt at the same level on abdominal palpation
  • Ultrasound is conclusive

During Labor (PV)

  • Frontal bone, supraorbital ridge, and root of nose are felt
  • Chin is NOT felt — key distinguishing feature from face presentation
⚠️ Danger — Mento-vertical Diameter
The engaged diameter is mento-vertical 13.75 cm which is longer than ANY diameter of the female pelvic inlet (largest AP = 11 cm true conjugate, largest transverse = 13 cm). Labor becomes obstructed from the start.

Mechanism of Labor

NO mechanism of labor is possible in brow presentation:

  • Not flexed enough to behave like vertex
  • Not extended enough to behave like face
  • The head is locked at the largest diameter of the skull

Management

🚨 Cesarean Section is the Only Safe Option
Because the mento-vertical diameter (13.75 cm) cannot pass through the pelvic inlet, vaginal delivery is impossible. Cesarean section as soon as the diagnosis is confirmed.

Complications

  • Maternal: PROM, cord prolapse
  • Fetal: Related to operative delivery and obstructed labor

7. Compound Presentation

Compound Presentation — Limb Prolapsed Beside Head — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

A cephalic presentation in which a limb (usually upper limb, less commonly lower limb) prolapses beside the presenting part. Two parts present simultaneously at the pelvic inlet.

Causes (7 Factors)

Causes creating excess room
  • Prematurity — smaller fetus, more space
  • Polyhydramnios — excess fluid, mobile fetus
  • Multiparity — lax abdominal walls
  • Twins — crowding + limb descent
Causes preventing head engagement
  • PROM — loss of forewaters cushion
  • Contracted pelvis — head can't engage
  • Pelvic tumors — space-occupying lesion

Diagnosis

  • Delayed engagement — head doesn't descend at expected time
  • PV examination: prolapsed limb felt beside the presenting part

Management

Conservative Step

Gently try to reduce the prolapsed limb upwards above the pelvic brim between contractions, hold it till contraction, then push the head down into the pelvis.

CS Indicated If:

1. Abnormal FHR Pattern

Fetal heart rate abnormalities signal fetal compromise.

2. Cord Prolapse

Compound presentation is high-risk for cord prolapse — emergency CS required.

3. Poor Progress of Labor

Head doesn't engage / labor doesn't advance despite conservative maneuver.

🚨 Critical Watch-item
Cord prolapse is the critical complication. Because the presenting part does not fit snugly, the cord can slip past. A sudden FHR deceleration should be assumed cord prolapse until proven otherwise.

8. Breech Presentation

Types of Breech Presentation — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition & Incidence

Breech presentation: fetus in longitudinal lie where the presenting part is the buttocks or feet (podalic part). Occurs in 3–4% of all deliveries — most common malpresentation at term.

Types of Breech

Frank Breech (50–70%)

Hips flexed, knees extended (pike position). Legs along abdomen, feet near head. Most common type.

Complete Breech (5–10%)

Hips flexed, knees flexed (cannonball). Fetus sitting cross-legged, feet beside buttocks.

Footling / Incomplete (10–30%)

One or both hips extended, foot presenting. Higher risk of cord prolapse. Indication for CS.

Knee Presentation (rare)

Hip extended, knee flexed on one or both sides.

Etiology (8 Predisposing Factors)

  • Prematurity — smaller fetus, easier to flip
  • Uterine malformations or fibroids
  • Polyhydramnios
  • Placenta previa — blocks head engagement
  • Fetal abnormalities (CNS, neck masses, aneuploidy)
  • Multiple gestations
  • Cord around the neck
  • Pelvic tumors

Positions (Denominator = Sacrum)

LSA

Left Sacro-Anterior (most common)

RSA

Right Sacro-Anterior

LSP

Left Sacro-Posterior

RSP

Right Sacro-Posterior

Mechanism of Labor (Sacro-Anterior)

A) Buttocks

  • Engagement by bi-trochanteric diameter (10 cm)
  • Internal rotation 1/8 circle → anterior buttock hinges under symphysis
  • Posterior buttock delivered by lateral flexion; anterior by straightening

B) Shoulders

  • Engagement by bisacromial diameter (11.8 cm)
  • Anterior shoulder hinges under symphysis; delivered same as buttocks

C) Head (most difficult part)

  • Engagement by occipito-frontal diameter (11.5 cm)
  • Head delivered by flexion

External Cephalic Version (ECV)

  • Performed at 36–37 weeks
  • Manual rotation of fetus from breech to cephalic
  • Success rate: ~50–60%
  • Requires continuous fetal monitoring

Methods to Deliver the Aftercoming Head

1. MSV (Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit)

Two fingers on maxilla + middle finger on occiput for flexion + shoulder traction. Most commonly used.

2. Burns-Marshall

Let baby hang by own weight + suprapubic pressure. Lift body toward abdomen when nape visible.

3. Piper's Forceps

Special forceps applied to the aftercoming head when it is engaged but not yet delivered.

Indications for CS in Breech

🚨 CS Indications
  • Footling breech (cord prolapse risk)
  • Extended legs (Frank breech) in primigravida
  • Previous unsuccessful ECV
  • Contracted pelvis / CPD
  • Fetal distress
  • Prolonged labor / arrest

Complications

Maternal
  • Prolonged labor, cord prolapse, PPH, cervical/l vaginal lacerations
Fetal
  • Cord prolapse (especially footling), head entrapment, birth injuries (spinal cord, brachial plexus), hypoxia

9. Shoulder Presentation

Shoulder (Transverse Lie) Presentation — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definition

Shoulder presentation occurs when the fetus lies with its long axis transverse or oblique to the maternal long axis. The presenting part is the shoulder (acromion process). It is a type of transverse lie.

Etiology

  • Prematurity — fetus too small to maintain longitudinal lie
  • Polyhydramnios — excess fluid, mobile fetus
  • Placenta previa — blocks lower segment
  • Contracted pelvis
  • Uterine anomalies (bicornuate)
  • Multiple pregnancies
  • Pelvic tumors
  • Pendulous abdomen
  • Short cord

Diagnosis

Abdominal Examination

  • Fundal grip: neither head nor breech felt — both are at same level (flanks)
  • Umbilical grip: back felt across the midline; limbs on both sides
  • Pelvic grip: neither head nor breech felt in pelvis

PV Examination

  • Scapula and ribs are felt
  • Eclamptic grip (shoulder grip): grasp the shoulder and try to push it above the pelvic brim while pushing the other shoulder down

Management

Before 24 Weeks

Usually resolves spontaneously as the fetus grows. Monitor with US.

After 36 Weeks

Planned cesarean section is recommended. Transverse lie at term is not suitable for vaginal delivery.

Internal Podalic Version (IPV)

A procedure to convert a transverse lie to a footling presentation by turning the fetus internally and extracting it by the feet. Performed in early labor with intact membranes as an emergency procedure.

⚠️ Warning
Obstructed labor is inevitable in shoulder presentation at term — the acromion (11 cm) cannot negotiate the pelvis in transverse orientation. Emergency CS is required if shoulder presentation is diagnosed in labor.

Complications

  • Maternal: Obstructed labor, cord prolapse, uterine rupture, maternal death
  • Fetal: Hypoxia, death (very high mortality if untreated)

10. Cord Presentation & Prolapse

Cord Prolapse — Umbilical Cord Ahead of Presenting Part — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Definitions

Cord Presentation

Umbilical cord presents ahead of the presenting part with membranes intact. Diagnosed by US or vaginal examination.

Cord Prolapse

Umbilical cord prolapses through the cervix alongside or ahead of the presenting part with membranes ruptured. This is an obstetric emergency.

Risk Factors for Cord Prolapse

  • Malpresentation: breech (especially footling), transverse lie, face/brow
  • Compound presentation
  • Prematurity — small presenting part doesn't occlude cervix
  • Polyhydramnios — sudden gush of fluid washes cord down
  • Artificial rupture of membranes (AROM) — if cord is above presenting part
  • Long cord
  • Battledore/velamentous insertion
  • Low-lying placenta

Diagnosis

  • Sudden, dramatic fetal bradycardia (variable decelerations → prolonged bradycardia)
  • PV examination: cord felt in the vagina or through the cervix
  • Visible cord at the vulva in overt prolapse
🚨 Emergency — Every Minute Counts
Cord prolapse causes compression of the umbilical vessels between the fetal presenting part and the maternal pelvis → acute fetal hypoxia → fetal death if not delivered urgently. Average time from prolapse to fetal death: ~15 minutes.

Management — Emergency Protocol

  1. Call for emergency CS immediately — do not delay
  2. Elevate the presenting part off the cord (push head up per vagina, knee-chest position, Trendelenburg)
  3. IV fluids + left lateral position
  4. Tocolytics (terbutaline) to relax uterus and reduce cord compression
  5. Oxygen to mother
  6. Continuous FHR monitoring
  7. Emergency cesarean section — the definitive management

Classification by Severity

TypeDescriptionUrgency
Overt cord prolapseCord visible at vulva or felt in vagina with ROMHighest urgency
Funic presentationCord presents with intact membranesHigh — prepare for CS
Cord ahead of presenting partCord below presenting part (not yet prolapsed)High risk — close monitoring
💎 Clinical Pearl
In all non-cephalic presentations (breech, transverse, face, brow), the cord should be excluded by vaginal examination before AROM. Never artificially rupture membranes without first ruling out cord presentation.

11. Operative Obstetrics

Operative Obstetrics — Forceps, Vacuum & Cesarean Section — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Overview

Operative obstetrics encompasses all instrumental and surgical procedures used to assist or complete delivery when spontaneous vaginal delivery is not possible or is unsafe.

A. Operative Vaginal Delivery

Indications (Common)

  • Prolonged second stage of labor
  • Fetal distress in second stage
  • Maternal exhaustion
  • Maternal medical conditions (cardiac disease, pre-eclampsia)
  • Malposition (OP position requiring rotation)

Prerequisites for Operative Vaginal Delivery

  1. Cervix fully dilated (10 cm)
  2. Membranes ruptured
  3. Fetal head engaged
  4. Bladder empty
  5. Adequate anesthesia
  6. Head position known

B. Vacuum Extraction (Ventouse)

Mechanism

Suction cup applied to the flexion point on the fetal scalp. Negative pressure (0.6–0.8 kg/cm²) creates a "chignon" (edema) that allows traction.

Advantages
  • Easier to apply than forceps
  • Less maternal perineal trauma
  • Can be used for rotational delivery
  • Less anesthesia required

Vacuum vs Forceps

FeatureVacuumForceps
AnesthesiaMinimal/localRegional preferred
Maternal traumaLessMore (perineal tears)
Fetal scalp injuryMore (cephalohematoma, retinal hemorrhage)Less
Failed attempt rateHigherLower
Useful for rotationYesYes (Kielland's)

C. Cesarean Section

Types of Incision

Low Transverse (LTC)

Most common. Incision in lower uterine segment. Lower risk of rupture in subsequent pregnancies. Preferred incision.

Classical (Vertical)

Vertical incision in the upper segment. Used in emergencies (extreme prematurity, transverse lie, placenta previa). Higher rupture risk.

Indications for CS

Absolute (Grade I)
  • CPD (cephalopelvic disproportion)
  • Placenta previa
  • Transverse lie at term
  • Prolapsed cord
  • Fetal distress not responding to conservative measures
  • Obstructed labor
  • Eclampsia not responding to treatment
Relative (Grade II)
  • Previous CS (VBAC trial vs repeat CS)
  • Breech in primigravida
  • Moderate CPD with adequate trial of labor
  • Severe pre-eclampsia with unfavorable cervix
  • Maternal request (in some settings)

Active Management of Third Stage (AMTSL)

  1. Administration of uterotonic (oxytocin 10 IU IM or ergometrine) soon after delivery of baby
  2. Delayed cord clamping (1–3 minutes)
  3. Controlled cord traction (CCT) with simultaneous uterine counter-pressure
  4. Uterine massage after placental delivery
💎 Clinical Pearl
AMTSL reduces PPH by reducing third-stage duration from 15 min to 5 min and decreasing the incidence of blood loss > 500 mL. It is the standard of care for all vaginal deliveries.

12. Obstetric Instruments

Obstetric Forceps — Wikimedia Commons (CC)

Classification of Obstetric Instruments

1. Forceps — Types

a) Simpson's Forceps

Most commonly used. For rotation and traction of the aftercoming head in OP or OA position. Has cephalic curve for the fetal head and pelvic curve for the birth canal.

b) Kielland's Forceps

Rotational forceps. Used for rotation of the head in deep transverse arrest. Minimal pelvic curve. Used with a sliding lock.

c) Piper's Forceps

Specialized for aftercoming head in breech delivery. Applied from below after the body has been delivered. Long, narrow blades.

d) Wrigley's Forceps

Short forceps for outlet delivery. Used when head is on the perineum (crowning). Quick application at delivery.

2. Vacuum Extractor (Ventouse)

Malmström's Ventouse

Metal cup with chain traction. Creates a "chignon" edema on the scalp.

Silastic (Oxford) Cup

Soft rubber cup — less scalp trauma. More commonly used today.

Parts of Obstetric Forceps

PartFunction
BladeApplied to the sides of the fetal head. Has cephalic and pelvic curves.
ShankConnects blade to handle. May be parallel (Simpson's) or crossed (Kielland's).
LockJoins the two blades. English lock (Simpson's) or sliding lock (Kielland's).
HandleGrip for the operator. Used for traction and rotation.

Classification by Application

CategoryLevelInstrument
Outlet forcepsScalp visible at introitus without separationWrigley's
Low forcepsLeading point at station +2 or belowSimpson's
Mid-cavity forcepsLeading point above station +2Kielland's
High forcepsHead not engagedContraindicated

Vacuum Extractor — Application

  1. Apply cup to the flexion point (3 cm anterior to posterior fontanelle along the sagittal suture)
  2. Create vacuum: 0.2 kg/cm² initially → increase to 0.6–0.8 kg/cm²
  3. Apply traction only during contractions + maternal pushing
  4. Avoid rotating > 45° with vacuum
  5. Maximum 3 pulls — if unsuccessful, convert to forceps or CS

Contraindications to Operative Vaginal Delivery

🚨 Absolute Contraindications
  • Unengaged head
  • Incomplete cervical dilatation
  • Face or brow presentation (for vacuum)
  • Cephalopelvic disproportion
  • Known fetal bleeding disorder
  • Fetal bone demineralization (osteogenesis imperfecta)
  • Prior fetal scalp electrode (for vacuum)

Forceps Application Technique

  1. Identify head position and station by PV
  2. Apply left blade first (held in left hand, inserted along left side of pelvis)
  3. Apply right blade second (held in right hand, inserted along right side)
  4. Lock the blades — check for correct application (sagittal suture in midline, no gap between blade and head)
  5. Apply traction downward and backward initially, then upward and forward as head crowns
  6. Remove blades when head is delivered
💎 Clinical Pearl
Forceps leave marks on the fetal face (usually disappear within 24–48 hours). The key principle is: never apply forceps to a head that is not engaged. When in doubt, deliver by cesarean section.
